The competitive service largely consists of all civil service positions in the executive branch, other than (1) positions
excepted from the competitive service by statute; (2) positions appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate; and
(3) the SES. Id. § 2101. Traditionally, OPM has administered examinations for entrance into the competitive service. 5
C.F.R. § 332.101(a). These examinations are meant to be “practical in character” and relate to “matters that fairly test the
relative capacity and fitness of the applicants for the appointment sought.” 5 U.S.C. § 3304(b)(1). Title 5 also authorizes
OPM to prescribe rules allowing agencies to hire candidates directly under specified circumstances. Id. § 3304(b)(3).

The excepted service includes designated civil service positions that are not in the competitive service or the SES and are not
subject to competitive examination. Id. § 2103(a). OPM maintains authority to exempt a position from the competitive
service when it determines that an appointment through competitive examination is not practicable, or the recruitment of
students or recent graduates would be better achieved through alternate recruitment and assessment processes. 5 C.F.R.
§ 213.102(b).

The pay structure for the competitive service and the excepted service is similar. Both services are typically paid in
accordance with the General Schedule, a schedule of annual basic pay rates that consists of 15 grades, designated “GS-1”
through “GS-15.” 5 U.S.C. § 5332. This fixed pay scale is generally designed to reflect, among other things, equal pay for
substantially equal work within a local pay area. Id. § 5301. Additionally, employees in the competitive service and those
meeting specified tenure requirements in the excepted service have similar notice and appeal rights for adverse personnel
actions. Id. § 7511(a)(1). For example, before a removal, a suspension for more than 14 days, a reduction in grade or pay, or a
furlough of 30 days or less, the agency must provide at least 30 days’ advance written notice to the affected employee. Id. §
7513(b)(1). The employee must also be given a reasonable time to respond to the notice and provide affidavits and other
evidence to support the answer. Id. § 7513(b)(2). Some adverse actions may also be appealed to the Merit Systems Protection
Board (MSPB or Board), an agency that reviews and adjudicates specified personnel actions taken against qualifying federal
employees. Id. § 7701.

The SES is a corps of high-level government employees who manage major programs and projects within most federal
agencies. Id. § 2101a. In these leadership roles, SES members may serve as a link between top-level political appointees of
an agency and career civil servants within the agency. The SES is governed by a regulatory structure separate from the
competitive and excepted services. Id. §§ 3131-3136. While SES members are primarily career appointees chosen through a
merit-based competitive hiring process, others are noncareer, limited term, or limited emergency appointees (commonly
political appointees) selected by agency leadership. Id. §§ 3393-3394. To shield certain SES roles from political influence,
some SES positions (career reserved positions) must be filled with career appointees, and Title 5 limits the number of
noncareer and limited term appointees who may serve in SES positions. Id. § 3134.

The SES pay structure is distinct from the rest of the civil service. Id. § 5382. Title 5 specifies that SES members are paid
within a particular range based on an executive’s individual performance or contribution to agency performance (or both), as
measured under a performance appraisal system. Id. In addition, Title 5 articulates special conditions and procedures for
removing, suspending, or taking other adverse actions against a member of the SES. Id. §§ 7541-7543. For example, career
SES appointees who have successfully completed a one-year probationary period may be removed or subject to adverse
action only for specified reasons, including misconduct and substandard performance. Id. § 7543(a). Career appointees must
receive advance written notice of these actions, and an opportunity to appeal the action. Id. § 7543(b), (d). In comparison,

Introduction

The federal government is the nation’s largest employer,1 with more than two million executive
branch employees in the United States, U.S. Territories, and foreign countries.2 A majority of
these employees work in the competitive service.3 Applicants for competitive service positions
compete with other applicants and are evaluated according to objective standards before they may
be appointed.4 The executive branch includes two other service classifications—the excepted
service and the Senior Executive Service (SES)—with hiring and removal standards that diverge
from those prescribed for the competitive service. Positions in the excepted service are
specifically excepted from the competitive service by statute, by the President, or by the Office of
Personnel Management (OPM).5 SES positions are also not in the competitive service.6 The SES
includes senior managerial, supervisory, and policy positions that are subject to a different pay
scale, as well as different hiring and removal standards.7 This report examines the three service
classifications, and reviews some of the central features and notable differences among these
classifications.

Competitive Service

The competitive service consists of all civil service positions in the executive branch, except the
following:
    •   positions that are specifically excepted from the competitive service by or under
         statute;
    •   positions to which appointments are made by presidential nomination for
        confirmation by the Senate, unless the Senate otherwise directs; and
    •   positions in the SES.8

The competitive service also includes non-executive branch positions and positions in the District
of Columbia government that are specifically included in the competitive service by statute.9

Appointment Process
OPM administers examinations for entrance into the competitive service.10 These examinations
are meant to be “practical in character” and relate to “matters that fairly test the relative capacity
and fitness of the applicants for the appointment sought[.]”11 OPM identifies the relative weights
for the subjects in an examination, and assigns numerical ratings on a 100-point scale.12
Applicants who meet the minimum requirements for entrance to an examination, such as
citizenship and residence requirements, and are rated 70 or more in the examination are eligible
for appointment in the competitive service.13 These individuals are placed on registers or lists of
eligibles.14 When an agency seeks to fill a competitive service position, it requests a certificate of
eligibles from OPM.15 This certificate is to include enough names from the top of the relevant
register to allow an agency appointing official to consider at least three individuals for every
position to be filled.16

The competitive service includes several types of appointments. An individual selected for a
continuing position is generally appointed as a career-conditional employee subject to an initial
one-year probationary period.17 After three continuous years of service in a career-conditional
appointment, an employee will be converted to a career appointment.18 A term appointment is a
nonpermanent appointment for a period of more than one year, but fewer than four years.19 An
agency may make a term appointment when the need for an employee’s services is not
permanent, but involves a special project, extraordinary workload, or reorganization.20 A
temporary appointment is a time-limited appointment for a period not to exceed one year.21 An
agency may make a temporary appointment to fill a short-term position or meet an employment
need that is scheduled to end within a specified timeframe.22

Pay Structure

Employees in the competitive service are generally paid in accordance with the General Schedule,
a schedule of annual basic pay rates that consists of 15 grades, designated “GS-1” through “GS-
15.”23 The grades include 10 steps that provide for increasing rates of pay. An employee may
receive a periodic step-increase, commonly referred to as a within-grade increase, if the employee
is paid less than the maximum rate for his or her position and the employee’s work is “at an
acceptable level of competence,” as determined by the head of an agency.24 The increase may be
made annually at steps 1, 2, and 3; every two years at steps 4, 5, and 6; and every three years at
steps 7, 8, and 9 of the General Schedule.25

General Schedule salaries are based on the principles that there is equal pay for substantially
equal work within a local pay area, that any pay distinctions are based on work and performance,
that federal pay rates are comparable with non-federal pay rates for the same level of work, and
that any pay disparities between federal and non-federal employees should be eliminated.26

Adverse Action Protections and Procedures

Employees in the competitive service who are not serving a probationary or trial period, or have
completed one year of current continuous service in a position other than a temporary
appointment limited to one year or less, maintain specified notice and appeal rights for adverse
personnel actions.27 Before an agency may suspend such a qualifying employee for up to 14 days,
the employee must be given an advance written notice that identifies the specific reasons for the
suspension.28 The employee must also be provided a reasonable time to answer the notice and
furnish affidavits and other evidence to support the answer.29

personnel actions. Before a removal, a suspension for more than 14 days, a reduction in grade or
pay, or a furlough of 30 days or less, the agency must provide at least 30 days’ advance written
notice to the employee.30 The employee must also be given a reasonable time to respond to the

Excepted Service

About one-third of all federal workers in the executive branch are employed in the excepted
service.38 The excepted service consists of those civil service positions that are not in the
competitive service or the SES.39 Positions in the excepted service may be designated by statute
or by OPM, and are not subject to competitive examination.40 OPM will exempt a position from
the competitive service when it determines that an appointment through competitive examination
is not practicable, or the recruitment of certain students or recent graduates would be better
achieved through alternate recruitment and assessment processes.41 For example, OPM may
determine that a position should be excepted from the competitive service because it is
impracticable to examine the knowledge, skills, and abilities required for a position.42

Appointment Process

Positions in the excepted service are categorized into seven schedules or categories of
appointment authority. Schedule A includes positions that are not of a confidential or policy-
determining character for which it is not practicable to examine applicants.43 Attorneys, chaplains,
and short-term positions for which there is a critical hiring need are examples of schedule A
positions.44

Schedule B also includes positions that are not of a confidential or policy-determining character
for which it is not practicable to examine applicants.45 Unlike Schedule A positions, however,
these positions require an applicant to satisfy basic qualification standards established by OPM
for the relevant occupation and grade level.46 Individuals appointed to schedule B positions
engage in a variety of activities, including policy analysis, teaching, and technical assistance.47

Positions in schedule C are policy-determining or involve a close and confidential working
relationship with the head of an agency or other key appointed officials.48 These positions include
most political appointees below the cabinet and subcabinet levels.49 Political appointees are
generally appointed by the President or his delegates and have no expectation of continued
employment beyond the presidential administration in which the appointment is made. An agency
may have a variety of titles for jobs designated as schedule C.50

Schedule D includes positions that are not of a confidential or policy-determining character for
which competitive examination makes it difficult to recruit a sufficient number of certain students
or recent graduates.51 Examples of schedule D positions include those involving science,
technology, engineering, or mathematics (STEM) occupations and positions in the Presidential
Management Fellows Program.52 Schedule D positions generally require an applicant to satisfy
basic qualification standards established by OPM for the relevant occupation and grade level.53

Pay Structure

Like employees in the competitive service, excepted service employees are generally paid in
accordance with the General Schedule.69 Some excepted service employees, however, may be
paid under administratively determined (AD) pay schedules or agency specific pay systems.70

Adverse Action Protections and Procedures

Most excepted service employees maintain the same notice and appeal rights as competitive
service employees for removals, suspensions for more than 14 days, reductions in grade or pay,
and furloughs of up to 30 days.71 These rights do not apply to employees in positions that have a
“confidential, policy-determining, policy-making or policy-advocating character.”72 Thus,
employees in Schedule C positions do not maintain these rights.73

In addition, some employees in the excepted service must satisfy different durational
requirements before the notice and appeal rights become available. So-called “preference
eligibles” in an executive agency, the Postal Service, or the Postal Rate Commission must
complete one year of current continuous service to avail themselves of the relevant notice and
appeal rights.74 The term “preference eligible” refers to specified military veterans and some of
their family members, such as an unmarried widow, and the spouse of a service-connected

Senior Executive Service

The SES is a cadre of high-level government employees who manage major programs and
projects within most federal agencies.77 While they are considered federal employees within the
civil service system, the SES is governed by a regulatory structure separate from the competitive
and excepted services. As defined in statute, SES positions are generally managerial or
supervisory positions that are classified above the GS-15 grade (or certain equivalent positions)
and need not be appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate.78 In these leadership
roles, SES members may serve as intermediaries between top-level political appointees of an
agency who seek to carry out the objectives of a particular President and career civil servants with
institutional experience relating to relevant issues.79 According to OPM data, as of April 2026, the
SES had approximately 6,647 members.80

Pay Structure
The SES pay structure is also distinct from the rest of the civil service.96 Title 5 specifies that the
pay rate of each senior executive is based on the executive’s individual performance or
contribution to agency performance (or both), as measured under a “rigorous” performance
appraisal system.97 Each federal agency must maintain at least one of these appraisal systems,
subject to OPM standards, review, and approval.98 Performance appraisals of SES members may
consider factors such as improvements in efficiency, productivity, and quality of work or service,
cost efficiency, and performance timeliness.99

Title 5 also sets out different pay rates for the SES, with a minimum rate of basic pay equal to 120
percent of the rate for GS-15, step 1, and a maximum rate of basic pay equal to the rate for Level
III of the Executive Schedule.100 SES members’ annual aggregate pay (that includes additional
compensation such as bonuses, awards,101 and other payments in addition to basic pay) is capped
at the rate for Level I of the Executive Schedule.102 If a senior executive’s total compensation
exceeds the aggregate limitation, the executive receives the overage in the following calendar
year.103 Agencies with performance appraisal systems that are certified by OPM as “making
meaningful distinctions based on relative performance” have a higher range of SES pay.104 These
agencies apply a maximum annual rate of basic pay for senior executives equal to the rate for
level II of the Executive Schedule or apply an annual aggregate limit equal to the salary of the
Vice President.105

Adverse Action Protections and Procedures

Title 5 also articulates conditions and procedures for removing, suspending, or taking other
adverse actions against a member of the SES. Career SES appointees who have successfully
completed a one-year probationary period may be removed or subject to adverse action only for
specified reasons.106 For example, an SES career appointee may be removed from the civil service
or suspended for more than 14 days only for misconduct, neglect of duty, malfeasance, or failure
to accept a directed reassignment or to accompany a position in a transfer of function.107 SES
members must receive advance written notice about the action and opportunity to provide an
answer or receive hearing, subject to exception.108 The senior executive may also appeal the
employment action to the MSPB.109

An agency’s performance appraisal system is to provide for annual summary ratings at the
following levels: one or more fully successful levels; a minimally satisfactory level; and an
unsatisfactory level.110 A career appointee receiving a single unsatisfactory performance rating
may be reassigned or transferred within the SES or removed from the SES.111 A career SES
member who receives two unsatisfactory ratings in any period of five consecutive years must be
removed from the SES.112 A career SES member who receives less than fully successful ratings
twice in any period of three consecutive years will also be removed from the SES.113 Affected
SES career appointees must receive advance written notice of these actions. While these
appointees may not appeal these actions to the MSPB, they may request an informal hearing
before the Board.114

Considerations for Congress

While Congress has provided the President with broad authority to prescribe rules for the
competitive service and identify positions in the excepted service, it has established a variety of
standards that govern the employment of individuals in these positions, as well as those employed
in the SES.121 The appointment, compensation, and adverse action standards discussed in this
report illustrate how Congress has legislated to govern civil service employment. The Civil
Service Reform Act of 1978 (CSRA)122 generally established these standards in response to a
civil service system described at the time as “an outdated patchwork of statutes and rules built up
over almost a century.”123 In considering the act, the Senate Committee on Governmental Affairs
observed that “[a]lthough the civil service system has largely succeeded in safeguarding merit
principles, there have been frequent attempts to circumvent them, some of which have been
successful.”124

Congress could maintain the status quo regarding regulation of the civil service, potentially
allowing the executive branch to continue to pursue reforms within existing statutory authorities.
Depending on the nature of these reforms, however, some may question whether they reflect the
CSRA’s original goals. For example, if a greater number of employees are placed in the excepted
service and are subject to different adverse action procedures, questions could arise about the
continued application of merit principles.

Congress could consider new legislation that would establish additional standards for federal
employees, including measures to allow for compensation outside of the General Schedule125 or
bills that would limit or expand the President’s ability to except positions from the competitive
service within constitutional bounds.126 A review of the statutory and regulatory authorities for the
excepted service schedules could also be part of the oversight agenda for the 120th Congress. One
focus could involve strengthening the current reporting requirements to Congress for positions
placed in the excepted service, potentially providing Congress with current information about
changes in which Congress would concur or to which it would object.

Congress could also seek more information and guidance that might inform future legislation on
the civil service. For example, Congress could require OPM to provide a report to the House
Committee on Oversight and Government Reform and the Senate Committee on Homeland
Security and Governmental Affairs on executive agency use of the excepted service schedules and
on OPM’s procedures for oversight of the excepted service. Congress could direct OPM to
convene a task force of the Chief Human Capital Officers Council, of which OPM is the chair, to
review agency practices with regard to excepted service positions, including any needed
enhancements to training for agency employees responsible for overseeing excepted service
appointments to ensure that procedures of import to Congress are followed. Congress could also
require the National Academy of Public Administration, a congressionally chartered nonprofit
organization that conducts research on public administration, to conduct a study and report to
Congress on the civil service, including any recommendations for congressional action.
