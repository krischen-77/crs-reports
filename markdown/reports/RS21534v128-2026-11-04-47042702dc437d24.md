similar to those of other GCC states.

Oman and the United States maintain strong defense ties, whereby Oman permits the U.S. military to use its military
facilities. However, when compared with other Gulf Arab states, such as Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Kuwait, U.S.-Omani
defense relations are smaller in scale. The United States has provided minimal foreign assistance to Oman, mainly for border
security. From 1946 to 2025, the United States provided Oman with $1.6 billion in total economic and military assistance.

The U.S.-Oman Free Trade Agreement was signed on January 19, 2006, and ratified by Congress (P.L. 109-283, signed by
the President September 26, 2006). Today, the United States is one of Oman’s largest trading partners and foreign investors.
In 2025, the total volume of bilateral trade was $3.3 billion. In July 2026, the Trump Administration imposed a 12.5% tariff
on most U.S. imports from Oman as a result of an investigation that found Oman and various economies failed to impose and
effectively enforce bans on imports made with forced labor. Certain categories of goods are subject to higher tariffs globally,
including aluminum, of which Oman is an exporter.

Oman: Politics, Security, and U.S. Policy


Overview

Oman is positioned astride the southeastern corner of the Arabian Peninsula, bordering the
Arabian Sea, Indian Ocean, and the Strait of Hormuz, one of the world’s key maritime choke
points (see Figure 1). Situated across the strait from Iran and bordering Saudi Arabia to the west,
Oman’s proximity to its much larger, more powerful neighbors has led the Sultanate to follow a
pragmatic foreign policy. For over half-a-century, the Sultanate has pursued friendly relations
with all of its neighbors, while maintaining close security ties with the United States and the
United Kingdom.

Since the start of the U.S.-Iran conflict and the ensuing contest over reopening the Strait of
Hormuz, Oman’s long-standing tradition of serving as a quiet diplomatic interlocutor has been
challenged. Iran and the United States have sought Oman’s support for their contrasting proposals
to reopen the strait. Oman’s attempts to balance competing demands from Washington and Tehran
have, at times, drawn criticism from Iranian and U.S. officials; in 2026, President Donald Trump
twice threatened military action against Oman if it opposed U.S. efforts to reopen the strait fully
and freely.

In the early months of the conflict, Iran struck several Omani ports and coastal facilities. Since
then, Iran has not targeted the Sultanate directly but has continued to strike commercial tankers
off Oman’s territorial waters. Oman has provided navigational guidance and search and rescue aid
to tankers transiting the strait. U.S. forces have reportedly been offering air-based defense to ships
transiting the strait along Oman’s coast.1

As the U.S.-Iran conflict continues and as fighting between neighboring Saudi Arabia and the
Yemen-based Houthis has intensified, Oman has increased its regional diplomatic efforts
independent of U.S. policy. In October 2026, Omani Foreign Minister Badr Albusaidi called on
the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) members to reach a nonaggression pact with Iran, saying
that the GCC can “no longer rely upon the co-operation of external partners.”2

Although Oman has found itself diplomatically wedged between combatants in a region beset by
war, Oman’s geographic position has helped insulate its economy from some of the effects of
restrictions on shipping through the Strait of Hormuz.3 In contrast to the economic downturns all
other GCC members appear to be experiencing this year, Oman’s gross domestic product (GDP)
for 2026 is projected by the International Monetary Fund to grow by over 4%.4

Since becoming sultan in 2020, Sultan Haythim bin Tariq Al Said has pursued economic
diversification aimed at expanding the private sector and reducing Oman’s dependence on
hydrocarbon revenues. Like other oil-producing Gulf monarchies, Oman is pursuing a plan
(“Vision 2040”) to boost non-oil sectors, such as logistics, manufacturing, tourism, mining, space,
and the digital economy. While Oman’s decades-long social contract has brought it prosperity,
Oman lacks the hydrocarbon reserves of most other GCC states. The gradual transition away
from hydrocarbon extraction and toward human capital-driven growth is arguably the biggest
challenge facing the Sultanate over the long term.

Oman has a bicameral parliament with limited legislative powers; members may question
ministers, select their own leadership, and review government-drafted legislation, but law-making
powers rest firmly in the hands of the sultan. Members of the Consultative Council (Majlis As
Shura) are directly elected by Omani citizens, male and female, over 21 years of age.47 In order to

Consultative Council seats for women across the governorates. The sultan appoints all members
of the upper house or State Council (Majlis Ad Dawla). Appointees are usually former high-
ranking government officials, military officials, tribal leaders, and other notables.

Oman is an absolute monarchy in which, according to international human rights groups, state
authorities often target individuals and entities that are critical of the sultan or state.48 Freedom
House’s annual survey of political rights and civil liberties globally ranks Oman as “not free.” In
2026, Sultan Haythim issued a new Royal Decree making it illegal to publish online content that
violates morals or public order (e.g., sedition, sectarianism, social division).49 The Oman Human
Rights Commission is a government entity that monitors human rights cases in the Sultanate.

Oman’s Economic Transformation

Sultan Haythim leads a stable, high-income nation (as categorized by the World Bank), though
Oman faces challenges in maintaining its decades-long social contract, in which oil has brought
prosperity to Omani citizens in exchange for their apparent acceptance of the sultan’s absolute
rule. Like the other GCC states—Saudi Arabia, Qatar, the United Arab Emirates (UAE), Bahrain,
and Kuwait—Oman derives most of its state income (over 70%) from hydrocarbon production
and export.50 However, Oman has the lowest per capita GDP in the GCC owing to its modest
levels of oil and gas production relative to its GCC neighbors.51

Although higher global prices stemming from the conflict with Iran and various attempts to
circumvent the Strait of Hormuz using Omani ports have boosted the economy, the Omani
government had begun to explore other options to generate revenue and cut spending—including
on development expenditures—well before the outbreak of the current conflict.52 Oman
introduced a Value Added Tax in 2021. In June 2025, Oman became the first GCC country to
impose a personal income tax.53 Though the new tax only affects a small number of high-earning
Omanis, it could, according to one report, “lead to a recalibration of the relationship between
citizens and their government.”54

Economists note that while Oman’s economy has been boosted by its neighbors’ maneuvers to
circumvent the Strait of Hormuz through proposed pipeline, rail, and port connections to Oman,
this growth will likely moderate should oil prices begin to decline as a result of greater stability in
the Gulf. However, according to the Economist Intelligence Unit, “A prolonged regional conflict
could sustain elevated hydrocarbon prices for an extended period, further strengthening Omani
fiscal and current-account surpluses.”55

To date, the Sultanate has used its relatively limited oil-generated wealth to provide citizens with
subsidized goods, low taxes, housing support, and public sector salaries. Nevertheless, at current
oil production rates (estimated at a million barrels per day or bpd),56 and barring any new
discoveries or major technological innovations, Oman could exhaust its proven oil reserves
(around 5 billion barrels-ranked 21st globally)57 in around 20 years.

Given the possibility of declining production, the Sultanate has embarked upon a diversification
strategy called “Vision 2040.” Under that whole-of-government approach, domestic and foreign
investment is being channeled into other sectors, such as tourism, software, manufacturing, green
hydrogen, renewable energy, and mineral-processing. With 64% of the population under 30, the
Sultan has directed the Omani government to monitor the implementation of Vision 2040 on a
regular basis.58 According to Bloomberg, “Just a few years ago, Oman’s ratings were placed in
junk territory by all three major rating companies and concerns mounted that it could require
financial support from wealthier neighbors. Fiscal reforms, higher oil prices and privatizations
have since helped Oman cut debt and restore its investment-grade ratings.”59

As other Gulf Arab monarchies attempt to enact their own economic diversification plans, it
remains to be seen what comparative advantages the Sultanate can capitalize on relative to its
Gulf neighbors. Though the non-oil sectors of the economy (e.g., transportation, financial
services, construction) have experienced recent growth, manufacturing has remained flat and the
bulk of foreign investment in Oman through 2025 was in the oil and natural gas sector.60

U.S.-Omani Defense Cooperation

Oman was the first Persian Gulf state to sign a formal accord permitting the U.S. military to use
its facilities (1980) as part of the two countries’ long-standing strategic partnership. In 2019,
Oman and the United States signed a “Strategic Framework Agreement” that expands the U.S.-
Oman facilities access agreements by allowing U.S. forces to use the ports of Salalah and Duqm;
the latter is large enough to handle U.S. aircraft carriers.62
U.S.-Omani security cooperation is ongoing. Through the U.S. Department of Defense’s63
National Guard Bureau State Partnership Program, the Arizona National Guard has partnered
with the Sultan of Oman’s Armed Forces. U.S. arms sales to Oman are modest when compared
with U.S. sales to Oman’s neighbors, such as Saudi Arabia and the UAE. From 1950 to 2024, the
United States completed nearly $3.4 billion worth of Foreign Military Sales cases with Oman.64
In 2026, the State Department notified Congress of a potential $188 million sale to Oman of
equipment to sustain its fleet of F-16 fighters.65

U.S.-Omani Economic Relations

The United States is one of the largest foreign direct investors in Oman after the United Kingdom
and has registered a cumulative trade surplus of $6.3 billion since 2006.68 The U.S.-Oman Free
Trade Agreement was signed on January 19, 2006, and ratified by Congress (P.L. 109-283, signed
by the President September 26, 2006). It was intended to facilitate Oman’s access to the U.S.
economy and accelerate Oman’s efforts to diversify its economy. The United States is one of
Oman’s largest trading partners. In 2025, the total volume of bilateral trade was $3.3 billion.
According to the U.S. Department of Commerce, total U.S. investment in Oman stood at $20
billion as of late 2025, and principal U.S. merchandise exports to Oman included vehicles,
aircraft, petroleum products, chemicals, plastics, and industrial engines.69 In July 2026, the Trump
Administration imposed a 12.5% tariff on most U.S. imports from Oman as a result of an
investigation that found Oman and various economies failed to impose and effectively enforce
bans on imports made with forced labor. Certain categories of goods are subject to higher tariffs
globally, including aluminum, of which Oman is an exporter.70
