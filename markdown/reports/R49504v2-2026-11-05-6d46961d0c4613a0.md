Selected Background on the Endangered Species Act

The ESA aims to conserve species in danger of or threatened with extinction that are listed as
endangered or threatened under the act.1 Under the ESA, it is the policy of Congress that all
federal agencies shall seek to conserve threatened and endangered species, use their authorities in
furtherance of the ESA, and cooperate with state and local agencies to resolve water resource
issues in concert with conserving endangered species.2 The ESA is administered by the U.S. Fish
and Wildlife Service (FWS) and the National Marine Fisheries Service (NMFS) (together, the
Services).3 Domestic and foreign species of animals (both vertebrate and invertebrate) and plants
can be listed under the ESA as either endangered or threatened, according to an assessment of the

Definition of Best Scientific and Commercial Data Available

Pursuant to statutory requirements, the Secretary (of either the Interior or Commerce depending
on the species) must decide whether to list species based only on the best available scientific and
commercial data, using information to which the agency has access and information obtained
from the public through the agency rulemaking process.9 The statute does not define the phrase
“best available scientific and commercial data,” and the Services have not interpreted this phrase
through regulation. The Services are also directed to use the best available scientific and
commercial data to make several other determinations under the ESA, for example designating
critical habitat,10 formulating a biological opinion (BiOp) during Section 7 consultation,11 and
delisting a species.12

Section 7 Consultation
Section 7 of the ESA requires federal agencies to ensure that their discretionary actions,13
including authorizing (e.g., issuing a permit) or funding actions of nonfederal parties, are “not
likely to jeopardize the continued existence” of any endangered or threatened species or adversely
modify or destroy critical habitat.14 Federal agencies undertaking actions (action agencies),
whether directly or through federal approvals, permits, or funding for nonfederal parties,15 must
consult with the Services, as appropriate, if those actions might affect a listed species or
designated critical habitat. This process is referred to as Section 7 consultation (consultation).
Action agencies are, in the first instance, to evaluate possible effects of proposed projects on
listed species or designated critical habitat. For major construction activities, this evaluation is
known as a biological assessment (BA). If the action agency concludes that the action may
adversely affect listed species or critical habitat, it initiates formal consultation with the Services.
The Services’ goal in conducting formal consultation is to determine whether the agency action is
likely to jeopardize listed species or adversely modify or destroy their critical habitat and, if so,
whether there are reasonable and prudent alternatives (RPAs) that would avoid those outcomes.
To conclude formal consultation, the Services publish a BiOp articulating their findings with
respect to jeopardy and RPAs. So long as the Services determine the action is not likely to
jeopardize listed species or there are RPAs, the Services issue an incidental take statement (ITS)
with a BiOp authorizing a certain amount of unintentional (i.e., incidental) take expected to result
from carrying out the action.16 If that number is exceeded, the action agency must reinitiate
consultation.17 According to a study, the majority of consultations result in no jeopardy opinions,
and nearly all of the rest identify RPAs that permit the action agencies to go forward with the
proposed actions.18

The Services generally must conclude consultation within 90 days of receiving an action agency’s
request for consultation, unless the Services and the action agency mutually agree to a longer
period.19 For consultations involving a nonfederal party (e.g., a permit applicant), the 90-day
period may be extended up to an additional 60 days without the nonfederal party’s permission;
the Services and action agency are to provide the nonfederal party with a written explanation for
the delay, any information required to complete consultation, and the estimated time frame for
completion.20 The Services and action agency must obtain the nonfederal party’s permission to
extend the consultation beyond 150 days from its initiation.21

Judicial Review

The ESA does not specify a particular venue or time frame for legal challenges to BiOps issued
by the Services under Section 7. Instead, Section 11(c) assigns federal district courts jurisdiction
over “any actions arising” under the ESA, which would include challenges to BiOps.22 Section
706 of the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) supplies the standards of review for challenges to
BiOps issued by the Services.23 Such actions are subject to a six-year statute of limitations under
28 U.S.C. §2401(a).

Definition of Best Scientific and Commercial Data Available

BAAJA would add a definition of the phrases “best scientific and commercial data available” and
“best scientific data available.” The phrases would be defined to mean

           all relevant and objective scientific and commercial information available at the time of the
        agency action; and include credible and reliable data, quantitative analyses, conceptual and
        numerical models, and model results that account for known or potential sources of error;
         are applied using prevailing principles, methods, tools, and professional standards of
         practice; and are impartially gathered and objectively applied without presumptions or
         policy prescriptions that bias the application.24

Some stakeholders contend that this definition would “raise the evidentiary burden” for protecting
listed species under the ESA by lowering the ability of FWS and NMFS to make decisions
accounting for scientific uncertainty.25 Some other stakeholders assert that this definition would
allow project applicants to work from a “standard set of facts” when completing Section 7
consultations.26

While “best scientific and commercial data available” and “best scientific data available” lack a
statutory definition in the ESA as currently enacted, the federal courts have construed these terms
for decades. As a general matter, courts have held that these phrases require the Services to
consider scientific and commercial data available at the time the agency makes its decision,27
prohibit the Services from ignoring available studies even if they discount or discredit such
studies,28 and do not require an action agency to conduct new tests or generate new data that do
not already exist.29 Some courts have also held that these phrases include a “benefit of the doubt
in favor of the species” under conditions of uncertainty,30 but other courts have rejected this
reading of the phrases.31 The Supreme Court has never explicitly addressed whether the best
available science standard includes a “benefit of the doubt” or presumption. In the 1997 case
Bennett v. Spear, the Court appeared to cast doubt on interpreting the phrase to include that kind

Project Certainty

Title IV of BAAJA, titled “Project Certainty,” contains provisions that could affect ESA
implementation. Among other things, BAAJA Section 1401 would prohibit a federal agency from
revoking, rescinding, withdrawing, terminating, suspending, amending, or altering a BiOp or ITS,
or other applicable federal authorization or permit in effect on or after September 16, 2026. This

1403 would generally require federal agencies to render a decision on certain complete
applications for federal authorizations or permits within one year after the date on which the latest
of the covered applications is deemed to be complete. This requirement would apply to federal
authorizations or permits for which an environmental impact statement (EIS) or environmental
assessment (EA) is not required.51 It would apply to actions that require a BiOp but not an EIS or
EA. This may include projects or actions where the scope or magnitude of the action’s impact
may be considered minor, or projects or actions that are granted categorical exclusions under the
National Environmental Policy Act.52 Environmental analysis under Section 7 consultation
typically is still required for determining any potential impacts of the activity on ESA-listed
species.53 Sections 1401-1403 of BAAJA would provide for judicial review of the application of
any exception, of any “improperly denied” authorization, or of an agency’s failure to comply with
a one-year decision deadline. Under particular circumstances, the bill would provide remedies to
the permit holder or applicant, including injunctive relief and monetary damages up to the full
project costs in certain situations.

may result in court orders requiring changes to or reissuance of a BiOp.

authorization, several exceptions contained in the provision may apply to the reinitiation of
consultation or to court orders requiring amendments to a BiOp. BAAJA Section 1401 likely

where a permit holder (in this case, the holder of an ITS) violates the terms of the permit or
