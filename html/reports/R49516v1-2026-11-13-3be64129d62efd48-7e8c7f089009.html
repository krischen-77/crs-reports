Summary

The corporate tax is the third-largest source of federal revenue, but its importance as a revenue source has diminished considerably over time. Corporate tax receipts as a share of GDP declined from 6.2% in 1951 to 1.6% in 2025, while receipts as a share of federal revenue declined from 34% in 1951 to 9.6% in 2024. Most of that decline occurred before 1980 because of rate reductions and investment subsidies. The marginal effective corporate tax rate on investment in structures and equipment declined more significantly, from 63% in 1953 to 6% in 2025.

The corporate tax is a backstop for the individual income tax: With no corporate tax, individuals could funnel business income into the corporate form. In some ways, the corporate tax changes in 2017 made the corporate tax a more attractive alternative for some high-income taxpayers, but not usually by significant amounts.

The corporate tax reduces the demand for investment and thus the amount of capital and output in the economy. A reduction in output could reduce the tax base and cause revenues to fall with higher tax rates, so that a corporate tax increase could lose revenue. The reduction in capital could also cause some of the burden of the tax to fall on labor. Some evidence suggests that investment demand increases with lower taxes, but the supply of capital is limited, reducing the effects on output and wages. In addition, debt finance is subsidized by the corporate tax, and that subsidy increases as corporate tax rates increase. A corporate tax cut financed by debt could eventually crowd out capital through government borrowing. Studies of the effect of the 2017 corporate tax cut generally did not provide strong evidence of an effect on investment.

Recent studies estimating the rate at which revenue would start to fall with a tax increase tend to find that the revenue-maximizing tax rate is high, with a number of studies estimating about 70%. That is, in part, because a flow of capital from abroad decreases the pretax rate of return as it increases the capital stock. Other empirical studies found lower rates, but those findings may be explained by statistical issues. The limits on increases in capital also suggest it is likely that little, if any, of the burden of the corporate tax falls on wages, indicating that the corporate tax is progressive.

The corporate marginal effective tax rate in the aggregate is estimated to be relatively low at 7.1%, according to a CRS model. For a given asset type, the noncorporate tax rate is similar, so there are no significant distortions between the corporate and noncorporate sectors. However, there are wide differences in the estimated tax rates by asset type and form of debt finance. Debt-financed investments are generally subject to subsidies, estimated at 18% of pretax profit (i.e., for each dollar of profit the government pays the investor $0.18), while equity-financed investment are subject to a tax of 19%. Equipment and intangibles are subject to low or negative rates, while most structures are subject to significant positive tax rates. At the average level of debt finance, aggregated groups of assets are subject to a range of tax from -15% (a negative tax rate or subsidy) for intangibles to 18% for certain structures.

With the 2017 changes, the United States has become a low-tax country compared to other large countries. Among the 10 largest economies by GDP, the United States previously had the highest combined national and subnational statutory corporate tax rates, whereas it is now lower than all but three countries. The United States tended to have a marginal effective tax rate lower than most countries before the 2017 change, but it now has the second-lowest marginal effective tax rate (with only Italy having a lower rate). It also has lower statutory and effective tax rates than two smaller countries that are important trading partners, Canada and Mexico. This suggests that if national corporate tax rates are creating a distortion in the worldwide allocation of capital, it is to locate too much tangible capital in the United States. There is, however, a remaining issue of profit shifting to tax haven countries, and evidence suggests that such profit shifting continues.

Given the limits on investment and the limited differences in corporate and noncorporate taxation, potential revisions could focus on narrowing the differentials across assets and forms of finance. These could include providing partial expensing to all assets, disallowing a portion of interest deductions, or moving to a cash flow tax. There are also further international revisions that could address profit shifting. Some evidence suggests that the current subsidy for investments in research could be simplified and increased to more closely reflect spillover effects. There are also a few special tax provisions that could be reconsidered. Another potential revision would be to tax all large partnerships and Subchapter S corporations under the corporate rather than the individual tax.

Introduction

The current corporate tax has become a relatively small share of GDP, due in part to lower rates and in part to provisions that reduce the base of the corporate tax. This report examines the changing importance of the corporate tax and reviews the issues that surround the tax, including its importance as a backstop to the individual income tax, and the effect of the tax on investment, revenues, distribution, and the allocation of investment by type, form of finance, organizational form, and location. The final sections discusses some options for revision.

The Corporate Tax As A Revenue Source

The corporate tax is the third-largest source of federal revenue, but its importance as a revenue source has diminished considerably over time.

Magnitude And Historical Pattern

Current corporate taxes are low by historical standards, whether measured as a share of output or based on the effective tax rate on income. Figure 1 shows the decline in corporate tax revenues from 6.2% of GDP in 1951 to 1.6% in 2025, a 74% decline. The share is projected to decline further, to 1.3%, after the passage of the FY2025 reconciliation law.1

Overall federal receipts were a steady share of GDP (18%) from 1951 to 2024, but the corporate tax declined from 34% to 9.6% of federal receipts, as shown in Figure 2. Today, the corporate tax is the third-largest federal revenue source, behind the individual income tax (which was 8.3% of GDP in 2024, and projected to rise to 9.4% in FY2031), and the payroll tax (about 6% of GDP in 2024). It is more significant than excise taxes (0.3% of GDP) and estate and gift taxes (0.1%).

Corporate tax revenues as a share of GDP are affected by both corporate profits as a share of GDP and the effective tax rate on those profits. The overall decline in corporate taxes as a percentage of GDP is due to legislative changes in the tax system, because corporate profits as a percentage of GDP grew over this period.2 The major legislative changes were the decrease in the top statutory corporate tax rate and changes in depreciation rules, as well as the provision of investment tax credits at some points.

The top statutory corporate tax rate fell from 52% to 34% from 1953 to 2017 (36% decrease), then to 21% in 2018 and after (a total 60% decrease). However, the decline was small in the initial period, with a reduction to 46% through 1986. During those years, however, more generous depreciation rules were enacted in 1953, 1963, 1971, and 1981, and an investment credit for equipment was allowed during most years from 1962 to 1986. In addition, the growing share of investment in intangibles—which receive favorable treatment, including a research credit enacted in 1981—contributed to lower tax rates. In the 21st century, many investments came to be expensed (deducted when incurred).

Depreciation changes delay the timing of taxes, reducing revenues more in the initial years after the change, and are responsible, along with business cycles, for fluctuation in the share of corporate taxes as a percentage of GDP. These changes have been important to investment incentives, and the value of depreciation can be accounted for if measured by the marginal effective tax rate (METR). The corporate firm-level METR on equipment and structures captures both the tax rate and other provisions—largely accelerated depreciation, including expensing (immediate deduction of the cost) of many investments, and interest deductions. From 1953 to 2025, the METR fell from 63% to 6%, a 90% decrease. These tax benefits tend to be imperfectly captured in overall receipts because they are timing benefits.3

The Role of the Corporate Tax in Backstopping the Individual Tax

Measuring corporate tax revenue falls short of describing the tax's full role in contributing to federal revenues because the corporate tax protects the collection of individual income taxes. If taxes on individual income are imposed, a significant corporate income tax is necessary to forestall the use of the corporation as a tax shelter. Without a corporate tax, high-income individuals could channel funds into corporations, and, with a large part of earnings retained, obtain lower tax rates than if they operated in partnership, proprietorship, or S corporation (corporations with a limited number of shareholders that can elect to be taxed as individuals) form.4

The share of business income earned in the corporate sector has declined from 80% in 1980 to less than 50% in 2015.5 In 2022, the most recent year of available data, corporations were responsible for 53% of business income.6 The share of unincorporated businesses grew due to increases in partnerships and S corporations; the share of proprietorships declined.

As suggested by the growth in unincorporated business forms, wealthy business owners may be quick to take advantage of tax rate differentials, which historically favored unincorporated businesses. In 1986, individual tax rates were lowered (the top rate fell from 70% to 28%, although they were eventually increased to 39.6%), but the combined corporate tax rate in 1986 (on the firm and on distributions) had been high relative to the individual tax rate.

Although the top tax rate on corporations prior to the 2017 revisions (35%) was close to the top individual rate (39.6%), the corporate tax was graduated. Consequently, high-income taxpayers had an incentive to shift part of their income into a corporation because corporate tax rates were graduated (15% on the first $50,000 and 25% on the next $25,000) and were lower than the top marginal tax rate. This opportunity, however, was restricted by (1) limiting to one the number of corporations income could be shifted to; (2) limiting the amounts on which rates were graduated; and (3) disallowing graduated rates for personal service corporations. In recognition of the potential use of corporations as shelters, past tax law has contained a tax on accumulated earnings. If dividends were taxed as ordinary income and the accumulated earnings tax was strict enough, it was difficult to use the corporate form to shelter a great deal of income.

This tax shelter constraint on lowering the corporate rate may have become more binding because of the lower rates on dividends enacted as part of the George W. Bush Administration's corporate relief package in the Jobs and Growth Tax Relief Reconciliation Act of 2003 (P.L. 108-27), although rates were increased in 2010 and 2013.7 The 2017 revision also changed the relative benefits of operating in corporate versus noncorporate form by lowering the top corporate rate to 21% and the top individual rate to 37%, which would tend to make the corporate form more attractive to high-income individuals. However, it also eliminated the graduated rates that allowed a restricted opportunity for use of the corporate form as a shelter. It also allowed a deduction for 20% of pass-through income for unincorporated businesses, although the deduction was phased out for certain taxpayers.8 The individual provisions were scheduled to expire after 2025, but were made permanent in 2025; the corporate rate cut was permanent. Businesses at high income levels can be subject to an additional 3.8% tax on their income either due to the Medicare A hospital insurance tax or the net investment income tax applied to passive investment income, passive business income, dividends, and capital gains. Active income from a partnership or Subchapter S firm that is not wages is not subject to the tax.

Unless a corporation distributed little of its income, combined rates at the corporate and dividend level were usually higher than individual rates, favoring the unincorporated form even more for individuals subject to lower tax rates. After the 2017 revision, the top corporate and noncorporate rates were closer together for the top individual rates, although with little distribution of income the corporate form could sometimes become beneficial even without the graduated rates. If most income were distributed, the tax on the corporate form of investment would be higher than most individual rates. For the intermediate case of 50% distribution, the corporate tax moved from being less favorable to more favorable. Although the 2017 changes weakened the preference for noncorporate form and therefore could be argued to weaken the use of the corporation as a backstop for the individual tax, they also eliminated the graduated corporate rates that made the corporate form attractive for some taxpayers.

For moderate-income taxpayers, who are subject to lower rates and are more likely to benefit from the pass-through deduction, the individual form would still be favored. In general, the corporate tax still acts to protect the individual tax revenue.

Effects on Investment, the Revenue-Maximizing Tax Rate, and Distributional Effects

The corporate tax can discourage capital investment from abroad and encourage investment of U.S. savings in other countries. Investment flows should be determined by the marginal effective tax rate. A reduction in the capital stock can reduce output, revenues, and wages. These international investment flows underlie two issues. One is the revenue-maximizing tax rate, the rate at which further increases in the corporate tax would lose revenue. The other is the distributional effects. The corporate tax can fall on capital income or labor income. The share of the burden, if any, that reduces wages (i.e., that falls on labor income) makes the corporate tax less progressive.

Investment Effects

In the long run, the effect of the corporate tax is driven by the ease with which capital can be substituted with labor (the factor substitution elasticity), the willingness of investors and firms to shift assets across countries, and the substitutability of imports for domestic production.10

The investment effect is based on the change in the user cost of capital, or the required output of an additional unit of investment, which has three parts: the after-tax return to shareholders and creditors, the tax paid over time, and the output necessary to replace depreciating capital. Taxes have a smaller proportional effect on short-lived investment because more of the user cost reflects economic depreciation.

The investment effect also depends on the ability to add capital to labor and thus to substitute capital for labor in the production process, measured by the factor substitution elasticity (the percentage change in the ratio of capital to labor divided by the percentage change in the ratio of user cost to wage). Most evidence suggests that this value is around -0.5 to -1.0, although it may be above 1.0 in absolute value.11 At a unitary factor substitution elasticity, a 1% decrease in the user cost of capital leads to a 1% increase in the capital stock.

However, this measure alone does not capture the effect of a change, because responses in the economy also drive the effect. The expansion of capital causes wages to rise as labor becomes more productive. This rise in wages causes a greater effect on capital, generally the factor substitution elasticity divided by the share of labor income given a fixed price of output. With that share at around 75%, an initial decrease of 1% in the user cost causes an increase of 1.33% in investment.

Other factors, however, cause the expansion of capital to be smaller, most notably a limit in the supply of capital, either through imports of capital from abroad or limits on savings. The evidence suggests that capital flow from abroad, while relatively elastic, is far from infinite, with an elasticity of around 3.12 According to a model estimating the effects of alternative elasticity, an elasticity of 3 reduces the effect on capital by 68% compared to the effect if capital were freely available at the same rate of return.13

It is also possible that a decrease in the corporate tax rate could cause the capital stock to fall because it reduces the value of interest deductions, and thus discourages debt-financed investment. This effect could more than offset any positive effect on inflows of equity capital if debt is more internationally mobile than equity.

The capital stock could also expand through increased savings. However, domestic savings are a less likely source of an increased capital stock. While one model of savings predicts that savings are perfectly elastic with respect to the tax rate, this model requires some extreme assumptions about human behavior, such as perfect information, an infinite planning horizon, and no liquidity constraints. Models allowing for finite lives (such as the life-cycle models) find results that vary, but if the revenue loss is made up by higher taxes on labor, there is little or no effect. Some economists believe that these models are inappropriate, as they assume too much information and skill on the part of individuals; these economists suggest that individuals use rules of thumb, such as fixed savings rates or targets. These rules of thumb suggest that a cut in capital income taxes either has no effect on saving or reduces savings. These economists also point out that most empirical evidence does not point to an increase in savings; historically, savings rates do not appear to respond to reduced tax rates.14

Note also that the increased government debt due to a corporate tax cut directly reduces the capital stock. This debt compounds and grows without limit over time, and would eventually more than offset an investment increase. Thus, the effect of corporate tax cuts depends on whether they are offset with spending cuts or other tax increases.

Some statistical studies of the effect of the corporate tax on investment focused on the effects of the 2017 tax revision. Several of these studies found significant effects on investment. However, a subsequent review found methodological issues that may have affected their results.15 In addition, the studies' findings were inconsistent with certain observed changes in investment following the revision.

Short-run investment effects are a limited guide to the long-run effects. The percentage change in investment can be larger in the short run as the economy is adjusting to the new long-run capital stock (and the accompanying change in the long-run investment level). It can also be smaller because of adjustment costs. In addition, any government debt due to a tax reduction compounds and grows over time.

The Revenue-Maximizing Tax Rate

The revenue-maximizing tax rate depends on the same factors that drive investment. These issues are discussed in more detail in CRS Report R48913, Corporate Taxation: The Revenue-Maximizing Tax Rate. That report shows that economic models predict, even when setting the factors most conducive to a low tax rate, the revenue-maximizing tax rate is around 70%, and higher if there is imperfect portfolio substitution. The report also discusses empirical studies that found a lower rate of around 30% based on cross-country estimates and analyzes methodological issues that may have resulted in less accurate estimations at this lower rate. The report also examines a more recent body of research focused on the taxable income elasticity.

Distributional Effects

The distributional effects of the corporate tax depend on the extent to which it falls on labor income rather than capital income. As shown in Table 2, income becomes more concentrated in capital sources in the higher-income groups. While capital income is 32% of income overall, it is 11% of income in the lowest 40% of the population. For the top 20%, capital income is almost half of income. Within that top group, the share rises, reaching 73% for the top 1%. This concentration would be even greater if an important source of income for very high-income individuals, unrealized capital gains, were included. Thus, a greater share of the corporate tax falling on labor income would make the tax either less progressive or regressive.

If an increase in the corporate tax causes a decrease in net capital flows, the smaller capital stock can cause labor to become less productive and result in a falling wage. An extensive body of research has developed, through both (1) formal models that reflect underlying parameters that determine the burden on wages and (2) reduced-form statistical estimates based on relating wages to the corporate rate using cross-country or cross-state data.

This research is reviewed in CRS Report R49358, Analyzing the Incidence of the Corporate Income Tax: Models and Statistical Estimates. In general, that analysis suggests that labor bears a small share of the tax (if any), likely not more than 20%, and examines methodological issues with certain statistical estimates that produce higher results. It is also possible that increasing the corporate tax reduces investment because it decreases the value of interest deductions, which may reduce net capital inflows if debt is more internationally mobile than equity.

Efficiency Effects

Domestic Investment

The corporate tax and its effect on investment depend not only on the statutory tax rate, but also on the tax base, most importantly depreciation, or how quickly costs of investment are deducted. Most equipment and intangibles, as well as manufacturing structures and some costs of oil and gas extraction, are expensed. The effective tax rate for an expensed asset is zero. The tax rate is increased with shareholder taxes, and decreased with debt finance. Finally, the total cost of investment is affected by taxes paid on interest, dividends, and capital gains by creditors and shareholders.

The corporate tax, therefore, can contribute to several types of potential investment distortions through differential tax treatment:

  • distortions across types of investments;
  • distortions between debt and equity;
  • distortions between corporate and noncorporate investments; and
  • distortions in savings (i.e., between present and future consumptions).

The tax treatment of different investments, as measured by the METR, is shown in Table 3. In all columns, the differences between tax burdens by type of asset are shown. The variations across assets for the corporate-level tax reflect the difference in depreciation, as well as the research tax credit. The lowest tax rates are on intangibles, which are largely expensed (costs deducted immediately). In the case of research and development, taxes are reduced by the research tax credit, resulting in an overall negative tax rate (a subsidy). Equipment is also expensed, except for a small share of equipment used by regulated electric and gas utilities, leading to a rate close to zero. Overall, the provisions that reduce income subject to the tax reduce the effective tax rate from 21% to 12.9%, a 39% reduction.

Tax rates on capital gains and dividends increase the tax rate by about 7 percentage points to 19.7% overall. Although these tax rates on dividends and capital gains are above 20%, most shareholders do not pay tax on these assets because they are held in tax-exempt retirement funds.

Debt finance significantly reduces effective tax rates because nominal interest is deducted while the real return on investment is taxed at effective rates that are lower than the statutory rate. Taxes paid by creditors do not provide much of an offset since most interest is not taxed because it is held through exempt retirement accounts or provided in the form of free banking services. Fully debt-financed investments are subsidized at an 18% rate, not taxed. There is a current limit on the deduction for interest that is considered excessive (interest above 30% of profits before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization), but this provision (which is incorporated in Table 3) has a small effect on METRs.

Noncorporate and corporate investments are taxed at similar rates within most asset classes. Although the individual tax rates are higher than the corporate rate, some businesses are eligible for a pass-through deduction that reduces taxable income by 20%. The overall noncorporate tax rate is higher in part because noncorporate assets tend to be more concentrated in assets taxed more heavily.

Overall, the METR analysis indicates marked differences across assets and forms of finance, but relatively small differences between corporate and noncorporate investments. With an overall effective marginal tax rate of 7%, the distortion between savings and consumption is small.

International Investment

Tax rules can affect the efficiency of capital allocation around the world, and if the U.S. corporate tax rate is different from other countries, it can cause misallocations of capital. The misallocations of physical capital are affected by the marginal effective tax rates. Taxes can also result in profit shifting, which depends on the statutory tax rate.

Table 4 compares the statutory corporate tax rates and the marginal effective tax rates of the 10 largest economies measured by GDP. These rates reflect both national and average subnational taxes, producing a statutory tax rate of 25.7% for the United States. For statutory tax rates, the U.S. rate is lower than all but three countries, which have a rate of 25%. The cut in corporate tax rates from 35% to 21% moved the United States from having the highest tax rate of the large countries (38.1%) to among the lowest.16 The marginal effective tax rate of 3.7% makes the United States a low-corporate-income-tax country, although the METR for the United States before the 2017 revisions (22.97%) was still lower than most large countries'.

These data indicate that the United States is a low-tax country with respect to investment, which would result in too much investment allocated to the United States for worldwide economic efficiency.17 However, even with a 25.7% combined statutory tax rate, there is still an incentive to shift profits abroad to low-tax countries, which is particularly feasible with intangible assets. Profit shifting occurs when firms use intercompany transfer prices for the sale of intangible assets or the charging of royalties to foreign affiliates. By understating these prices, the firm can shift profits out of the United States. Another method of shifting profits is to locate debt (and therefore interest deductions) in the United States.

Profit shifting is partly due to not taxing foreign-source profits at the same rates that apply to domestic profits. This shifting is not due to the corporate effective tax rate, but rather to tax benefits for investment abroad. In 2017, the United States moved to a new system of international taxation that taxes income from foreign subsidiaries on a current basis (rather than only when paid as dividends) but at lower rates, with the tax aimed at profit shifting. The provisions were revised in 2025. Under current law, the minimum tax on foreign-source income (called net tested CFC income) is 40% lower than the rate on domestic profit. There is also a reduction in the tax rate for foreign-source income from assets held domestically (called foreign-derived deduction-eligible income, or FDDEI) that reduces the tax rate on this income by a third and is designed to achieve a similar treatment for assets held in the United States or abroad. The system also allows a credit against U.S. tax due (currently limited to 90% of taxes paid). The credit is limited to U.S. tax paid on foreign-source income, but this limit is applied on an overall basis (rather than country-by-country), so that taxes in high-tax countries can be used to offset U.S. taxes on income in low-tax or no-tax countries. In this case, income in tax havens can avoid any U.S. tax and could pay no taxes. The 2025 change also eliminated a deduction for a percentage of tangible assets, meant to focus the tax on intangible assets.18

There are indications of profit shifting, which can be shown by comparing the source of profits with the location of tangible investment. Table 5 shows the countries with the largest shares of foreign profits of U.S. multinationals and their tax rates. The three most common locations—Ireland, Singapore, and Switzerland—have been long viewed as tax havens, although Ireland has recently made changes that eliminate tax avoidance techniques and is participating in the worldwide global minimum tax regime. These three countries account for 30.5% of U.S. multinationals' foreign profits. Cyprus, also on the list, is considered a tax haven and has a noticeable share of profits although it is a small country. The average tax rates (cash taxes divided by profits) for these four countries are lower than those in the other countries: Ireland has an effective tax rate of 15.2%, and the tax rates for Singapore, Switzerland, and Cyprus are considerably below the statutory rate, while the rates for other countries are more in line with statutory tax rates. The profits for those countries, which are either large countries or geographically close, probably reflect real economic activity. The U.S. average effective tax rate is 22.8%, similar to the statutory rate.

An analysis by the Penn Wharton Budget Model found that the 2017 change in tax regime did not have much effect on the U.S. effective tax rate on foreign-source income, which remained around 2%.20 It also found the effective tax rate including foreign taxes remained about the same at around 13%. The U.S. rate would be higher under current law.

The elimination of the 10% deduction for tangible assets refocuses the tax regime on all income, not only tangible income, which has consequences for the allocation of capital investment across countries. That effect depends on the depreciation of foreign-source assets as well as tax rates. Investment in equipment abroad is treated less generously than domestic investment under U.S. tax rules used for measuring GILTI, as expensing is not allowed and the slower alternative depreciation system is imposed. This system allows recovery in equal amounts over 10 years for most equipment. There is little difference in the treatment of structures. However, the treatment also depends on how foreign countries treat recovery and would be reflected in the METRs in Table 6, which are higher than the U.S. tax rate on domestic investment in equipment and structures.

Potential Revisions

There are broad and narrow changes in the tax code that could potentially increase allocational efficiency, address international profit-shifting, and reduce favorable treatment for a narrow set of taxpayers.

Narrowing Differentials Across Assets And/Or Forms of Finance

As shown in the discussion of economic efficiency, the METR on investment in equipment, structures, and intangibles is about 7%, a relatively small tax. However, this composite rate is the result of widely varying rates by asset type and form of finance.

Congress could consider various options to make the corporate (and noncorporate) tax more neutral across assets and forms of finance. These options could also be applied to noncorporate investments.

Substituting Partial Expensing For All Assets

One option is to allow only partial expensing, but to allow it for all assets. Because partial expensing is a neutral subsidy, this option would eliminate most of the asset differentials in the corporate sector. Some variations would remain because the depreciation systems of the remaining assets also contain some subsidies, although they would be smaller. This expensing would not apply to land and inventories. Congress could choose an expensing ratio that aimed to maintain revenue neutrality over some time horizon.

Substituting Partial Expensing For All Assets and Disallowing Proportionate Interest Deductions

This option would add to the partial expensing a disallowance for the share of interest deductions reflecting the share of investment expensed. It would allow a larger share of assets to be expensed with revenue neutrality due to the revenue gain from disallowing interest. It would narrow the differentials across both assets and forms of finance.

Substituting A Cash Flow Tax

A cash flow tax would allow full expensing for investments and disallow interest deductions, effectively making the tax a consumption rather than an income tax. The METR would only reflect taxes of shareholders and creditors. The CRS METR model estimates effective tax rates of 12.6% for debt and 7.8% for equity, reflecting the average tax rates that apply to the share of interest, dividends, and capital gains that are taxed. The statutory tax rate could be adjusted to achieve a revenue objective.

An argument for such a move is that the current tax system contains some of the elements of a cash flow tax, but does not disallow the deduction for interest that is appropriate for a cash flow tax. However, the incidence of a cash flow tax using the corporate tax base is on old assets. Again, such a tax already effectively exists for a large share of the corporate income tax.

If assets such as land and inventories were excluded from the expensing regime, a partial deduction for interest could be allowed to reflect the shares represented by these assets.

Some coordination would be needed for foreign-source income, where expensing is not allowed in computing the U.S. tax due or the limit on the foreign tax credit. The same rules could be applied to foreign-source income

Substituting A Destination-Based Cash Flow Tax

A variation of the previous options is to substitute a destination-based cash flow tax (DBCFT). Such a proposal has recently been made in a Brookings Institution study.21 A proposal for a DBCFT was included in the Better Way Tax Reform Task Force Blueprint proposed by then-House Speaker Paul Ryan in 2016.22 A destination-based tax is designed to apply the tax only to domestic consumption, so the tax is imposed on imports and rebated on exports. This aspect of the tax created concerns from businesses relying on imports, although any effect on import or export prices should be eliminated by exchange rate adjustments.23 Imposing the tax only on production that is for domestic consumption would eliminate incentives to shift production abroad.

Disallowing Deductions For The Inflation Portion of Interest

A narrower provision to reduce the favoritism to debt is to disallow a deduction for the portion of interest that reflects inflation. For example, if the interest rate is 6% and the inflation rate is 2%, one-third of deductions would be disallowed. An alternative method would be to include the gain from repaying debt in cheaper dollars, which has the same effect.

Revisions In The International Tax Regime

It appears that additional tax on U.S.-source income from tangible investments abroad is not justified on efficiency grounds because the countries where the most tangible investment is located have higher METRs than those imposed on U.S. investment.

The main issue remaining that could be addressed by tax changes is profit shifting. Perhaps the most significant change that could address this issue is to apply the foreign tax credit on a country-by-country basis. This would prevent unused credits in high-tax jurisdictions from offsetting U.S. tax on low- or no-tax countries, where most profit shifting occurs. This change, along with full taxation of income of foreign subsidiaries, would also allow the elimination of the deduction for foreign-derived intangible income from intangibles in the United States. Another change to address profit shifting could allocate worldwide debt to each location depending on its share of assets or income. These provisions would raise revenues.

In the 119th Congress, the No Breaks For Outsourcing Act, S. 409 and H.R. 995, would address profit shifting by (1) taxing CFC income at the full rate, (2) imposing a per-country foreign tax credit limit, and (3) allocating interest based of the share of earnings before taxes, interest, depreciation, and amortization.

The bill would also make changes to inversions, where a U.S. firm generally merges with a foreign firm and the foreign location becomes the parent location.24 Currently, if the firm is 80% owned by the former U.S. company shareholders, it will be treated as a domestic firm, and if it is 60% to 80% owned, it will be limited in the deductions and credits it can take against U.S. income. The bill would change the 80% limit to 60% and the 60% limit to 50%. It would also treat firms managed and controlled in the United States as domestic firms. These anti-inversion rules are also proposed in S. 3847 and H.R. 7493.

One of the significant anti-inversion provisions enacted in 2017 is scheduled to end in 2027. This provision taxes unrepatriated accumulated gains at 35% rather than 8% and 15% for firms that inverted after the 2017 tax revision.

Tax Treatment of Intangible Investments

Intangible investments include those in research, human capital, advertising, computer software, and artistic and literary works (movies, television, sound recordings). Most of these intangible investments are expensed, although sound recordings are subject to a dollar limit and investments in literary works must be recovered over time based on income forecasts. Investments in research are also eligible for a research tax credit.

Although these are all intangible investments, efficiency justifications vary. There is a case, some can argue, for subsidizing investments in research because these investments are estimated to provide spillover effects for the rest of the economy that are large relative to current tax subsidies. Thus, Congress might consider retaining expensing and simplifying and increasing the credit.25

Similar justifications generally do not appear for other intangible investments. In the case of advertising, there have been proposals over the years to capitalize these expenditures and deduct them over time. The Congressional Budget Office included a proposal in its budget options to amortize (deduct in equal amounts per year) half of advertising expenditures over 5 or 10 years.26

Other Corporate Tax Expenditures

Special provisions for measuring corporate income are listed in tax expenditure lists, including a list compiled by the Joint Committee on Taxation.27 Most of the largest tax expenditures have already been discussed (international, research, accelerated depreciation, and expensing). One significant remaining one is the low-income housing credit, the major program for expanding the supply of affordable housing, estimated to cost $14.8 billion in FY2026.28 Many economists suggest that providing tenant subsidies would be a more effective method of providing affordable housing.29

Other tax expenditures that Congress might consider are the exemption of tax for credit unions and several provisions that reduce the taxation of insurance company income.

Taxing Large Partnerships As Corporations

Owing in part to changes in state law that allowed the creation of large limited-liability corporations that did not meet the standards for being taxed as corporations, very large partnerships are not subject to the corporate tax. Taxing these partnerships as corporations would bring the tax treatment of large, multiple-investor enterprises closer together. Similarly, because of increases in the numbers of shareholders allowed for S Corporations that elect partnership treatment, some of these enterprises are large. A recent study proposed treating partnerships and S corporations with gross receipts over $25 million as corporations.30 Large real estate investment trusts (REITs) might also be considered for this treatment.
