Domestic Situation
Ecuador is an ethnically and geographically diverse country of 18.1 million people,                 Joshua Klein
encompassing parts of South America’s Pacific coast, Amazon Basin, and Andean highlands.        Analyst in Foreign Affairs
President Daniel Noboa (2023-present), of the right-leaning National Democratic Action (ADN)
movement, has largely centered his policymaking on addressing Ecuador’s public security crisis.
Since roughly 2021, illicit activity linked to transnational criminal organizations (TCOs) has
contributed to a security crisis in Ecuador, with gangs fighting for control of drug trafficking
routes and prisons and attempting to influence politics. Other aspects of Noboa’s agenda include pursuing International
Monetary Fund-backed economic reforms to increase investment and improve government finances. Noboa’s ADN has
forged an informal legislative majority with the support of smaller parties to advance his legislative agenda; he also has
sought to advance his agenda through two referendums, with mixed results.

Under the Noboa administration, some observers have voiced concerns about an erosion of democratic norms, including
judicial independence and press freedoms. Critics also have argued that several law enforcement investigations targeting
members of Ecuador’s largest political opposition party, left-of-center Citizen Revolution, are politically motivated. The
political opposition also has accused the Noboa administration of co-opting independent electoral institutions following
several decisions favorable for ADN ahead of subnational elections scheduled for November 2026. Noboa has rejected claims
of rising authoritarianism under his government.

U.S. Relations and Congressional Interests
Following a period of bilateral tensions under former President Rafael Correa (2007-2017), U.S.-Ecuador ties have
strengthened, including under President Noboa. During the second Trump Administration, the United States and Ecuador
have expanded bilateral security cooperation and conducted combined military operations to curtail drug trafficking and other
illicit activity linked to TCOs. Since August 2025, the U.S. government has designated four Ecuadorian gangs as foreign
terrorist organizations and specially designated global terrorists. The two countries also have signed nonbinding bilateral
agreements to allow the transfer of some third-country migrants from the United States to Ecuador (July 2025) and to
advance cooperation on critical minerals (February 2026) and trade (March 2026).

Congress, at times, has sought to shape the executive branch’s approach to U.S.-Ecuador relations. In 2022, for example,
Congress enacted the United States-Ecuador Partnership Act of 2022 as Title LV, Subtitle B, of the James M. Inhofe National
Defense Authorization Act for FY2023 (P.L. 117-263). Authorized until December 2027, the act includes measures aimed at
strengthening bilateral security cooperation and commercial ties to promote democracy and regional stability and to counter
malign foreign influence.

In the 119th Congress, Members have expressed a range of views on Ecuador and U.S. policy. Some Members have
commended President Noboa as a strong U.S. ally and called for an expansion of bilateral security cooperation to dismantle
TCOs. Some Members have voiced concern about human rights conditions amid Ecuador’s security crisis and possible
democratic erosion under President Noboa; those of this view have called on the Trump Administration to suspend combined
U.S.-Ecuador military operations and to ensure U.S. security assistance does not contribute to potential human rights abuses.
Looking ahead, Congress may continue to assess security, economic, and human rights conditions in Ecuador—including any
implications for regional security efforts—and consider legislative and other measures to shape bilateral relations.

Political and Economic Conditions

President Daniel Noboa of the right-leaning National Democratic Action (ADN) political
movement first took office in November 2023. Ecuadorians elected Noboa to complete the term
of former President Guillermo Lasso (2021-2023), who had called snap elections held in August
2023. In April 2025, Noboa won a full four-year presidential term after obtaining 55.6% of the
vote in a presidential runoff election against Luisa González of the left-of-center Citizen
Revolution (RC) movement.1 Although González alleged voter fraud in the election, international
observers expressed confidence in the results, while noting concerns about “conditions of
inequity during the campaign” that favored Noboa’s candidacy.2

In February 2025, Ecuador held legislative elections to elect all 151 members of the unicameral
National Assembly. Support was largely divided between ADN (66 seats) and RC (67 seats)
blocs. Noboa’s ADN has forged an informal legislative majority with the support of smaller
parties. Intraparty disagreements since the elections have reduced RC’s legislative bloc and
eroded the block’s voting cohesion.3

Governance and Human Rights Concerns

Some observers have voiced concerns about an erosion of democratic norms under the Noboa
administration. In August 2025, the National Assembly passed a law that increased government
oversight and regulation of nongovernmental organizations (NGOs), which President Noboa
argued was necessary to detect and prevent possible illicit financial flows tied to money
laundering and tax evasion.8 In late 2025, U.S.-based NGO Human Rights Watch asserted that
Noboa administration officials used the law to freeze the bank accounts of Indigenous and
environmental groups that had protested the government’s removal of diesel subsidies.9 Some
observers have asserted that President Noboa has used combative rhetoric to increase political
pressure on Ecuador’s Constitutional Court—potentially undermining judicial independence—
since the court suspended some Noboa-backed security legislation in mid-2025.10 Some groups
have raised alarms about deteriorating press freedoms, pointing to increased violence against
journalists by criminal groups and pressure from government officials.11

November 2026 Subnational Elections

On November 29, 2026, Ecuador is scheduled to hold subnational elections for more than 5,000
provincial, municipal, and local offices, including 23 prefectures (provincial government heads)
and 222 mayorships.12 Voters are also set to elect all seven members of the Council for Citizen
Participation and Social Control (Consejo de Participación Ciudadana y Control Social)—an
autonomous oversight entity charged with conducting the selection process for several
independent national offices, including the attorney general, human rights ombudsman,
comptroller, and electoral authorities, among other functions.13 All elected officials are scheduled
to take office on May 14, 2027, for four-year terms.

The political opposition has accused the Noboa administration of co-opting independent electoral
institutions following several decisions seemingly favorable for ADN ahead of the elections.14 In
March 2026, a judge on Ecuador’s Electoral Dispute Tribunal (TCE) imposed a nine-month
suspension on RC, the largest opposition party, amid an ongoing illicit campaign finance
investigation.15 The suspension prevents RC from fielding candidates in the elections. Later that
month, electoral authorities rescheduled the elections to be held in November 2026 instead of
February 2027, as originally scheduled, to avoid potential weather disruptions related to an
anticipated El Niño climate pattern.16 Some critics argued that the move was a politically
motivated attempt to reduce the time available for RC-affiliated candidates to change party
affiliation ahead of the elections.17 In April 2026, electoral officials dissolved two other political
organizations for alleged insufficient membership; the TCE reversed one party’s dissolution
following a legal challenge in June.18 In July 2026, the TCE suspended another left-of-center
party (AMIGO)—which had partnered with RC to field some of its candidates—under similar
circumstances to that of RC.19 In August 2026, the Inter-American Commission on Human
Rights, an independent regional commission, called on the Noboa administration to ensure “fair
political participation” in the upcoming elections.20

Security Crisis21

Homicide rates in Ecuador were among the lowest in Latin America for decades, but violence has
risen since roughly 2021; from 2023 to 2025, Ecuador reportedly had the highest homicide rate in
the region.22 The increase in violence has been driven primarily by increased competition among
Ecuadorian gangs affiliated with Mexican cartels and other TCOs, shifts in regional drug-
trafficking patterns, and entrenched corruption in Ecuador’s law enforcement and judicial
institutions. These gangs fight for control of drug trafficking routes, primarily in Ecuador’s
coastal provinces, that are used to ship Colombian and Peruvian cocaine through Ecuador’s ports
to international markets. In March 2026, a U.S. Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) official
stated that about 70% of the world’s cocaine is shipped from Ecuadorian ports.23 High-fatality
riots in prisons, from which criminal groups reportedly run their operations, have plagued
Ecuador since 2020. Ecuador also has faced increased violence tied to TCO-linked illicit mining
operations. Some TCOs appear to have infiltrated Ecuadorian government institutions. In late

Economic Conditions

After three years of declining economic growth amid security challenges and seasonal energy
shortages driven by drought and aging energy infrastructure, Ecuador’s economy rebounded with
3.7% gross domestic product (GDP) growth in 2025; the International Monetary Fund (IMF)
projects 2.7% GDP growth in 2026.27 Ecuador’s growth in 2025 was aided in part by low
inflation, an improved labor market, and strong non-oil exports.28 Although Ecuador is a net
energy exporter, which may allow it to benefit somewhat from increased international oil prices
tied to the 2026 U.S.-Israel conflict with Iran, elevated energy prices also have increased
government expenditures on remaining fuel subsidies that the Noboa administration has not yet
phased out.29 Fluctuations in rainfall tied to the El Niño climate pattern could also disrupt
agricultural production and increase food prices, according to the Economist Intelligence Unit.30
Reduced rainfall could also hinder hydroelectric power output, which accounts for more than
70% of Ecuador’s electricity generation; the Noboa administration initiated electricity rationing
measures for some commercial consumers in late September 2026.31

A priority for the Noboa administration is to improve Ecuador’s fiscal situation in line with a
$5 billion IMF financing agreement originally approved in May 2025 (IMF officials expanded the
agreement by $1 billion in July 2025). In April 2026, the IMF Executive Board approved a
disbursement of about $400 million, bringing total disbursements to date under the agreement to
about $3.7 billion.32 President Noboa also has sought to boost foreign investment in Ecuador,
including from the People’s Republic of China (PRC, or China), which reportedly has served as a
key source of financing and investment since roughly 2009.33 A free trade agreement between the

U.S.-Ecuador Relations and Issues for Congress

During the second Trump Administration, U.S.-Ecuador cooperation on several issues of U.S.
concern has increased. The two countries have expanded bilateral security cooperation and
initiated combined military operations to combat TCO-linked illicit activity under the Shield of
the Americas and Americas Counter Cartel Coalition (A3C)—two interrelated U.S.-led regional
initiatives composed largely of ideologically aligned Western Hemisphere governments.35 During
a September 2026 visit to Ecuador, U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio stated that Ecuador was
“the most involved and aggressive partner” in the Shield of the Americas and A3C.36 During his
visit, Secretary Rubio also announced the launch of a Millennium Challenge Corporation
compact with Ecuador, which he stated would include a $12 million grant to the Ecuadorian
government to benefit the country’s electricity sector and economy.37 In 2025 and 2026, the two
countries signed nonbinding bilateral agreements on migration, critical minerals, and trade
(discussed below).

Congress, at times, has sought to shape the executive branch’s approach to U.S.-Ecuador
relations. In 2022, Congress enacted the United States-Ecuador Partnership Act of 2022 as Title
LV, Subtitle B, of the James M. Inhofe National Defense Authorization Act for FY2023 (P.L. 117-
263). Authorized until December 2027, the act includes measures aimed at strengthening the
bilateral partnership between the United States and Ecuador in support of democratic institutions
and rule of law, sustainable and inclusive economic growth, and conservation.

participating in security operations would “enjoy immunity” in accordance with international
agreements Ecuador has signed.39 In March 2026, the FBI opened a permanent office in the

Migration Cooperation
The Noboa administration has cooperated with the United States on migration issues. In July
2025, the two governments entered into an agreement allowing the transfer of some third-country
nationals from the United States to Ecuador.77 Although the agreement text does not specify
requirements for such transfers, Ecuadorian officials reportedly have stated that Ecuador has
agreed to receive a maximum of 300 adult Spanish-speaking asylum seekers with no criminal
records from the United States per year to pursue their protection claims in Ecuador.78 Between
January and July 2026, Ecuador reportedly received 121 migrants of various nationalities (with
Venezuelan, Cuban, and Honduran nationals accounting for the majority) from the United States
pursuant to the agreement.79
Some NGOs have argued that Ecuador is not equipped to receive asylum seekers, in part due to
the country’s security challenges, which have contributed to emigration from Ecuador.80
According to UN data, an increasing number of Ecuadorians have sought asylum abroad since
2020, including over 122,000 in 2025 (with 93% of claims made in the United States).81 Violence
also has driven increased internal displacement. One NGO recorded 132,000 internal
displacements due to conflict and violence in Ecuador in 2025, a 31% increase from 2024 and the
third-highest level recorded among countries in the Americas, after Haiti and Colombia.82

Legislative Action and Issues for Congress
Congress may conduct oversight over third-country migrant transfers from the United States to
Ecuador as it assesses whether to support, modify, or reject such transfers through its legislative
prerogatives. During the 119th Congress, some Members have sought more information about
human rights conditions in Ecuador and measures to deter potential human rights abuses,
including within the context of the country’s third-country migrant transfer agreement with the
United States. S.Res. 879, introduced in September 2026, would require the State Department to
provide a report within 30 days of adoption on human rights practices in Ecuador, including the
treatment of third-country migrants transferred from the United States, pursuant to Section 502B
of the Foreign Assistance Act of 1961, as amended (22 U.S.C. §2304). The resolution would
prohibit the delivery of security assistance to Ecuador unless subsequently authorized by law until
the report is provided.83 Congress also could consider whether to authorize, restrict, or require
reporting on the use of U.S. foreign assistance to implement the third-country migrant transfer
agreements with Ecuador and other countries in FY2027 NSRP appropriations legislation.
