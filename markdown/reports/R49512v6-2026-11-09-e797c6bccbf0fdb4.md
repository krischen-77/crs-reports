There are many federal civil service leadership and support positions in the legislative and            Senior Research Librarian
executive branches of the federal government that may be subject to noncareer (political)
appointments. The nature of these positions varies widely and includes positions such as agency
heads and their immediate subordinates, policy executives and advisors, and aides who report to
these officials. Since 1952, successive volumes of United States Government Policy and
Supporting Positions have identified the positions in the federal government to which political appointments can be made.
The book is often referred to as the “Plum Book” due to both the purple cover of the physical book and the list of positions
found within its pages. In 2022, Congress passed legislation requiring the information in the Plum Book to be published in an
online database.

Congress may be interested in identifying certain information about these positions. Such information could include, for each
position, its title, type of appointment, name of individual(s) currently holding the role, location, salary, tenure (if applicable),
and expiration date of the term (if applicable).

This report provides a brief history and overview of the Plum Book and Plum Reporting website, an explanation of the
various political appointment types listed on the website, and examples of regional positions within federal agencies.

United States Government Policy and Supporting Positions (The
“Plum Book”)

After winning the presidential election in 1952, President Eisenhower requested a list of federal
government positions to which he could make noncareer appointments. As a result, the United
States Government Policy and Supporting Positions book1 was created. The book is often referred
to as the “Plum Book” due to both the purple cover of the physical book and the list of positions
found within its pages. Since 1960, the Senate Committee on Homeland Security and
Governmental Affairs and the House Committee on Government Reform (and the committees’
predecessors) alternately published new editions of the Plum Book every four years.

The major categories of positions listed in the Plum Book include the following:
    •   Executive Schedule and salary-equivalent positions paid at the rates established
        for levels I through V of the Executive Schedule
    •   Senior Executive Service “General” positions
    •   Senior Foreign Service positions
    •   Schedule C positions excepted from the competitive service by the President or
       by the director of the Office of Personnel Management (OPM) because of the
        confidential or policy-determining nature of the position duties
    •   Other positions at the GS-14 level and above excepted from the competitive civil
        service by law because of the confidential or policy-determining nature of the
        position duties2

The Plum Reporting Website

In 2022, Congress passed the Periodically Listing Updates to Management Act (or PLUM Act) as
a title of the James M. Inhofe National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2023.3 The
PLUM Act requires the OPM director to establish and maintain an online database4—entitled the
“Plum Reporting website” 5—of content previously published in the Plum Book.6

to which political appointments can be made. The database is organized by appointing agency and
generally includes, for each position, position title, name of incumbent, geographic location of the

A Brief Overview of Select Political Appointment Types

The Plum Reporting website includes positions that can be filled through political (or noncareer)
appointment. It does not include positions in the civil service that are filled through a competitive
hiring process.24

A brief description of the position types listed in the database is provided below.
    •   Presidentially appointed, Senate-confirmed (PAS). These positions are often
        referred to as “advice and consent positions” or “PAS positions.” PAS positions
        are filled through nomination of an individual by the President. The President
       submits the nomination to the Senate for its consideration.25 When the Senate
       confirms a nomination, the President may formally appoint the individual to the
        position. Under certain circumstances, the President can exercise constitutional
        recess appointment authority to appoint an individual to an office while the
       Senate is in recess.26 Most PAS appointees serve at the pleasure of the
         President.27

      Most SES positions are filled through career appointments.37 And, not all career
      SES positions are listed on the Plum Reporting website. For example, the website
       does not include SES positions that are “career reserved.”38 Approximately half
        of federal SES positions fall into this category. Career-reserved positions may be
         filled only through career appointments and therefore do not meet the criteria for
        inclusion in the Plum Book or Plum Reporting website. OPM publishes an
       annual list of “career reserved” SES positions in the Federal Register.39
    •  Noncareer SES appointment. Noncareer SES appointees are not hired through a
        competitive process. However, they must meet the position qualifications,40 and
     OPM must generally approve an agency’s use of noncareer hiring authority. A
       noncareer appointee serves at the pleasure of the appointing authority (usually
        the agency head) and may be removed at any time.41 Up to 10% of SES positions
        across the executive branch may be filled by noncareer appointees at any given
         time.42 The proportion of noncareer appointees may vary by agency, however, so
       noncareer appointments at some agencies may exceed 10%, while such
       appointments at other agencies may be fewer than 10%. Generally, no agency
      may exceed a 25% limit.43
    •  Limited term SES appointment (TA). Limited term appointees typically serve
        in short-term project-related or presidential transition roles. Their service may not
       exceed a period of three years.44 Such an appointee serves at the pleasure of the
        appointing authority (usually the agency head) and may be removed at any time.
      By law, limited appointments may not exceed 5% of all SES positions
       government-wide.45
    •  Limited emergency SES appointment (EA). Limited emergency appointees
        typically serve in short-term roles to meet urgent and unanticipated needs. Their
        service may not exceed a period of 18 months.46 Such an appointee serves at the
        pleasure of the appointing authority (usually the agency head) and may be
       removed at any time.
