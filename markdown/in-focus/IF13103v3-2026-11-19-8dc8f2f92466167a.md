Artificial Intelligence in Capital Markets: Policy Issues

Updated November 19, 2026
(IF13103)

[Artificial intelligence (AI)](https://www.crs.gov/Reports/R48909) has the potential to transform [operations and regulation of capital markets](https://www.crs.gov/Reports/R48521). This In Focus lays out some background and policy implications relevant to congressional oversight and legislative considerations for AI applications in capital markets. For discussions on AI-related investments, see CRS In Focus IF13301, [*AI Investments and Potential Government Stakes in Private AI Firms*](http://www.crs.gov/Reports/IF13301), by Eva Su.

AI Use in Capital Markets

The term *a**rtificial* *i**ntelligence (AI)* was defined in the National Artificial Intelligence Initiative Act of 2020 ([P.L. 116-283](http://www.congress.gov/law/116/pub/283), Division E) as "a machine-based system that can … make predictions, recommendations or decisions influencing real or virtual environments." The scope and speed of [AI adoption](https://www.fsb.org/uploads/P14112024.pdf) in the financial sector are dependent on both supply-side factors (e.g., technology enablers, data, and business model) and demand-side factors (e.g., revenue or productivity improvements and competitive pressure from peers that are implementing AI tools to obtain market share). Both capital markets industry participants and the U.S. capital markets regulator, [the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)](https://www.crs.gov/Reports/IF11714), may find uses for AI, as discussed below.

Capital Markets Use

A large language model ([LLM](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-definitions/what-is-a-llm)) is a subset of [generative AI](https://www.crs.gov/Reports/IF12426) that is capable of generating responses to prompts in natural language format once the model has been trained on [a large amount of text data](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/dataset-size-trend). An LLM can have applications in capital markets, such as answering questions and generating computer code. The Financial Industry Regulatory Authority, a self-regulatory organization for broker-dealers under the oversight of the SEC, has suggested that [AI applications](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/key-topics/fintech/report/artificial-intelligence-in-the-securities-industry/ai-apps-in-the-industry), such as LLM-based chatbots, may enhance communications between brokers and their customers; assist with account, portfolio, and trading management; and assist with compliance, risk management, and administrative tasks.

Common AI usage in [capital markets](https://www.crs.gov/Reports/IF11062) includes (1) investment management and execution, such as [investment research](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jofi.13298), portfolio management, and trading; (2) client support, such as [robo-adviser](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/robo-adviser) service, chatbots, and other forms of client engagement and underwriting; (3) regulatory compliance, such as [anti-money-laundering and counterterrorist financing](https://www.crs.gov/Reports/IF11064) reporting and other compliance processes; and (4) back-office functions, such as internal productivity support and risk management. [A survey](https://www.iosco.org/library/pubdocs/pdf/IOSCOPD788.pdf#page=18) by an international regulatory body found that the most frequently cited near-term uses include communication with clients, [algorithmic trading](https://www.sec.gov/files/marketstructure/research/algo_trading_report_2020.pdf) (trading based on preprogrammed instructions), and robo-advising.

Regulatory Use

In April 2025, the Office of Management and Budget (OMB) issued [Memorandum M-25-21](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/M-25-21-Accelerating-Federal-Use-of-AI-through-Innovation-Governance-and-Public-Trust.pdf), providing guidance to agencies (including the SEC) on accelerating AI use and requiring each agency to develop an AI strategy, share certain AI assets, and enable "an AI-ready federal workforce." In September 2025, the SEC issued its [OMB Memorandum M-25-21 Compliance Plan](https://www.sec.gov/files/2025-sec-ai-compliance-plan.pdf). The plan stated that the SEC's AI program "aims to foster innovation and efficiency, while maintaining appropriate risk-based governance, and promoting public trust." The SEC reported 60 internal [AI use cases](https://www.sec.gov/files/2025-sec-individual-ai-use-cases.csv) in 2025. Examples include comparing the SEC's online filing system access request forms, improving efficiency of comment letter ingestion, tracing digital asset transactions, and using natural language to query crypto market data.

Selected Policy Issues

While AI offers potential benefits associated with the applications discussed above, its use in capital markets also raises policy concerns. Below are examples of issues relating to AI use in capital markets that Congress may consider.

**Auditable and explainable capabilities.** Advanced AI financial models can produce sophisticated analysis that may not have outputs [explainable to a human](https://www.bis.org/fsi/fsipapers24.htm). This has led to concerns about human capability to review and flag potential mistakes and biases embedded in AI analysis. Some financial regulatory authorities have developed AI tools (e.g., [Project Noor](https://www.bis.org/about/bisih/topics/suptech_regtech/noor.htm)) to introduce more auditability into high-risk financial AI models.

**Accountability.** The issue of accountability centers around the question of who bears responsibility when AI systems fail or cause harm. The first known case of an investor suing an AI developer over autonomous trading [reportedly](https://www.bloomberglaw.com/bloomberglawnews/banking-law/XA51N7GO000000) occurred in 2019. In that instance, the investor expected the AI model to outperform the market and generate substantial returns. Instead, it incurred millions in losses, prompting the investor to seek remedy from the developer.

**AI-related information transparency and disclosure.** "[AI washing](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/speeches-statements/sec-chair-gary-gensler-ai-washing)"—that is, false and misleading overstatements about AI use—could lead to failures to comply with [SEC disclosure requirements](https://www.crs.gov/Reports/IF11256). Specifically, certain exaggerated claims that overstate AI usage or AI-related productivity gains may distort the assessments of the investment opportunities and lead to investor harm. The SEC initiated multiple enforcement actions against certain [securities offerings](https://www.sec.gov/enforcement-litigation/litigation-releases/lr-26157) and [investment advisory services](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2024-36) that appeared to have misled investors regarding AI use.

**Concentration and third-party dependency.** The substantial costs and specialized expertise required to develop advanced AI models have resulted in a market dominated by [a relatively small number of developers](https://crs.gov/Reports/IF12968) and data aggregators, creating concentration risks. This concentration could lead to operational vulnerabilities as disruptions at a few providers could have widespread consequences. Even when financial firms design their own models or rely on in-house data, these tools are [typically hosted](https://www.iosco.org/library/pubdocs/pdf/IOSCOPD788.pdf#page=50) by third-party cloud providers. Such [third-party risks](https://mitsloan.mit.edu/ideas-made-to-matter/third-party-ai-tools-pose-increasing-risks-organizations) expose participants to vulnerabilities regarding information access, model control, governance, and cybersecurity.

**Market correlation.** A common reliance on similar AI models and training data within capital markets may amplify financial fragility. [Some observers](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/financial-stability-implications-of-generative-ai-taming-the-animal-spirits.htm) argue that herding effects—where individual investors make similar decisions based on signals from the same underlying models or data providers—could intensify the interconnectedness of the global financial system, thereby increasing the risk of financial instability.

**Collusion.** [One academic paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34054) indicates that AI systems could collude to fix prices and sideline human traders, potentially undermining market competition and market efficiency. One of its authors [explained](https://www.bloomberglaw.com/product/blaw/bloomberglawnews/bloomberg-law-news/BNA%2000000198-5b19-de2d-a7bb-7f1dcb6a0000) during an interview that even fairly simple AI algorithms could collude without being prompted, and they could have widespread effects. [Others](https://www.bloomberglaw.com/product/blaw/bloomberglawnews/bloomberg-law-news/BNA%2000000198-7992-de2d-a7bb-7d9fc57d0001) challenged the paper, arguing that AI's effects on market efficiency are unclear.

**Model bias.** While AI could overcome certain human biases in investment decisionmaking, it could also introduce and amplify [AI bias](https://www.ibm.com/think/topics/ai-bias) derived from human programming instructions or training data deficiencies. Such bias could lead to AI systems favoring certain investors over others (e.g., providing more favorable terms or easier access to funding for certain investors based on [race, ethnicity](https://hai.stanford.edu/news/covert-racism-ai-how-language-models-are-reinforcing-outdated-stereotypes), or other characteristics) and potentially amplifying inequalities.

**Data.** AI models are data driven. Data availability, reliability, infrastructure, security, and privacy are all sources of policy concerns. If an AI system is trained on limited, biased, and nonrepresentative data, it could result in overgeneralization and misinterpretation in capital markets applications.

**AI-enabled fraud, manipulation, and cyberattacks.** AI could lower the entry barriers for bad actors to distort markets and enable more sophisticated and automated ways to generate fraud and manipulate markets. Hackers are [reportedly](https://www.economist.com/business/2025/08/19/how-ai-powered-hackers-are-stealing-billions) using AI both to distribute malware and deepfake emails targeting financial victims and to develop new types of malicious tools designed to reach and exploit a wider set of targets.

**Costs.** AI adoption involves significant investments in technology platforms, expenses related to system transitions and business model adjustments, and ongoing operating costs, such as licensing or service fees. For certain large-scale capital markets operations, there is often a lag between initial AI investments and the realization of revenue or productivity gains. As a result, these market participants may face financial pressures when AI spending is not immediately offset by the system's benefits. Aside from financial impact, some stakeholders are concerned about AI's [environmental costs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.15734) and the potential costs associated with the transition of the workforce that is [displaced by AI](https://privatebank.jpmorgan.com/nam/en/insights/markets-and-investing/ideas-and-insights/jobs-in-the-ai-revolution-disruption-today-growth-tomorrow).

SEC Actions

The SEC has not issued new regulations specifically targeting the use of AI. In recognition of AI's transformative potential, the SEC launched an [AI task force](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2025-103-sec-creates-task-force-tap-artificial-intelligence-enhanced-innovation-efficiency-across-agency) in August 2025 to enhance innovation in its operations and regulatory oversight. In addition, the SEC has engaged with stakeholders to discuss broader AI issues in capital markets. For example, the SEC hosted [AI roundtables](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/meetings-events/sec-roundtable-artificial-intelligence-financial-industry) covering AI-related benefits, costs, and applications; fraud and cybersecurity; governance and risk management; and [machine-understandable data](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/meetings-events/machine-understandable-data-age-super-intelligence-si).

In a 2023 [proposed rulemaking](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2023/08/09/2023-16377/conflicts-of-interest-associated-with-the-use-of-predictive-data-analytics-by-broker-dealers-and), the SEC discussed AI use in capital markets as it sought to address certain conflicts of interest associated with broker-dealers' or investment advisors' use of predictive data analytics technologies. The SEC notice was [withdrawn](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/final/2025/33-11377.pdf) in June 2025, along with other SEC-proposed rules introduced during the previous Administration. As of October 2026, the SEC had received [petitions](https://www.sec.gov/rules-regulations/2026/02/4-882) for rulemaking to mandate AI governance and risk management disclosure in public filings, but it has not indicated whether it would address AI in future rulemaking. The SEC's [FY2027 examination priorities](https://www.sec.gov/files/2027-exam-priorities.pdf) continue to emphasize AI disclosures, AI-related risks, and cybersecurity risks, among other issues, scrutinizing AI-related conduct under existing securities regulatory frameworks.

Considerations for Congress

[Some financial authorities](https://home.treasury.gov/system/files/136/Artificial-Intelligence-in-Financial-Services.pdf) and other [stakeholders](https://www.iosco.org/library/pubdocs/pdf/IOSCOPD788.pdf) have released [reports](https://www.imf.org/-/media/Files/Publications/GFSR/2024/October/English/ch3.ashx) addressing AI's capital markets use cases and policy implications. Examples of policy recommendations include to (1) evaluate the adequacy of current securities regulation in addressing AI-related vulnerabilities; (2) enhance regulatory capabilities by incorporating AI tools into regulatory functions; (3) enhance data monitoring and data collection capabilities; and (4) adopt coordinated approaches to address critical system-wide risks, such as AI third-party provider risks and cyberattack protocols.

In addition, emerging trends, such as the use of [agentic AI](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2026/02/the-agentic-ai-landscape-and-its-conceptual-foundations_a9d4b451/396cf758-en.pdf) at [SEC-regulated brokerage platforms](https://robinhood.com/newsroom/robinhood-is-now-open-to-agents/), have attracted congressional attention. Some Members have [raised concerns](https://foster.house.gov/sites/evo-subsites/foster-evo.house.gov/files/evo-media-document/foster_sherman-request-for-information-re-agentic-trading-6.23.2026.pdf) about retail investor protection and whether existing securities regulations adequately address agentic trading or whether additional congressional action may be warranted.

Congress has considered legislative proposals aimed at establishing *AI innovation labs* at the SEC and other financial regulators (e.g., [H.R. 4801](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119/hr/4801), 119th Congress). These offices would allow AI test projects to operate with relief from certain regulations and without expectation of enforcement actions—a type of arrangement sometimes referred to as a "regulatory sandbox." Congress may consider whether and how to address participation in any proposed sandboxes to mitigate potential risks, including requesting participating entities to apply and gain approval through their primary regulators and demonstrate that the projects promote investor protection and do not pose systemic risk. The AI Act of 2024 ([H.R. 10262](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118/hr/10262), 118th Congress), among other things, would have required the SEC to provide a study on both the realized and potential benefits, risks, and challenges of AI for capital market participants as well as for the agency itself. The study was to incorporate public input through a request-for-information process and include both regulatory proposals and legislative recommendations.
