Summary

The federal government has a long history of investing in programs for feeding children, starting with federal aid for school lunch programs in the 1930s. Today, federal child nutrition programs support food served to children in schools and a variety of other settings. Administered by the U.S. Department of Agriculture's (USDA's) Food and Nutrition Administration (formerly Food and Nutrition Service [FNS]), the child nutrition programs include:

* National School Lunch Program (NSLP)
* School Breakfast Program (SBP)
* Child and Adult Care Food Program (CACFP)
* Summer Food Service Program (SFSP)
* Seamless Summer Option (SSO)
* Summer Electronic Benefits Transfer for Children (Summer EBT) Program
* Fresh Fruit and Vegetable Program (FFVP)
* Special Milk Program (SMP)

The child nutrition programs vary in terms of size and target population. The largest programs are NSLP and SBP (the *school meals programs*), which subsidize meals for nearly 30 million children in more than 90,000 elementary and secondary schools in a typical school year. CACFP supports food served to children in child care, day care, and after-school settings. SFSP and SSO provide funding for summer meals and snacks. Summer EBT provides grocery benefits for households with school-age children over the summer. FFVP sponsors fruit and vegetable snacks in elementary schools. SMP subsidizes milk in schools and institutions that do not participate in other child nutrition programs. In general, the largest subsidies are provided for free or reduced-price meals and snacks served to qualifying children. Other child nutrition activities include after-school meal and snack programs, farm-to-school initiatives, and school meal equipment grants.

Federal spending on child nutrition programs and activities totaled roughly $32.5 billion in FY2025, the majority of which was mandatory spending. Most child nutrition programs are considered *appropriated entitlements*, meaning that their authorizing statutes establish a legal obligation to make payments, but that obligation is fulfilled through funding that is provided in annual appropriations acts. Most child nutrition funding is provided in the form of per-meal cash reimbursements that states distribute to schools and institutions, which is used toward the cost of operating the programs. A smaller amount of federal funding is provided in the form of federally purchased foods and cash for states' administrative expenses.

The child nutrition programs are primarily governed by two statutes: the Richard B. Russell National School Lunch Act and the Child Nutrition Act of 1966, as amended. These laws were most recently reauthorized by the Healthy, Hunger-Free Kids Act of 2010 (HHFKA; [P.L. 111-296](https://www.congress.gov/law/111/pub/296)), which made several changes to the child nutrition programs. For example, the act created the Community Eligibility Provision, an option for eligible schools to provide free meals to all students. It also required USDA to update nutrition standards in the school meals programs and CACFP within a certain time frame. While certain provisions of the HHFKA expired at the end of FY2015, program operations have continued with annual appropriations. More recently, temporary changes to the child nutrition programs were made in response to the COVID-19 pandemic. In addition, the FY2023 Consolidated Appropriations Act ([P.L. 117-328](https://www.congress.gov/law/117/pub/328)) authorized the national Summer EBT program and made changes to the summer meal programs, and the Whole Milk for Healthy Kids Act ([P.L. 119-69](http://www.congress.gov/law/119/pub/69)) added whole milk, 2%, and nondairy milk options to child nutrition programs.

Background

The child nutrition programs (summarized in [**Table 1**](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#_Ref101865115)) support meals and snacks served to children in schools, child care, summer programs, and other institutional settings in all 50 states, the District of Columbia, and the U.S. territories.[1](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn1 "Virtually all of the child nutrition programs operate in Puerto Rico, Guam, and the U.S. Virgin Islands . The term state in this report refers to these jurisdictions as well. In the case of Summer EBT, it also includes some tribal entities. The term does not include the Northern Mariana Islands and American Samoa, which receive block grants in lieu of child nutrition programs. For more information on child nutrition programs in the Northern Mariana I s lands and American Samoa, s...") They also support summer grocery benefits for households with school-age children. The programs are administered by the U.S. Department of Agriculture's (USDA's) Food and Nutrition Administration (FNA)—formerly known as the Food and Nutrition Service (FNS)[2](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn2 "Effective June 1, 2026, the Secretary of Agriculture, as part of a reorganization of USDA , renamed FNS to FNA. See FNA, \" USDA Announces Actions to Better Serve States, Nutrition Program Recipients, and the American Taxpayer ,\" press release, April 30, 2026, https://www.fna.usda.gov/newsroom/usda-0062.26 ; and FNA, \"Reorganization,\" https://www.fna.usda.gov/reorganization . This report generally uses the term FNS when referencing documents published by FNS at the time it was...")—which provides federal aid to state agencies (often state departments of education) for distribution to school districts and other participating institutions and, in the case of summer grocery benefits, for distribution to households.[3](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn3 "For a list of administering agencies by state, see FNA, \"Contact Map,\" https://www.fna.usda.gov/contacts . In past instances , the federal government ( via FN S regional offices ) has, for certain states, taken the place of state agencies (e.g., where a state has chosen not to operate a specific program or where there is a state prohibition on aiding private schools).") In general, federal funding largely supports food for children from low-income or otherwise qualifying households.

A related program is the Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children (WIC), which provides grocery benefits to pregnant and postpartum women, infants, and young children. WIC has typically been reauthorized with the child nutrition programs but is generally not considered a child nutrition program because it is funded under a separate account and is not discussed in this report.[4](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn4 "For more information on WIC, see CRS Report R44115, A Primer on WIC: The Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children .")

The federal child nutrition programs date back to the National School Lunch Act (NSLA) of 1946, which created the National School Lunch Program (NSLP).[5](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn5 "P.L. 79-396; Gordon W. Gunderson, \" National School Lunch Program: Background and Development , \" 1971, https://fna-bwbufwdzbabpezgc.z01.azurefd.us/sites/default/files/resource-files/NSLP-program-history.pdf . The 1946 law supported school lunch programs by giving formula grant funding to states based on factors such as per capita income rather than the present-day open-ended entitlements based largely on eligibility and participation rules.") This act formalized federal support for school lunches following early aid beginning in the 1930s. Other child nutrition programs were added in the decades to follow as policymakers expanded feeding programs beyond the school setting. The Child Nutrition Act of 1966 formalized the Special Milk Program (SMP) and created the School Breakfast Program (SBP) as a pilot program.[6](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn6 "P.L. 89-642.") Soon after, a program for child care and summer meals was piloted in 1968 and separated into the Child Care Food Program (now the Child and Adult Care Food Program, or CACFP) and the Summer Food Service Program (SFSP) in 1975.[7](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn7 "P.L. 90-302; P.L. 94-105 . Adult day care was added in 1987 (Older Americans Act Amendments of 1987; P.L. 100-175 ). Also see Institute of Medicine, Child and Adult Care Food Program: Aligning Dietary Guidance for A ll , 2011, p. 30; FNS , \" Summer Food Service Program History ,\" https://www.fns.usda.gov/sfsp/program-history .") More recently, the Fresh Fruit and Vegetable Program (FFVP) was piloted in 2002 and expanded to all states in 2008.[8](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn8 "P.L. 107-171 ; P.L. 110-246 .") Likewise, following a more-than-decade-long demonstration, the Summer Electronic Benefits Transfer for Children (Summer EBT) program became available as an option to all states starting in summer 2024. (See [**Appendix**](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#_Ref25076090) for a legislative history of child nutrition programs.)

Historically, the child nutrition programs have been aimed at both improving children's nutrition and supporting U.S. agriculture, with the dual mission "to safeguard the health and well-being of the Nation's children and to encourage the domestic consumption of nutritious agricultural commodities and other food."[9](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn9 "See declaration of purposes in the NSLA and the Child Nutrition Act of 1966.")

The child nutrition programs are currently authorized under the Richard B. Russell National School Lunch Act and the Child Nutrition Act of 1966.[10](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn10 "In 1999, P.L. 106-78 renamed the NSLA in Senator Richard B. Russell's honor.") Section 32 of the Act of August 24, 1935, also provides a portion of child nutrition funding. Congressional jurisdiction over the underlying three laws has typically been exercised by the Senate Agriculture, Nutrition, and Forestry Committee; the House Education and Workforce (or Labor) Committee; and, to a limited extent (relating to Section 32), the House Agriculture Committee.

Congress has periodically amended the child nutrition programs' authorizing laws and reauthorized expiring authorities. The child nutrition programs were most recently reauthorized by the Healthy, Hunger-Free Kids Act of 2010 (HHFKA; [P.L. 111-296](https://www.congress.gov/law/111/pub/296)). Some of the authorities created or extended in the HHFKA expired on September 30, 2015. However, program operations have continued with annual appropriations.[11](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn11 "Exceptions include a California program to provide SFSP snacks year-ro und , which was not extended. USDA's authority to conduct food safety audits and funding for a N ational Hunger Clearinghouse were not extended in FY2016 but were subsequently extended by appropriations acts in each of FY2017 - FY2022. For more information, see CRS In Focus IF10266, Child Nutrition Reauthorization (CNR): An Overview ; or CRS memo CD1304737 , Expiration of the Healthy, Hunger-free Kids Act of 2010...") During the 114th Congress, the committees of jurisdiction marked up child nutrition reauthorization bills, but these proposals were not enacted.[12](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn12 "For more information, se e CRS Report R44373, Tracking Child Nutrition Reauthorization in the 114th Congress: An Overview .") Likewise, during the 117th Congress, the House Education and Labor Committee reported a child nutrition reauthorization bill ([H.R. 8450](https://www.congress.gov/bill/117/hr/8450)) out of committee. The 117th Congress ended without further action on the bill. However, the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2023 ([P.L. 117-328](https://www.congress.gov/law/117/pub/328)), was enacted at the end of the 117th Congress, making changes to the summer meals programs and authorizing the national Summer EBT program (discussed further in this report).

This report starts with an overview of child nutrition programs' funding structure and then provides detail on each program, including a discussion of how they are administered, eligibility rules for institutions and participants, nutritional and other program requirements, and recent policy changes. Changes to child nutrition programs that occurred in response to the COVID-19 pandemic are summarized in the next section.

Child Nutrition Funding

Federal Funding

Most funding for child nutrition programs is considered mandatory spending. However, unlike some mandatory programs, child nutrition programs require appropriations of funding. This is because the programs' authorizing laws include benefit and eligibility criteria that create the requirement for a certain level of spending, but the statute does not provide the funding directly. Such programs are sometimes referred to as *appropriated entitlements* or *appropriated mandatories*.[19](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn19 "For further discussion of appropriated entitlements, see CRS Report RS20129, Entitlements and Appropriated Entitlements in the Federal Budget Process .") If the necessary funds are not appropriated and the authorized benefits are not made available, entitled recipients (e.g., states, institutions, and participants) may have legal recourse.[20](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn20 "U.S. Government Accountability Office (GAO), A Glossary of Terms Used in the Federal Budget Process , GAO-05-734SP , p. 13, http://www.gao.gov/products/GAO-05-734SP .")

The benefit and eligibility criteria that govern much of the appropriated mandatory spending for child nutrition programs are open-ended. Because there is no specified limit on the number of beneficiaries or the total amount of benefits that will be paid, spending fluctuates based on the number of meals and snacks served in the programs and statutorily set, annually adjusted per-meal reimbursement rates. Congress typically considers USDA's forecast for program needs in its appropriations decisions.

Summer EBT Benefits

Like most other child nutrition programs, Summer EBT is an appropriated entitlement. Administrative Funds

The NSLA allows USDA to retain up to 3.5% of annual child nutrition funding for its administrative expenses related to child nutrition programs and WIC.[38](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn38 "Section 6 of the NSLA (codified at 42 U.S.C. §1755).") In addition, the Child Nutrition Act of 1966 authorizes open-ended funding for USDA's administrative expenses in carrying out the programs and activities under that act (which include SBP, SMP, other child nutrition activities, and WIC).[39](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn39 "Section 14 of the Child Nutrition Act of 1966 (codified at 42 U.S.C. §1783).")

There is also specific funding authorized for state agencies' expenses related to the administration of child nutrition programs. According to statute, federal funding for states' administrative expenses must equal at least 1.5% of federal expenditures on NSLP, SBP, CACFP, and SMP in the second preceding fiscal year.[40](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn40 "Section 7 of the Child Nutrition Act of 1966 (codified at 42 U.S.C. § 1776).") The majority of these funds are allocated to states based on their share of spending on the four programs. Any remaining funds are allocated by the Secretary of Agriculture on a discretionary basis. Per program regulations, states receive additional amounts for CACFP, commodity distribution, and administrative reviews of schools/institutions.[41](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn41 "7 C.F.R. §235.4.") Once states receive administrative funds, they can apportion them among child nutrition programs and activities as they see fit.[42](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn42 "7 C.F.R. § 235.6 .")

In addition, states receive separate administrative payments through SFSP that equal at least 2.5% of their summer meal aid.[43](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn43 "Section 13(k)(1) of the NSLA (codified at 42 U.S.C. §1761(k)(1)); 7 C.F.R. § 225.5 .") (They must cover the other half through nonfederal sources.[44](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn44 "Section 13A(d) of the NSLA (codified at 42 U.S.C. §1762(d)). For further information on the administrative funding process, see FNA, \"Summer EBT Administrative Funding Process for FY 2024,\" October 18, 2023, https://www.fna.usda.gov/sebt/administrative-funding-process-fy24 .")) States may also retain a portion of FFVP aid for their administrative expenses.[45](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn45 "Section 19 of the NSLA (codified at 42 U.S.C. §1769(i)(6)(B)).")

At the local level, schools and institutions may use per-meal reimbursements to cover their administrative costs. Spending Trends

Federal spending on child nutrition programs has generally increased over time (see [**Figure 2**](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#_Ref243056646)). When adjusting for inflation, the growth appears much slower (approximately $1.5 billion higher spending in FY2025 compared to FY2019—a pre-pandemic year). There are several reasons for this increase, including annual inflation adjustments to per-meal reimbursement rates, participation changes, spending under pandemic response policies, authorization of the national Summer EBT program, and authorization of rural non-congregate feeding policies in the Summer Food Service Program.[55](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn55 "For more information, see CRS Report R48515, School Lunch and Breakfast Participation: A Snapshot of Recent Trends .")

A recent exception to this trend occurred in FY2020, at the start of the COVID-19 pandemic. Participation in programs declined as institutions closed, decreasing spending. By FY2021, an increase in summer meal program participation again increased spending. (Pandemic policies are discussed in the "Child Nutrition Program Operations During the COVID-19 Pandemic" text box.)

National School Lunch Program (NSLP) and School Breakfast Program (SBP)

NSLP and SBP (the *school* *meals programs*) provide federal support for meals served in more than 90,000 public and private elementary and secondary schools nationwide.[58](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn58 "A s of fall 202 4 , 94,159 schools participated in NSLP , and 91,229 schools participated in SBP (with substantial overlap), according to FNA, June 2026 Keydata Report . This includes private schools. In FY202 5 , 4,179 private schools participat ed in NSLP , and 2,906 private schools participat ed in SBP, according to CRS communication with FNA in September 2025 .") They also support meals served in a smaller number of residential child care institutions.[59](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn59 "In fall 202 4 , approximately 1, 6 00 residential child care institutions participated in NSLP and SBP ( CRS communication with FNA in September 2025 ). This report refers to \"schools,\" but for NSLP and SBP, it means both schools and residential child care institutions , which are defined as \" homes for the mentally, emotionally or physically impaired, and unmarried mothers and their infants; group homes; halfway houses; orphanages; temporary shelters for abused children and for ru...") Schools receive federal aid in the form of cash reimbursements for every meal they serve that meets federal nutritional requirements (limited to one breakfast and lunch per child daily). The largest subsidies are provided for free and reduced-price meals served to eligible students based on income eligibility and categorical eligibility rules (discussed below). Schools also receive a certain amount of commodity assistance per lunch served (discussed previously). Schools participating in NSLP have the option of providing after-school snacks through the program, and schools participating in NSLP or SBP have the option of providing summer meals and snacks through the Seamless Summer Option (discussed in the "[After-School Meals and Snacks](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#_Ref520463444)" and "[Seamless Summer Option](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#_Ref9524367)" sections).

Schools are not required by federal law to participate in NSLP or SBP. However, some states require schools to have school lunch and/or breakfast programs, and some require schools to operate such programs through NSLP and/or SBP. Schools that do not participate in the federal school meals programs may still operate locally funded meal programs.[60](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn60 "There is little research on schools that opt out of the federal school meals programs. A 1993 GAO analysis found that smaller and wealthier schools were more likely to drop out of NSLP. GAO found that common reasons for departure included financial considerations and compliance with federal nutrition standards. See GAO, Schools That Left the National School Lunch Program , GAO/RCED-94-36BR, December 1993, https://www.gao.gov/assets/80/78774.pdf .") Some states also provide state funding for the school meals programs, including nine states (as of the date of this report) that have authorized funding to provide free meals to all students.[61](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn61 "For a list of states that adopted state funding for universal free meals, see the state map available at FRAC, \"Healthy School Meals for All. \" The states that had adopted universal free school meal policies were California, Colorado, Maine, Massachusetts, Michigan, Minnesota, New Mexico, New York, and Vermont . School meal state policy trackers include FRAC , \"School Meals Legislation and Funding by State,\" and School Nutrition Association , \"Quarterly State Legislative Reports...") As of school year 2023-2024, approximately 60% of NSLP schools operated universal school meal programs using state or federal policy options.[62](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn62 "For more information, see CRS Report R48515, School Lunch and Breakfast Participation: A Snapshot of Recent Trends .")

The HHFKA ([P.L. 111-296](https://www.congress.gov/law/111/pub/296)) made several changes to the school meals programs. Among those changes was a requirement that USDA update the nutrition standards for school meals and create new nutritional requirements for foods sold in NSLP and SBP schools within a certain time frame. The law also created the Community Eligibility Provision, through which eligible schools can provide free meals to all students. These changes are discussed further within this section.

NSLP and SBP are two separate programs, and schools can choose to operate one and not the other (although most participating schools operate both).[63](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn63 "USDA estimated that 94% of schools operating NSLP also operated SBP in FY2020 (USDA , Office of Budget and Program Analysis , 2023 USDA Explanatory Notes — Food and Nutrition Service , p. 35-14, https://www.usda.gov/sites/default/files/documents/35-2023-FNS.pdf ).") The programs are discussed together in this report because they share many of the same requirements. Differences between the programs are noted where applicable. Student participation in SBP tends to be lower than in NSLP for several reasons, including the traditionally required early arrival by students in order to receive meals before school starts.

This section discusses topics specific to the school meals programs. Other food service topics relevant to child nutrition programs more broadly (e.g., the Farm to School program) are discussed in the "[Other Child Nutrition Activities](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#_Ref28853862)" section.

Eligibility and Reimbursement

The school meals programs do not exclusively serve low-income children. Any student in an NSLP or SBP participating school may purchase a school meal. However, children must meet program eligibility rules in order to receive free or reduced-price meals.

In most schools (excluding schools that participate in the Community Eligibility Provision or other special options), children are certified for free or reduced-price school meals through one of two pathways (shown in [**Figure 4**](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#_Ref398797551)):

* Household application (based on income eligibility or *categorical eligibility*, meaning that the child participates in another qualifying program or has a certain qualifying status)
* *D**irect certification*(automatic enrollment in free meals for categorically eligible children without additional means testing—with the exception of Medicaid, in which case income data are considered and children may be enrolled in free *or* reduced-price meals)

If children are certified for free meals, the school food authority receives the free meal reimbursement for those meals. If children are certified for reduced-price meals, the school food authority receives a slightly lower reimbursement. School food authorities also receive a much smaller paid-rate reimbursement for meals served to children who pay for "full price" meals. School food authorities must follow federal guidelines in setting prices for paid meals.[70](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn70 "The HHFKA set requirements around the price of paid meals, amending Section 12(p) of the NSLA ( codified at 42 U.S.C. § 1760(p)). However, appropriations laws from FY2018 forward waived these requirements for many schools. For more information, see FNA , \"Paid Lunch Equity: Guidance for SY 202 6 - 202 7 ,\" April 14, 202 6 , https://www.usda.gov/guidance-documents/child-nutrition/fns/paid-lunch-equity-guidance-school-year-2026-2027 ; and CRS Report R45486, Child Nutri...")

Certain schools follow different eligibility and reimbursement procedures because they participate in the Community Eligibility Provision or other special options (discussed below in the "[Special Options](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#_Ref23405531)" section).

Income Eligibility

Children are eligible for free or reduced-price meals if their households' incomes fall within the following ranges:

* **Free meals:** household income at or below 130% of the federal poverty guidelines[71](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn71 "For the purposes of school meal eligibility, household is defined as \" a group of related or nonrelated individuals, who are not residents of an institution or boarding house, but who are living as one economic unit\" (7 C.F.R. §245.2).")
* **Reduced-price meals** (charges of no more than 40 cents per lunch and 30 cents per breakfast): household income above 130% and less than or equal to 185% of the federal poverty guidelines[72](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn72 "Se ction 9(b)(1) of the NSLA (42 U.S.C. § 1758(b)(1) .")

These thresholds are based on the annual federal poverty guidelines established by the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services and are updated annually for inflation. FNA publishes the corresponding income limits by household size for free and reduced-price meals in the *Federal Register* on an annual basis.[73](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn73 "Links to Federal Register notices for recent school years are available at FNA , \"Child Nutrition Programs Income Eligibility Guidelines,\" https://www.fna.usda.gov/schoolmeals/income-eligibility-guidelines .") [**Table 3**](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#_Ref400030588) provides an example of the income limits for free and reduced-price meals for a household of four.

To become income eligible for school meals, a parent or guardian must complete a paper or online application that includes the income of each household member, the household size, and other information.[74](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn74 "There is a requirement that the adult household member filling out the application provide the last four digits of his or her Social Security number (Section 9(d)(1) of the NSLA) or, according to program regulations, indicate that the individual does not have one (7 C.F.R. § 245.6 (a)(6)) . The law does not allow for citizenship eligibility restrictions . Section 742(a) of P.L. 104-193 states that individuals who are eligible for free public education benefits under state and local...") Household income is defined as total gross income (before taxes or deductions), including earnings and wages, certain public assistance benefits (such as unemployment compensation, social security benefits, and child support payments), and retirement and pension income.[75](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn75 "7 C.F.R. §245.6(a) (5)(ii). Also see FNA , \"Child Nutrition Programs: Income Eligibility Guidelines,\" 91 Federal Register 17932, April 9, 2026; FNA , Eligibility Manual for School Meals ; and FNA , \" Applying for Free and Reduced Price School Meals ,\" https://www.fna.usda.gov/schoolmeals/model-application .") Households are asked to provide current weekly, biweekly (every two weeks), twice monthly, or monthly income, which school district officials compare to the federal poverty guidelines to determine eligibility for free meals, reduced-price meals, or neither.[76](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn76 "Ibid. Each household is asked to report this income for the most recent period prior to the application, unless it does not reflect the household's typical income, in which case it can provide the amount of income it normally receive s in a month.") Categorical Eligibility

Children can also become eligible for free school meals if they fall into a certain category (called *categorical eligibility*). Per statute, children are automatically eligible for free lunches and breakfasts (without consideration of household income) if they:

are in households receiving benefits through the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP); Food Distribution Program on Indian Reservations (FDPIR, a program that operates in lieu of SNAP on some Indian reservations); or Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF);

are enrolled in Head Start; or

qualify as foster, migrant, runaway, or homeless children.[77](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn77 "See Section 9(b)(12)(A) of the NSLA (codified at 42 U.S.C. §1758(b)(12)(A)) for the more specific definitions of these categories. SNAP, FDPIR, and TANF have income limits , but the other qualifications as defined in the statute are not limited by income.")

Categorical eligibility for free meals may be determined via a case number on the household's application or through direct certification (discussed in the next section). The vast majority of categorically eligible children are certified for free meals through direct certification.[78](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn78 "According to CRS calculations using USDA administrative data for FY202 5 , 9 7 % of categorically eligible students were directly certified for free school meals, compared to 3 % certified by household application.")

Categorical eligibility for free school meals with SNAP and TANF began in the 1980s (then, the Food Stamp and Aid to Families with Dependent Children programs, respectively).[79](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn79 "FNS , Direct Certification in the National School Lunch Program: State Implementation Progress : Report to Congress , December 2008, p. 3.") Categorical eligibility enabled schools to make use of other programs' more in-depth certification processes and reduced the number of applications that families had to fill out.[80](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn80 "See, for example, GAO , School-Meals Programs: USDA Has Enhanced Controls, but Additional Verification Could Help Ens ure Legitimate Program Access , GAO-14-262, May 2014, pp. 16-19, http://www.gao.gov/products/GAO-14-262 .") Other programs and categories were added over time.

Direct Certification

Direct certification is a process through which a state agency or school district automatically certifies a child for free meals based on documentation of the child's status in a program or category without the need for a household application.[81](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn81 "Direct certification authority is in Section 9(b)(4)-(5) of the NSLA (codified at 42 U.S.C. §1758(b)(4)-(5)) . The term d irect certification is defined in NSLP/SBP program regulations at 7 C.F.R. §245.2.") States are required to conduct direct certification with SNAP and have the option of conducting direct certification with the other programs and categories that convey categorical eligibility.

For SNAP and other federal programs, the direct certification process typically involves state agencies (e.g., state SNAP and state educational agencies) cross-checking program rolls.[82](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn82 "FNS , Direct Certification in the National School Lunch Program: State Implementation Progress, School Year 2014–2015: Report to Congress , December 2016, p. xiii.") A list of matched children is sent to the school district, which certifies children for free meals without the need for household applications.[83](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn83 "P arents and guardians are notified of the ir child ren 's enrollment in free meals and are allowed to opt out.") For foster, homeless, migrant, and runaway children, direct certification typically involves school district communication with local or state officials who can provide documentation of the children's status in these categories.[84](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn84 "FNA , Eligibility Manual for School Meals .")

The 2004 child nutrition reauthorization act ([P.L. 108-265](https://www.congress.gov/law/108/pub/265)) required states to conduct direct certification with SNAP, with nationwide implementation taking effect in school year 2008-2009. As of school year 2018-2019 (the most recent data available), USDA reported that 98% of children in SNAP households were directly certified for free school meals.[85](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn85 "FNS , Direct Certification in the National School Lunch Program State Implementation Progress Report to Congress School Year 2017-2018 and School Year 2018-2019 , June 2021, https://www.fns.usda.gov/cn/direct-certification-national-school-lunch-program-state-implementation-progress-report .")

The HHFKA made further policy changes to expand direct certification. One of those changes was a demonstration project to test direct certification with Medicaid (see the text box below). The law also funded performance incentive grants for high-performing states and authorized corrective action plans for low-performing states in direct certification activities.[86](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn86 "See CRS Report R41354, Child Nutrition and WIC Reauthorization: P.L. 111-296 , for further discussion of these and related policies.")

Verification of Eligibility

Each fall, each school district is required to verify a sample of approved household applications on file, with a focus on applications close to the eligibility threshold ("error-prone" applications).[91](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn91 "In general, a local educational agenc y must review the smalle r of 3,000 of all applications or 3% of error -prone applications. If the agency has a nonresponse rate below 20% or has more than 20,000 children approved by application for free/reduced-price meals and a recently improved response rate, it may use alternative sampling approaches. See Section 9(b)(3)(D) of the NSLA or program regulations at 7 C.F.R. § 245.6a.") School districts may also conduct verification of questionable applications. Verification is not required for children who are directly certified for free or reduced-price meals. (Districts participating in Provisions 1, 2, and 3—see the "[Provisions 1, 2, and 3](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#_Ref202782187)" section—must meet verification requirements for the years in which they administer household applications.)

Many districts employ *direct verification* (matching data from other low-income programs) to conduct their verification activities, but if data cannot be verified in this way, schools must contact households to verify the information provided on the applications. A child's eligibility status may stay the same or change (e.g., from free meals to reduced-price meals or loss of eligibility) as a result of verification of household income or if the household does not respond to verification outreach (in which case eligibility would be lost, though that decision can be appealed).

Reimbursement

School food authorities must keep track of the daily number of meals they serve in each category (free, reduced-price, and paid) that meet federal nutrition requirements. School food authorities then submit claims for reimbursement to their state agencies, which submit the claims to FNA. Approved reimbursements are distributed to school food authorities by the state agencies, usually on a monthly basis. Per statute, reimbursement rates are adjusted for inflation annually.[92](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn92 "See Section 4 and Section 11 of the NSLA for the lunch reimbursement rates and Section 4 of the Child Nutrition Act of 1966 for breakfast reimbursement rates.") [**Table 4**](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#_Ref324679596) shows NSLP and SBP reimbursement rates for school year 2026-2027. (School food authorities also receive a per-lunch commodity reimbursement, discussed previously under "[Commodity Assistance](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#_Ref150774057)".)

The law provides a higher reimbursement rate for meals meeting certain criteria. For example, school food authorities that are compliant with the updated federal nutrition standards for school meals (nearly all school food authorities as of school year 2023-2024[93](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn93 "FNA , \" SFA Certification of Compliance with Meal Requirements Data by State ,\" July 30, 2024, https://www.fns.usda.gov/cn/coc-meal-requirements-state-data .")) receive an additional 9 cents per lunch.[94](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn94 "T he HHFKA provided an additional 6 cents per-lunch reimbursement (adjusted annually for inflation) to schools meeting the updated nutritional guidelines requirements . See 91 Federal Register 43597 ( which separately lists rates for Alaska, Guam, Hawaii, Puerto Rico, and the U.S. Virgin Islands).") School food authorities also receive an additional 2 cents per lunch if they serve 60% or more of their lunches at a free or reduced price.[95](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn95 "Section 4(b)(2) of the NSLA (codified at 42 U.S.C. §1753(b)(2)).") For breakfasts, school food authorities receive higher reimbursements for free and reduced-price meals (an additional 47 cents per breakfast) if they serve 40% or more lunches at a free or reduced price (referred to as *severe need* schools).[96](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn96 "Section 4(d) of the Child Nutrition Act of 1966 (codified at 42 U.S.C. §1773(d)).")

Once school food authorities receive the cash reimbursements, they can use the funds to support almost any aspect of the school food service operation. However, federal cash reimbursements must go into a nonprofit school food service account that is subject to federal regulations.[97](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn97 "7 C.F.R. § 210.14 .") Payments for non-program foods (e.g., vending machine sales) must also accrue to the nonprofit school food service account.[98](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn98 "Section 12(p) of the NSLA ( codified at 42 U.S.C. §1760(q )).")

FNA periodically studies the costs of producing a reimbursable meal. In April 2019, FNA released a *School Nutrition and Meal Cost Study*, which found that the average reportedcost of producing a reimbursable lunch was $3.81 in school year 2014-2015 (reported costs were defined as those charged to the school food service account).[99](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn99 "USDA, School Nutrition and Meal Cost Study .") This exceeded the average federal cash reimbursement ($3.32) for lunches in school year 2014-2015. When unreported costs were included (costs outside of the food service account—for example, labor costs associated with processing applications), the cost of producing the average reimbursable lunch was $6.02. As noted previously, children's payments and state and local funds may also cover meal costs.

Special Options

Community Eligibility Provision (CEP)

The HHFKA authorized CEP, which allows eligible schools, groups of schools, and school districts to offer free meals to all enrolled students.[100](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn100 "For further detail on CEP, see CRS Report R46371, Serving Free School Meals through the Community Eligibility Provision (CEP): Background and Participation .") To participate in CEP, the school(s) must have an identified student percentage (ISP) of at least 25%. (USDA lowered the eligibility threshold from 40% to 25% as of school year 2023-2024.[101](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn101 "FNS, \"Child Nutrition Programs: Community Eligibility Provision-Increasing Options for Schools,\" 88 Federal Register 65778, September 26, 2023.")) The ISP is the percentage of students in the school(s) who are certified for free meals without household applications (i.e., who are directly certified for free meals through SNAP or another program/category).[102](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn102 "A school's number of identified students is essentially the same as its number of directly certified students, except that the number of identified students does not include students who are directly certified for reduced-price meals through the Medicaid demonstration. For the definition of identified students in regulations, see 7 C.F.R. §245.9(f)(1)( ii) .") In addition, the school(s) must operate both NSLP and SBP in order to participate in CEP, and they must opt in to CEP.

Based on the statutory parameters, USDA piloted CEP in various states over three school years and expanded the option nationwide in school year 2014-2015. Eligible schools, groups of schools, and entire school districts may participate. If participation is as a group, the ISP is calculated on a group basis. Local educational agencies have until June 30 of each year to notify USDA of the schools in their jurisdictions that will participate in CEP.[103](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn103 "7 C . F . R . § 245.9(f)(4) ; FNS , \"National School Lunch Program and School Breakfast Program: Eliminating Applications Through Community Eligibility as Required by the Healthy, Hunger-Free Kids Act of 2010,\" 81 Federal Register 50194, July 29, 2016.") According to a database maintained by the Food Research and Action Center (a research and advocacy organization), more than 55,300 schools participated in CEP in school year 2025-2026, up from approximately 40,200 schools in school year 2022-2023.[104](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn104 "FRAC, \" Community Eligibility: The Key to Hunger-Free Schools, School Year 202 5 - 202 6 ,\" July 2026 , https://frac.org/community-eligibility .") [**Figure 5**](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#_Ref151713408) displays the estimated proportion of CEP schools out of all NSLP schools since school year 2014-2015 (the first year of nationwide implementation) using USDA administrative data.

Though CEP schools serve free meals to all students, they are not reimbursed at the free rate for every meal served. Instead, the ISP is multiplied by a factor of 1.6 to estimate the proportion of students who would be eligible for free or reduced-price meals had they been certified via application.[105](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn105 "Statute allows USDA to set the reimbursement multiplier between 1.3 and 1.6 . USDA has set the multiplier at 1.6. FNS, \" National School Lunch Program and School Breakfast Program: Eliminating Applications Through Community Eligibility as Required by the Healthy, Hunger-Free Kids Act of 2010 ,\" 81 Federal Register 50194, July 29, 2016, p. 50201 .") The result is the percentage of meals served that will be reimbursed at the free-meal rate, with the remainder reimbursed at the much lower paid-meal rate. For example, if a CEP school has an ISP of 40%, then 64% of its meals served would be reimbursed at the free-meal rate and 36% would be reimbursed at the paid-meal rate. Schools that identify 62.5% or more students as eligible for free meals receive the free-meal reimbursement for all meals served (62.5% multiplied by 1.6 equals 100%). [**Figure 6**](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#_Ref26258164) provides a visual representation of the CEP reimbursement formula for participating schools, groups of schools, and school districts.

CEP participating schools must recalculate their ISPs at least once every four years, but they can choose to do so more frequently.[106](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn106 "7 C.F.R. § 245.9(f).") While eligibility determinations occur every four years, schools can drop out of CEP at any time.[107](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn107 "7 C.F.R. § 245.9(j).")

CEP is intended to reduce paperwork for families and schools and enable schools to provide more free meals. However, the option may or may not be financially beneficial for schools depending on their proportions of identified students.

Provisions 1, 2, and 3

Schools, groups of schools, and school districts can also use Provisions 1, 2, and 3 to establish alternative certification and reimbursement procedures. These options are intended to reduce paperwork for school administrators and families.[108](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn108 "FNS, \" Provisions 1, 2, and 3 ,\" May 6, 2014, https://www.fns.usda.gov/school-meals/provisions-1-2-and-3 .") The options predate CEP, and unlike CEP, they still require some household applications. A school's decision to participate in a special option may depend on financial considerations. There were 4,440 schools operating Provisions 1, 2, or 3 in school year 2024-2025.[109](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn109 "USDA, 2027 USDA Explanatory Notes—Food and Nutrition Service .")

**Provision 1** allows schools with high proportions (80% or more) of students eligible for free and reduced-price meals to make free meal eligibility determinations that remain in effect for two school years. This reduces the number of applications they have to process (though they still have to process reduced-price meal applications annually).[110](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn110 "Section 11(a)(1) of the NSLA (codified at 42 U.S.C. §1759a(a)(1)); 7 C.F.R. § 245.9; FNS , \" Provisions 1, 2, and 3 . \"")

**Provision 2** and **Provision 3** are open to all schools. As with CEP, schools, groups of schools, or school districts must agree to provide free meals (lunches *or* lunches/breakfasts) to all students in order to participate in Provision 2 or Provision 3. Under Provision 2, schools are reimbursed over a four-year period by applying the proportion of meals served at a free/reduced-price/paid rate during the first year to total meals served in subsequent years. Eligibility determinations in the first year are based on direct certification *and* household applications (a difference from CEP). Under Provision 3, schools are similarly required to make eligibility determinations in the first year of a four-year period. However, in this case, schools receive the same level of federal assistance over the next three years, which is adjusted for enrollment and inflation. (There are no separate payments for free/reduced-price/paid meals.[111](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn111 "Ibid."))

Nutrition Standards and Food Service

Nutrition Standards for School Meals

Nutritional requirements for school meals have changed throughout the history of the school meals programs.[112](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn112 "The current nutrition standards for school meals are located at 7 C.F.R. §210.10 (lunches) and 7 C.F.R. §220.8 (breakfasts).") The most recent child nutrition reauthorization, the HHFKA in 2010, required USDA to update the nutrition standards for school meals within 18 months of the law's enactment based on recommendations from the Food and Nutrition Board at the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine.[113](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn113 "Section 201 of P.L. 111-296 . Institute of Medicine , National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine , School Meals: Building Blocks for Healthy Children , 2010.") The law also provided a "performance-based" bonus reimbursement of 6 cents per lunch (adjusted annually for inflation) for school food authorities certified as compliant with the updated standards (which nearly all school food authorities receive, as discussed in the "[Reimbursement](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#_Ref202782908)" section).[114](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn114 "The rate is 9 cents in school year 2026-2027 . See 91 Federal Register 43597 .")

USDA published the updated nutrition standards for school meals in 2012.[115](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn115 "FNS, \"Nutrition Standards in the National School Lunch and School Breakfast Programs,\" 77 Federal Register 17, January 26, 2012, https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2012/01/26/2012-1010/nutrition-standards-in-the-national-school-lunch-and-school-breakfast-programs .") They were based on the 2010 Dietary Guidelines for Americans (per an existing statutory requirement) as well as the recommendations from the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine.[116](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn116 "The 1994 child nutrition reauthorization ( P.L. 103-448 ) required schools to serve meals consistent with the Dietary Guidelines for Americans. The Dietary Guidelines for Americans are food-based recommendations developed jointly by USDA and the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services and updated every five years. For more information, see CRS Report R44360, Dietary Guidelines for Americans: Frequently Asked Questions .") The standards required increased servings of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and meats/meat alternates in lunches and breakfasts. They also restricted milk to unflavored low-fat (1%) and flavored and unflavored fat-free varieties, set limits on calories and sodium in school meals, and prohibited trans fats in school meals, among other changes. Separate from the final rule, USDA also implemented a requirement in the HHFKA that schools make water available to children during meal service in the cafeteria.[117](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn117 "FNS, \" Revised Child Nutrition Reauthorization 2010: Water Availability During National School Lunch Program Meal Service ,\" July 12, 2011 , https://www.usda.gov/guidance-documents/child-nutrition/fns/child-nutrition-reauthorization-2010-water-availability-during-national-school-lunch-program-meal . Also see FNS , \"Clarification on the Milk and Water Requirements in the School Meal Programs,\" September 23, 2019 , https://www.fna.usda.gov/cn/clarification-milk-and-water-requirement...")

The revised nutrition standards largely took effect in school year 2012-2013 for lunches and in school year 2013-2014 for breakfasts. A few requirements phased in over multiple school years.[118](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn118 "See \"Implementation Timeline\" in FNS , \"Nutrition Standards in the National School Lunch and School Breakfast Programs,\" 77 Federal Register 17, January 26, 2012, https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2012/01/26/2012-1010/nutrition-standards-in-the-national-school-lunch-and-school-breakfast-programs .") Some schools experienced difficulty implementing the new standards.[119](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn119 "For further background, see CRS Report R45486, Child Nutrition Programs: Issues in the 115th Congress .") Subsequent changes to the whole grain, sodium, and milk requirements were made through appropriations acts and USDA rulemaking (for example, to reinstate flavored 1% milk in the programs).[120](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn120 "For more information, see CRS Report R47522, USDA's 2024 Update to Nutrition Standards for School Meals .")

USDA and Congress have initiated other changes to the nutrition standards for school meals, sometimes in response to updated versions of the Dietary Guidelines for Americans. For example, in an April 2024 rule—following the release of the 2015-2020 Dietary Guidelines for Americans—USDA set new limits on added sugars in school meals.[121](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn121 "See CRS Report R47522, USDA's 2024 Update to Nutrition Standards for School Meals . Among other changes, starting in school year 2027-2028, added sugars are limited to less than 10% of weekly calories in school meals.") More recently, in January 2026, the Whole Milk for Healthy Kids Act ([P.L. 119-69](http://www.congress.gov/law/119/pub/69)) was enacted, allowing schools to offer whole milk, 2% milk, and certain non-dairy beverages to students.[122](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn122 "For more information, see CRS Report R49345, Implementation of the Whole Milk for Healthy Kids Act of 2025 (P.L. 119-69): In Brief .")

States and school districts are allowed to implement additional nutritional requirements for school meals so long as they meet the federal standards.[123](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn123 "FNS , \" Enhancing Child Nutrition Program Meal Standards ,\" June 2, 2025, https://www.fns.usda.gov/schoolmeals/enhancing-meal-standards .")

Nutrition Standards for Competitive Foods

The HHFKA also required USDA to develop nutrition standards for other foods sold in NSLP- and SBP-participating schools on campus during the school day. These foods are known as *competitive foods* (i.e., foods sold in competition with school meals). Competitive foods include foods and drinks sold in vending machines, a la carte lines, snack bars and concession stands, and school fundraisers. These foods do not receive federal reimbursement. The HHFKA required USDA to publish proposed nutrition standards for competitive foods within one year of the law's enactment and align the standards with the most recent Dietary Guidelines for Americans.

Relying on recommendations made by the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, FNA promulgated a proposed rule in April 2013 and then an interim final rule in June 2013, which went into effect in school year 2014-2015.[124](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn124 "Institute of Medicine, National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine , Nutrition Standards for Foods in Schools: Leading the Way Toward Healthier Youth , 2007, https://www.nap.edu/catalog/11899/nutrition-standards-for-foods-in-schools-leading-the-way-toward ; FNS , \" Interim Rule: NSLP and SBP Nutrition Standards for All Foods Sold in Schools as Required by the Healthy, Hunger-Free Kids Act of 2010 ,\" 78 Federal Register 79567 , December 31, 2013 , https://www.federal...") The interim final rule created nutrition standards for all non-meal foods and beverages that are sold during the school day (defined as midnight until 30 minutes after dismissal). The final rule, published in July 2016, maintained the interim final rules with minor changes.[125](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn125 "FNS , \" National School Lunch Program and School Breakfast Program: Nutrition Standards for All Foods Sold in School as Required by the Healthy, Hunger-Free Kids Act of 2010; Final Rule,\" 81 Federal Register 50131, July 29, 2016, https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2016/07/29/2016-17227/national-school-lunch-program-and-school-breakfast-program-nutrition-standards-for-all-foods-sold-in . R elated resources are available at the FNA website at https://www.fna.usda.gov/s...") Under the final standards, competitive foods must have certain primary ingredients; meet whole-grain requirements; and comply with calorie, sugar, sodium, and fat limits, among other criteria. Schools are also limited to a list of specific beverages they may sell (with larger portion sizes and caffeine allowed in high schools).[126](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn126 "7 C.F.R. § 210.11 .") The April 2024 final rule (discussed previously) further limited added sugars in milks sold as competitive beverages and exempted bean/hummus dips from fat limits.[127](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn127 "For more information, see CRS Report R47522, USDA's 2024 Update to Nutrition Standards for School Meals .") Subsequently, rulemaking in May 2026 implementing the Whole Milk for Healthy Kids Act ([P.L. 119-69](http://www.congress.gov/law/119/pub/69)) added whole milk and 2% milk as allowable competitive beverages.[128](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn128 "For more information, see CRS Report R49345, Implementation of the Whole Milk for Healthy Kids Act of 2025 (P.L. 119-69): In Brief .")

Fundraisers held outside of the school day and fundraisers in which the food sold is clearly not intended for consumption on campus during the school day are not subject to the competitive food nutrition standards. In addition, the law and the final rule provided states with discretion to exempt infrequent fundraisers selling foods or beverages that do not meet the nutrition standards.

The federal standards are minimum standards, and states and school districts are permitted to issue more stringent policies. Many districts already had local competitive food standards prior to the HHFKA because of the 2004 child nutrition reauthorization law ([P.L. 108-265](https://www.congress.gov/law/108/pub/265)), which required local educational agencies to implement local school wellness policies (discussed in the next section) that included nutritional guidelines for foods sold in schools.

Local School Wellness Policies

A local educational agency participating in the school meals program is required to have alocal school wellness policy, which sets nutrition and health-related goals and guidelines for schools within the jurisdiction.[129](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn129 "The 2004 child nutrition reauthorization created the requirement that l ocal educational agencies establish school wellness policies, and the HHFKA expanded requirements around local school wellness policies. Section 9A of the NSLA (42 U.S.C. §1758b); 7 C.F.R. § 210.31 .") A local school wellness policy must include goals related to nutrition and physical activity, nutrition standards for school foods that meet or exceed federal nutrition standards, and an implementation plan, among other content. Local educational agencies must provide opportunities for input from parents, students, school nutrition professionals, physical education teachers, school health professionals, school administrators, and the general public in developing and updating local school wellness policies.

Food Procurement and Preparation

The majority of foods used in the school meal programs are purchased by school food authorities using federal cash reimbursements or other funds. School food authorities also receive USDA Foods (as discussed previously). School food authorities must comply with federal procurement rules when purchasing foods for the school meals programs.[130](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn130 "7 C.F.R. § 210.21 .") In addition, there is a "Buy American" requirement in statute that requires schools participating in the school meal programs to purchase domestic commodities and products "to the maximum extent practicable."[131](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn131 "Section 12(n) of the NSLA (42 U.S.C. §1760(n)) ; also see 7 C.F.R. § 210.21 (d) . USDA has issued guidance on the implementation of this provision . For example, see FNA, \"Buy American Accommodation Process for School Year 2026-2027,\" June 25, 2026 , https://www.fna.usda.gov/schoolmeals/buy-american-sy26-27 ; and FNS , \" Buy American Provisions Related to the Final Rule - CNP Meal Patterns Consistent with the 2020-25 DGAs ,\" May 29, 2024, https://www.fns.usda.gov/cn/buy-american-pro...") There are also flexibilities pertaining to local foods. Specifically, when procuring unprocessed agricultural products, schools and other child nutrition program operators are allowed to restrict bids or give preference to locally grown, raised, or caught products (as defined by their school food authorities or state agencies).[132](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn132 "7 C.F.R. §210.21(g); Section 9(j) of the NSLA (42 U.S.C. §1758(j)), added by t he 2008 farm bill ( discussed in CRS Report R43950, Local Food Systems: Selected Farm Bill and Other Federal Programs ). More recently, a 2026 USDA rule aimed to clarify such options (FNA, \"Simplified Geographic Preference Procurement in Child Nutriti...")

Meal Times and Settings

In general, lunches and breakfasts are intended to be consumed on-site during the school day.[136](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn136 "FNS, \" Clarification of the Policy on Food Consumption Outside of Foodservice Area, and the Whole Grain-Rich Requirement,\" April 2014, https://www.fns.usda.gov/cn/clarification-policy-food-consumption-outside-foodservice-area-and-whole-grain-rich-requirement .") Surveys have found that schools typically provide roughly 20 minutes for breakfast and 25-30 minutes for lunch.[137](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn137 "FNS Office of Policy Support, Special Nutrition Program Operations Study: State and School Food Authority Policies and Practices for School Meals Programs School Year 2011-12 , March 2014, https://fns-prod.azureedge.net/sites/default/files/SNOPSYear1.pdf ; and School Nutrition Association, \" School Nutrition Operations Report: The State of School Nutrition 2018 , \" August 2018.")

Under SBP, students were traditionally required to arrive early for breakfast and eat it in the cafeteria. However, in recent years, schools and states have increasingly adopted alternative models of breakfast service such as breakfast in the classroom, grab-and-go carts, and breakfast during morning breaks. Anti-hunger advocacy groups have encouraged new models of breakfast service as a way to increase SBP participation.[138](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn138 "For example, see FRAC, \"School Breakfast Expansion Str ategies,\" http://www.frac.org/programs/school-breakfast-program/school-breakfast-expansion-strategies .") According to a 2018 survey by the School Nutrition Association (SNA), a membership and advocacy organization, more than half of surveyed school districts offered both a traditional cafeteria line and alternative modes of breakfast service, while 43% of schools offered a cafeteria line only. Common alternatives were grab-and-go stations (particularly in middle and high schools) and breakfast in the classroom (particularly in elementary schools).[139](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn139 "School Nutrition Association, \" School Nutrition Operations Report . \"")

Administration

At the local level, sponsor organizations administer CACFP for all participating day care homes and centers that elect to have sponsors. Sponsors are responsible for conducting audits of providers, distributing federal reimbursements, and (in some instances) preparing and distributing meals.[150](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn150 "Per statute, a sponsor must make at least one scheduled visit to its sponsored day care home or center each year and periodic unannounced site visits at not less than three-year intervals (Section 17(d)(2) of the NSLA [codified at 42 U.S.C. §1766(d)(2)]). Per regulations, a sponsor must make at least three site visits each year, two of which must be unannounced, with some exceptions (7 C.F.R. § 226.16 (d)(4)(iii)). CACFP has a \"serious deficiency\" process that outlines the procedures...") They can be public or nonprofit institutions or, in some cases, for-profit institutions.[151](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn151 "A f or-profit institution may be s ponsor of a for-profit center if they are part of the same legal entity . Section 17(a)(2)(D) of the NSLA (codified at 42 U.S.C. §1766(a)(2)(D)).") Centers that choose to handle their own administrative responsibilities are referred to as independent centers.

Unlike centers, day care homes are required to have sponsor organizations. Sponsors receive monthly federal administrative payments based on the number of homes for which they are responsible. (Sponsors, on average, have more than 100 day care homes under their supervision.[152](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn152 "The number of day care homes divided by the number of sponsors of day care homes. FNA, June 2026 Keydata Report .")) A sponsor may also receive a portion of the per-meal reimbursement if it has an agreement with the day care home to prepare meals.[153](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn153 "See program regulations at 7 C.F.R. §226.13.") If a center opts to have a sponsor, the sponsor may retain a portion of the per-meal reimbursements for its administrative expenses.[154](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn154 "Sponsors of centers may retain up to 15% of the per-meal reimbursements for administrative expenses . In CACFP, the state administering agency is typically the state department of education or department of health and/or human services.[155](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn155 "For a list of CACFP state administering agencies, see FNA , \" FNA Contacts,\" https://www.fna.usda.gov/contacts .") Further rules are specified at 7 C.F.R. § 226.6(m) .")

As with the school meals programs, FNA provides oversight of state agencies and issues guidance and regulations to states and providers.

Eligibility and Reimbursement

CACFP Centers

The following institutions are eligible to participate as centers in CACFP:

* Public or private *nonprofit* (tax exempt) organizations providing nonresidential child care or adult day care (including school food authorities and Head Start centers)[157](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn157 "Section 17(a)(2) of the NSLA (codified at 42 U.S.C. §1766(a)(2)). Private nonprofit institutions must have tax-exempt status under the Internal Revenue Code of 1986 per program regulations (7 C.F.R. § 226.15 ).")
* Private *for-profit* organizations providing nonresidential child care or adult day care that enroll a certain proportion of low-income participants[158](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn158 "Section 17(a)(2) and Section 17(d)(1)(B) of the NSLA (codified at 42 U.S.C. §1766(a)(2), (d)(1)(B)). Private for-profit institutions qualify if at least 25% of enrolled children meet the income eligibility criteria for free or reduced-price school meals, if the institution s receive compensation under the Social Services Block Grant for at least 25% of their enrolled children, or if at least 25% of enrolled adults are Medicaid or Social Services Block Grant beneficiaries .")
* Emergency shelters for homeless families[159](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn159 "Section 17(a)(2) and Section 17(t) of the NSLA (codified at 42 U.S.C. §1766(a)(2), (t)). Emergency shelters are facilities that provide temporary housing as defined in the McKinney-Vento Homeless Assistance Act (42 U.S.C. §11351).")

Adult day care centers and outside school hour centers fall under the first two categories, but they are subject to specific federal regulations.[160](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn160 "7 C.F.R. § 226.19 ; 7 C.F.R. §226.19a.")

Income eligibility rules for CACFP centers are the same as the school meals programs: Participants in households at or below 130% of the federal poverty guidelines qualify for free meals and snacks, and those between 130% and 185% of the poverty guidelines qualify for reduced-price meals and snacks (a charge of no more than 40 cents for a lunch or supper, 30 cents for a breakfast, and 15 cents for a snack).[161](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn161 "Section 17(c)(4) of the NSLA (codified at 42 U.S.C. §1766(c)(4)); 7 C.F.R. §226.2.") CACFP centers also use similar categorical eligibility criteria, including participation in Head Start, foster child status, and household participation in SNAP, FDPIR, or TANF assistance. Adults are categorically eligible if they participate in SNAP, FDPIR, Supplemental Security Income, or Medicaid.[162](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn162 "See definition of free meal at 7 C.F.R. § 226.2 . Statute provides categorical eligibility for adults who are members of household s receiving assistance under the Food and Nutrition Act of 2008 (7 U.S.C. § 2011 et seq.) and recipients of Supplemental Security Income or Medicaid. Section 17(o)(5) of the NSLA (codified at 42 U.S.C. §1766(o)(5)). For a summary of CACFP eligibility rules , see FNA, \" Why CACFP Is Important ,\" http://www.fna.usda.gov/cacfp/why-cacfp-important .") Eligibility is determined through paper applications or, in some states, direct-certification-like processes.

For CACFP centers, the reimbursement rates for breakfasts and lunches/suppers are the same as the SBP breakfast reimbursement rate and NSLP lunch reimbursement rate, respectively.[163](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn163 "Federal R egister notices for recent school years are available at FNA , \" CACFP Reimbursement Rates ,\" https://www.fna.usda.gov/cacfp/reimbursement-rates .") The largest subsidies are provided for free and reduced-price meals and snacks, while paid meals receive a lower reimbursement.[164](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn164 "Section 17(c) of the NSLA (codified at 42 U.S.C. §1766(c)) .") Unlike the school meals programs, CACFP allows centers certain flexibilities for tracking meal counts and submitting claims for reimbursement.[165](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn165 "7 C.F.R. § 226.9 . Also see FNA , Independent Child Care Centers: A Child and Adult Care Food Program Handbook , May 2014, pp. 46-51, https://www.fna.usda.gov/cacfp/independent-centers-handbook .")

Compared to school meals, CACFP centers are also less likely to collect meal payments from participants and more likely to incorporate meal costs into tuition. Centers are not required to adjust tuition and fees to account for CACFP funding. Centers are also allowed to charge families separately for meals and snacks so long as there are no charges for children who qualify for free meals and limited charges for those who qualify for reduced-price meals.[166](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn166 "7 C.F.R. § 226.6 (f)(1)(i). Also see FNA , Independent Child Care Centers .")

Nutrition Standards and Food Service

Nutrition Standards

The HHFKA required the Secretary of Agriculture to update CACFP's meal patterns. USDA's final rule, effective October 1, 2017, revised the meal patterns for meals and snacks served in centers and day care homes.[169](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn169 "FNS , \" Child and Adult Care Food Program: Meal Pattern Revisions Related to the Healthy, Hunger-Free Kids Act of 2010 : Final Rule ,\" 81 Federal Register 24348 et seq., April 25, 2016, https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2016/04/25/2016-09412/child-and-adult-care-food-program-meal-pattern-revisions-related-to-the-healthy-hunger-free-kids-act .") It also aligned nutrition standards for meals served to preschool-age children through NSLP and SBP.

For infants (under 12 months of age), the revised meal patterns eliminated juice, encouraged breastfeeding, and set guidelines for the introduction of solid foods, among other changes. For children ages one and older and adult participants, the new meal patterns increased whole grains, fruits, and vegetables; limited fat content and flavorings in milk; limited sugar in cereals and yogurts; and prohibited deep-fried foods.[170](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn170 "The original rule limited milk to unflavored whole milk for one-year-olds, unflavored low-fat (1%) or fat-free (skim) milk for two- to five-year-olds, and flavored or unflavored fat-free milk or unflavored low-fat milk for children six years an d older. USDA's April 2024 rule changed the product-specific \" total \" sugar limits to \" added \" sugar limits, as discussed in CRS Report R47522, USDA's 2024 Update to Nutrition Standards for School Meals .") They also required that potable water be available to children throughout the day. Subsequent laws and regulations expanded milk varieties, allowing flavored milks to be served to children ages six and older and allowing higher-fat milks for all children.[171](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn171 "Since 2018, CACFP operators have been allowed to serve flavored 1% milk to children ages six and older (discussed in CRS Report R47522, USDA's 2024 Update to Nutrition Standards for School Meals ). As of 2026, CACFP operators may serve whole milk to any child and 2% milk to any child age two and older (including flavored whole and 2% milk for children ages six and older) , as discussed in CRS Report R49345, Implementation of the Whole Milk for Healthy Kids Act of 2025 (P...")

Procurement and Meal Service

CACFP institutions may purchase their own foods and prepare their own meals, or they may contract with school or food service management companies that prepare meals for them. In either case, institutions must comply with federal, state, and local procurement regulations.[172](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn172 "7 C.F.R. §226.22; FNA , Independent Child Care Centers , pp. 38-39 .") As noted previously, CACFP institutions may also receive USDA Foods. However, most receive cash in lieu of USDA commodities.[173](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn173 "In FY2025, 98.8% of CACFP entitlement commodity aid was in the form of cash, according to FNA, June 2026 Keydata Report .")

Meals must comply with state or local health, safety, and sanitation requirements for storing, preparing, and serving food, and institutions must undergo annual food safety inspections. Family-style meal service (where children can serve themselves from shared bowls or platters) is encouraged in CACFP.[174](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn174 "FNA , Independent Child Care Centers , p. 37.")

Summer Food

The SFSP and the Seamless Summer Option (SSO) provide federal reimbursements for summer meals served to children ages 18 and under.[175](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn175 "For an overview of summer meal benefits for children , see CRS In Focus IF11633, Summer Food for Children: An Overview of Federal Aid .") SFSP is open to school food authorities, local public agencies, and private nonprofit organizations, while SSO is specifically for school food authorities, allowing them to continue operating under certain NSLP/SBP requirements into the summer. The programs share many of the same requirements, including a requirement that children consume meals on-site—known as the *congregate feeding* requirement—except in rural areas (discussed further below).[176](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn176 "7 C.F.R. §225.6(e)( 15). USDA issued waivers of this requirement during the COVID-19 pandemic. USDA has also provided exemptions from the congregate feeding requirement to SFSP and SSO outdoor meal sites experiencing excessive heat.") Summer food benefits for households with school-age children are also available through the Summer EBT program, which was permanently authorized by the FY2023 Consolidated Appropriations Act ([P.L. 117-328](https://www.congress.gov/law/117/pub/328)).

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the summer meals programs were deployed during the regular school year to facilitate free meal service (not discussed in this section).[177](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn177 "Pandemic response poli cies are discussed briefly in the \" Background \" section of this report and in detail in CRS Report R46681, USDA Nutrition Assistance Programs: Response to the COVID-19 Pandemic .")

Summer Food Service Program (SFSP)

The SFSP provides federal aid to school food authorities and other local public and nonprofit organizations that serve meals and snacks to children during the summer months.[178](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn178 "Sponsors may operate SFSP from May through September for children on school vacation. Sponsors may also receive SFSP reimbursements for meals during unanticipated school closures, and sponsors administering SFSP under a continuous school calendar system may operate SFSP at any time (7 C.F.R. §225.6(e)).") Federal aid is provided in the form of per-meal cash reimbursements and a smaller amount of commodity foods and administrative funds (discussed previously). In July 2026 (the peak month of participation), the program served roughly 2.9 million children at approximately 35,500 meal sites.[179](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn179 "FNA, June 2026 Keydata Report . According to a May 2018 GAO report, estimates of participation in SFSP may be unreliable because they have been calculated inconsistently across states and years . See GAO , Actions Needed to Improve Participation Estimates and Address Program Challenges , GAO-18-369 , May 2018, https://www.gao.gov/products/GAO-18-369 .")

Similar to CACFP, SFSP is administered at the local level by sponsor organizations that operate the program at one or more meal sites (the physical location where food is served and eaten). Each SFSP meal site is required to have a sponsor. Sponsors may operate meal sites at a variety of locations, including schools, recreation centers, parks, churches, and public libraries.

Unlike the other child nutrition programs, SFSP participation is generally limited (with the exception of camps and certain rural areas) to meal sites that serve children from *areas in which poor economic conditions exist*—defined as areas or sites in which at least 50% of children are eligible for free and reduced-price school meals (discussed further below).[180](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn180 "Section 13 (a) of the Richard B. Russell National School Lunch Act ( 42 U.S.C. § 1761 (a)).")

Administration

The following public and private nonprofit institutions are eligible to participate in SFSP as sponsors:

* Nonprofit organizations
* School food authorities

According to statute, when selecting sponsors, states must give priority to school food authorities, public and nonprofit organizations that have demonstrated successful program performance in prior years, new public sponsors, and new nonprofit sponsors (in that order). Sponsors are responsible for selecting meal sites, distributing meals to sites, and monitoring sites.[185](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn185 "Most sponsors are responsible for overseeing one to 10 sites according to FNA , USDA Summer Meals Study , vol. 2, prepared by Westat, October 2021, p. 3-1, https://www.fns.usda.gov/cn/usda-summer-meals-study . Officials at meal sites are responsible for distributing meals to children, monitoring the food service, and keeping track of meals served for reimbursement. At times, a sponsor may also be a site. (For example, camps are both sponsors and meal sites.)

Further details are available at 7 C.F.R. § 225.7(d)(2)(ii) .") State agencies receive SFSP funds for administrative costs in addition to general child nutrition program administrative funds (discussed previously in the "[Administrative Funds](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#_Ref23774630)" section).

FNA distributes funds and commodities to state agencies, oversees states' implementation of SFSP, and provides guidance and technical assistance to states and participating institutions.

Eligibility and Reimbursement

SFSP's eligibility and reimbursement rules differ based on the type of meal service or meal site:

* **N****on-congregate** **(off-site)** **meal service**is an option that sponsors may use in rural areas.[187](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn187 "SFSP regulations define rural at 7 C.F.R. § 225.2. USDA publishes a map of such rural areas at FNA , \"Rural Designation,\" https://www.fna.usda.gov/sfsp/rural-designation .") All meals are served for free, but the meal service must either (1) be located in a low-income area (defined as at least 50% of children qualifying for free or reduced-price school meals)[188](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn188 "FNA, \" Area Eligibility in Child Nutrition Programs .\"") or (2) serve children who, on an individual basis, qualify for free or reduced-price school meals.[189](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn189 "FNA , \" Non-Congregate Summer Meal Service ,\" https://www.fna.usda.gov/sfsp/state-agency/non-congregate .") Sponsors may receive reimbursement for up to two meals, or one meal and one snack, per child daily. (However, sponsors may seek state approval to distribute up to 10 days' worth of meals at a time.) This policy became effective in summer 2023. According to USDA, more than 50 states and territories used the rural non-congregate feeding option in summer 2025, serving more than 73 million meals (out of 164 million total SFSP meals) at more than 16,000 rural points of service.[190](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn190 "USDA, 2027 USDA Explanatory Notes—Food and Nutrition Service , p. 34-26. USDA notes that this may be an underestimate of rural non-congregate meals due to recent changes in reporting requirements.")
* **Open sites** aremeal sites that are open to all children in the community.[191](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn191 "Open sites may become \"restricted open sites\" if they need to restrict attendance for reasons related to security, safety, or control (7 C.F.R. §225.2). According to USDA guidance, a sponsor of a restricted open site must publicly announce the restriction. FNA , Summer Food Service Program Administration Guide , 2024 , p. 12 , https://www.fna.usda.gov/sfsp/administration-guide .") Open sites are the most common type of physical site in SFSP, comprising an estimated 83% of summer meal sites in summer 2018 (the most recent year of data).[192](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn192 "FNA, USDA Summer Meals Study , vol. 2 , p. 3-7.") To participate in SFSP, open sites must be located in areas in which at least 50% of the children would be eligible for free or reduced-price school meals as demonstrated through school data, Census Bureau data, or other approved data sources.[193](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn193 "Section 13(a)(1)(A) of the NSLA (42 U.S.C. §1761(a)(1)(A)). For more information on area eligibility, see FNA, \" Area Eligibility in Child Nutrition Programs .\"") Meals must be served free to all children at these sites, and the sponsors of the sites receive reimbursement for every meal served (up to two meals or one meal and one snack per child daily).
* **Closed enrolled sites** are meal sites (other than camps) that serve only enrolled children. For the site to participate in SFSP, closed enrolled sites must establish that at least 50% of children qualify for free or reduced-price school meals based on area eligibility data or individual eligibility information for enrolled children.[194](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn194 "7 C . F . R . § 225.2 , as amended by FNS, \" Streamlining Program Requirements and Improving Integrity in the Summer Food Service Program (SFSP) ,\" 87 Federal Register 57304 , September 19, 2022 .") As with open sites, meals are served free to all children, and sponsors receive reimbursement for every meal served (up to two meals or one meal and one snack per child daily).
* **Camps** include residential and day camps that provide organized programs for enrolled children. Unlike open and closed enrolled sites, camps do not have to demonstrate that a certain percentage of children meet the free and reduced-price eligibility standards in order to participate in SFSP. Instead, eligibility works like NSLP and SBP: Camps make eligibility determinations using similar income and categorical eligibility criteria for free and reduced-price meals.[195](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn195 "Section 13(a)(5) of the NSLA (codified at 42 U.S.C. §1761(a)(5)).") However, unlike with the school meals programs, camps receive the same reimbursement rate for free and reduced-price meals. Camps may receive reimbursement for up to three meals or two meals and one snack per eligible child daily.[196](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn196 "Section 13(b)(2) of the NSLA (codified at 42 U.S.C. §1761(b)(2)).") Camps are not required to serve meals for free to all children, and there is no paid reimbursement provided for full-price meals.
* **Migrant sites** must demonstrate that they serve predominantly migrant children as certified by migrant organizations or sponsors.[197](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn197 "7 C . F . R . §225.6(g)(1)(xiii).") They follow the same eligibility and reimbursement rules as do open sites, except that they may receive reimbursement for up to three meals or two meals and one snack per child daily.[198](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn198 "Section 13(b)(2) of the NSLA (codified at 42 U.S.C. §1761(b)(2)).")

SFSP reimbursement rates (inflation-adjusted rates displayed in [**Table 8**](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#_Ref324258883)) are based on a statutory formula that takes into account operating costs (e.g., food, storage, and labor costs) and administrative costs.[199](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn199 "The authority for operating cost reimbursements is provided in Section 13(b)(1) of the NSLA ( 42 U.S.C. § 1761(b)(1) ), and the authority for the administrative cost reimbursement is provided in Section 13(b)(3) of the NSLA (42 U.S.C. §1761(b)(3)).") While such factors are taken into account in calculating the rates, once sponsors receive the funds they can use them for any allowable program cost. Higher reimbursements are provided for sponsors of rural meal sites and *self-preparation* sites (meal sites in which sponsors rather than vendors prepare food).

Nutrition Standards

Meals and snacks served through SFSP must meet federal nutrition standards. In contrast to the child nutrition programs discussed thus far, SFSP's nutrition standards are not required to align with the Dietary Guidelines for Americans but are "prescribed by the Secretary on the basis of tested nutritional research."[200](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn200 "Section 13(f) of the NSLA (codified at 42 U.S.C. §1761(f)).") Program regulations outline the nutrition standards for breakfasts, lunches/suppers, and snacks.[201](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn201 "7 C.F.R. § 225.16 (d).") The standards prescribe minimum servings of fruits and vegetables, meats/meat alternatives, breads/bread alternatives, and milk. Unlike school meals and CACFP, there are no limits on calories, saturated fats, and milk varieties in SFSP. Participating school food authorities may instead choose to use the NSLP and/or SBP nutrition standards for SFSP.[202](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn202 "7 C.F.R. § 225.16 (f).")

Meal Service

As noted, children are required to consume meals on-site in SFSP unless they receive meals under the rural non-congregate feeding policy. There are also requirements around the timing of congregate meals in SFSP, including at least one hour between meals.[203](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn203 "7 C.F.R. § 225.16 (c).") Like the other child nutrition programs, SFSP sponsors must comply with local or state health and sanitation requirements.

Seamless Summer Option (SSO)

School food authorities may participate in SFSP, or they can choose to offer summer meals through SSO. SSO allows school food authorities to continue operating under certain NSLP/SBP requirements into the summer.[204](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn204 "Section 13(a)(8) of the NSLA (codified at 42 U.S.C. §1761(a)(8)).") For example, it allows them to use the school meals programs' nutrition standards, administrative review process, and reimbursement rates (see [**Table 4**](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#_Ref324679596) for NSLP/SBP reimbursement rates). Other requirements are the same as SFSP, including rural area and site eligibility rules.[205](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn205 "For a comparison of SFSP and SSO, see FNS , \"Comparison of Programs: SFSP/NSLP/Seamless Option ,\" January 22, 2015, https://www.fna.usda.gov/summer/comparison-programs .") School food authorities are the only eligible sponsors in SSO, but they can operate the program at a variety of meal sites (e.g., parks, recreation centers, libraries) and through meal delivery and other off-site feeding mechanisms in rural areas.[206](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn206 "For USDA's guidance on the rural non-congregate feeding policy, see FNA, \" Non-Congregate Summer Meal Service ,\" https://www.fna.usda.gov/sfsp/state-agency/non-congregate .")

The school lunch and breakfast reimbursement rates used in SSO are lower than SFSP's reimbursement rates. However, school food authorities participating in SSO also receive the NSLP commodity reimbursement (discussed in the "[Commodity Assistance](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#_Ref150774057)" section). School food authorities may also have a reduced administrative burden under SSO. A 2018 study by USDA found that three-quarters of sponsors operated SFSP and one-quarter operated SSO.[207](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn207 "FNS, USDA Summer Meals Study , vol. 2 , pp. 3-4 to 3-7.")

Special Milk Program (SMP)

SMP provides funding for milk served in approximately 1,500 schools, child care institutions, summer camps, and other institutions.[219](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn219 "FNA , \"National Level Annual Summary Tables FY1969- 202 5 : Special Milk Program (SMP) — Outlets and Milk Served,\" September 11, 2026 , https://www.fna.usda.gov/pd/child-nutrition-tables .") Generally, schools and other participating institutions may not participate in other child nutrition meal service programs along with SMP. However, schools may administer SMP for pre-kindergartners and kindergartners who are in part-day sessions and do not have access to the school meals programs.[220](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn220 "Section 3 of the Child Nutrition Act of 1966 (42 U.S.C. §1772).")

In SMP, participating institutions provide milk to children for free and/or at a subsidized paid price. Institutions are reimbursed differently based on whether they decide to provide milk for free to all children, sell milk to all children, or combine these options (provide free milk to eligible children and sell milk to other children) (see [**Table 9**](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#_Ref324679885)). If institutions choose the combined option, they must establish eligibility rules for free milk.[221](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn221 "Institutions can set eligibility at or below the income threshold for free school meals (130% of the poverty guidelines ) (7 C.F.R. § 215.13a ).")

SMP's nutritional requirements for milk are consistent with the other child nutrition programs: Whole milk is required for children under the age of two, and children ages two and older may be served whole, 2%, 1%, or fat-free milk.[222](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn222 "Since 2018, CACFP operators have been allowed to serve flavored 1% milk to children ages six and older (discussed in CRS Report R47522, USDA's 2024 Update to Nutrition Standards for School Meals ). After-School Meals and Snacks

CACFP and NSLP both provide federal support for snacks and meals served during after-school programs.[224](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn224 "The CACFP At-Risk Afterschool snack/meal program is authorized in Section 17(r) of the NSLA (42 U.S.C. §1766(r)) . T he NSLP snack program is authorized in Section 17A of the NSLA (42 U.S.C. §1766a).") The CACFP At-Risk Afterschool component provides reimbursement for up to one snack and one meal (usually supper) per child daily, whereas the NSLP Afterschool Snack option provides reimbursement for snacks only. Reimbursement rates for CACFP At-Risk Afterschool meals/snacks and NSLP after-school snacks are the same as CACFP reimbursement rates (listed in [**Table 7**](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#_Ref24554789)).

CACFP At-Risk Afterschool Meals and Snacks

The CACFP At-Risk Afterschool component was authorized as a demonstration project in 1994 ([P.L. 103-448](https://www.congress.gov/law/103/pub/448)), expanded over time, and made available to all states by the HHFKA ([P.L. 111-296](https://www.congress.gov/law/111/pub/296)).[225](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn225 "Prior to the HHFKA ( P.L. 111-296 ) , 13 states were permitted to offer CACFP At-Risk After-School meals (instead of just a snack) . T he law allowed all CACFP state agencies to offer such meals. S.Rept. 111-178 , p. 7.") At-Risk Afterschool meal providers include the same types of institutions that are eligible to become CACFP centers. (See the institutional eligibility rules in the "[CACFP Centers](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#_Ref28875694)" section.) However, they do not need to operate the child/day care component of CACFP in order to operate the At-Risk Afterschool component. Additionally, At-Risk Afterschool providers must be located in the attendance areas of schools in which at least 50% of children are eligible for free or reduced-price school meals.[226](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn226 "Emergency shelters do not need to meet this requirement.") The after-school program must also have "an educational or enrichment purpose."[227](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn227 "Section 17(r) of the NSLA (42 U.S.C. §1766(r)).") Unlike the traditional CACFP, which is available to children ages 12 and under, the At-Risk Afterschool component allows participation through age 18.

Participating institutions receive reimbursement for up to one snack and one meal (e.g., supper) per child daily, and meals and snacks are provided for free to all children. Meals and snacks must meet federal nutrition standards.[228](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn228 "7 C.F.R. § 226.20 .") Institutions may operate the At-Risk Afterschool program in the after-school hours and on weekends, holidays, and breaks during the school year.

In FY2025, the CACFP At-Risk Afterschool component served a daily average of 2.8 million children.[229](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn229 "USDA, 2027 USDA Explanatory Notes—Food and Nutrition Service , p. 34-25.")

NSLP Afterschool Snacks

The NSLP Afterschool Snack option was authorized in the 1998 child nutrition reauthorization act ([P.L. 105-336](https://www.congress.gov/law/105/pub/336)). It allows NSLP-participating schools to receive federal reimbursement for one snack per child daily in eligible after-school programs during the school year.[230](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn230 "Schools may also operate the NSLP Afterschool Snack component in the hours after summer school sessions.") According to USDA guidance, eligible after-school programs must provide "organized, regularly scheduled activities in a structured and supervised environment," including an educational or enrichment activity.[231](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn231 "FN A , \" NSLP Afterschool Snack Service — FAQs ,\" https://www.fna.usda.gov/school-meals/afterschool-snacks-faqs .")

Schools that choose to operate the NSLP Afterschool Snack component may do so in one of two ways:

* 1. As with the CACFP At-Risk Afterschool component, if at least 50% of students in the school are eligible for free and reduced-price meals, the school may provide free snacks to all children.
* 2. If this criterion is not met, the school may offer free, reduced-price, or full price snacks based on household income eligibility (as with the school meals programs).

Historically, the vast majority of snacks provided through this program have been provided under the first option.[232](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn232 "Joanne Guthrie, \" Feeding Children After School: The Expanding Role of USDA Child Nutrition Programs , \" USDA , Economic Research Service, March 1, 2012, https://www.ers.usda.gov/amber-waves/2012/march/feeding-children-after-school .") Snacks served through the NSLP Afterschool Snack component must comply with federal nutrition standards.[233](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn233 "7 C.F.R. §210.10(o). An April 2024 final rule aligned required nutrition standards in the NSLP Afterschool Snack program with those used in the CACFP At-Risk Afterschool meal and snack program starting in school year 2025-2026, as discussed in CRS Report R47522, USDA's 2024 Update to Nutrition Standards for School Meals .")

As of FY2025, the NSLP Afterschool Snack component served 1.4 million snacks on average daily in approximately 18,000 schools.[234](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn234 "FNA, June 2026 Keydata and communication with FNA in September 2026 .")

Fresh Fruit and Vegetable Program (FFVP)

FFVP provides formula grants to states to fund fresh fruit and vegetable snacks in selected elementary schools.[235](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn235 "Section 19 of the NSLA (42 U.S.C. §1769a). FFVP currently operates according to statute and USDA guidance. USDA issued a proposed rule in 2012 to codify statutory requirements in regulations, but a final rule has not been published.") Under a statutory formula, about half the funding is distributed equally to each state, and the remainder is allocated by state population. States must prioritize funding for schools with high proportions of students who are eligible for free or reduced-price meals. Schools must participate in NSLP in order to receive FFVP grants. States set annual per-student grant amounts (between $50 and $75). Schools may provide fresh fruit and vegetable snacks to students at any time of day outside of the breakfast or lunch service.[236](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn236 "FNA , Fresh Fruit and Vegetable Program: A Handbook for Schools , December 2010, https://www.fna.usda.gov/ffvp/handbook-schools .") Schools offer snacks to all children in attendance regardless of family income.

As noted previously, FFVP's funding structure differs from the other child nutrition programs. FFVP is funded by a mandatory transfer of funds from Section 32 of the Act of August 24, 1935. The authorizing law provided $150 million for school year 2011-2012, which is adjusted annually for inflation.[237](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn237 "Section 19(i) of the NSLA (42 U.S.C. §1769a(i)).") For FY2026, FNA allocated approximately $243 million (including carryover funds) for FFVP to states.[238](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn238 "FNA, \"Fresh Fruit and Vegetable Program: Allocation of Funds for FY 2026,\" June 4, 2025, https://www.fna.usda.gov/ffvp/allocation-funds-fy26 .")

FFVP has been amended over time both by farm bills and by child nutrition reauthorization bills. The 2004 child nutrition reauthorization act ([P.L. 108-265](https://www.congress.gov/law/108/pub/265)) made the program permanent and provided funding for a small number of states and Indian reservations. The 2008 farm bill ([P.L. 110-246](https://www.congress.gov/law/110/pub/246)) expanded FFVP's mandatory funding through Section 32 and enabled all states to participate in the program. The 2014 farm bill ([P.L. 113-79](https://www.congress.gov/law/113/pub/79)) essentially made no changes to FFVP but provided $5 million for a demonstration project to test offering frozen, canned, and dried fruits and vegetables in the program. Four states (Alaska, Delaware, Kansas, and Maine) participated in the pilot in school year 2014-2015, and an evaluation was published in 2017.[239](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn239 "Ronette Briefel et al., Evaluation of the Pilot Project for Canned, Frozen, or Dried Fruits and Vegetables in the Fresh Fruit and Vegetable Program (FFVP-CFD) , FNS, prepared by Mathematica Policy Research , January 2017 , https://www.fns.usda.gov/research/evaluation-elementary-schools-pilot-project-canned-frozen-or-dried-fruits-and-vegetables-fresh . The Emergence of School Lunches and the National School Lunch Program

When the first federal aid for school lunches was provided in the 1930s, local school lunch programs were already operational in many cities and localities across the United States. Many of these early lunch programs were started by charitable women's organizations at the turn of the century in an effort to feed hungry children. Over time, they transitioned to school boards and school districts. These programs received a combination of private, local, and state funding.[258](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn258 "The first cities to institute school lunch programs included Boston, Chicago, Cleveland, Cincinnati, Los Angeles, Milwaukee, New York, Philadelphia, and St. Louis. Gunderson, \" National School Lunch Program \" ; A. R. Ruis, Eating to Learn, Learning to Eat: The Origins of School Lunch in the United States (Rutgers University Press, 2017), pp. 22-27; Susan Levine, School Lunch Politics: The Surprising History of America 's Favorite Welfare Program ( Princeton University Pres...")

The federal government became involved in school lunch programs during the Great Depression to both feed hungry children and support the farm economy. Initially, federal aid was provided in the form of cafeteria equipment and labor. In 1932, the Reconstruction Finance Corporation began providing loans to states and school districts to cover the cost of cafeteria space and equipment for school lunch programs.[259](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn259 "Levine, School Lunch Politics , p. 44.") In 1935, the Works Progress Administration, a New Deal agency, began sponsoring women's employment in school lunchrooms. Federal food support for school lunches began that same year, when Section 32 of the Act of August 24, 1935 (P.L. 74-320), was enacted. The act provided 30% of customs receipts to USDA to purchase surplus commodities from farmers impacted by the depression. These commodities were donated through various outlets for domestic consumption, including school lunch programs.

With commodity aid came the first federal regulations for school lunch programs. USDA required recipient organizations, through their agreements with state agencies, to operate school lunch programs on a nonprofit basis, maintain any existing local funding for school lunches, keep records of foods received, serve meals free to poor children, and ensure that such children would not be identified to their peers, among other requirements.[260](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn260 "Gunderson, \" National School Lunch Program \" ; USDA, Bureau of Agricultural Economics, \" The School Lunch Program and Agricultural Surplus Disposal, \" Miscellaneous Pub lications No. 467, October 1941.")

The availability of federal aid contributed to a rapid increase in the number of school lunch programs. However, in 1943, federal commodity aid declined as Section 32 surplus commodities were diverted to feed U.S. servicemembers in World War II. In addition, federal support for lunchroom labor disappeared with the elimination of the Works Progress Administration.[261](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn261 "Gunderson, \" National School Lunch Program .\"") In the midst of declining aid, Congress provided the first cash assistance—$50 million in Section 32 funds—for "a school milk and lunch program" in the 1944 Department of Agriculture Appropriation Act (P.L. 78-129). The introduction of cash assistance marked a shift in the lunch program.[262](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn262 "C ommodity assistance continued to make up a large share of federal support for school lunches and exceeded cash assistance until 1970. Jean Yavis Jones, \"Appendix A: Child Nutrition Programs: A Narrative Legislative History and Program Analysis , \" in U.S. Congress, House Committee on Education and Labor, Child Nutrition Programs: Issues for the 103 d Congress , 103 rd Cong., 2 nd sess., Serial No. 103-H (GPO, 1994).") For the first time, schools could purchase their own foods in addition to receiving federally purchased commodities.

Annual appropriations acts continued cash support for school lunches until 1946, when the National School Lunch Act (P.L. 79-396) was enacted. Signed into law on June 4, 1946, by President Truman, the act permanently authorized appropriations of "such sums as may be necessary" for the National School Lunch Program. (The act would later be renamed the "Richard B. Russell National School Lunch Act," recognizing Senator Russell's role in the passage of the legislation and his earlier support for the school lunch program within New Deal programs and during his tenure as chairman of the Agriculture Appropriations subcommittee.[263](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn263 "J. T. Gay , \"Richard B. Russell and the National School Lunch Program,\" Georgia Historical Quarterly , vol. 80 , no. 4 ( 1996 ) , pp. 860-863.")) The law required participating schools to serve lunches for free or at a reduced price to students whom local school authorities deemed unable to pay the full cost of a lunch. Funds were to be distributed to states based on the number of school-age children in the state and the state's need, as measured by per-capita income, and states were to match federal funds dollar for dollar. States were to distribute funding on a monthly basis to schools based on the number of meals served that met "minimum nutritional requirements prescribed by the Secretary on the basis of tested, nutritional research" (P.L. 79-396).[264](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn264 "Jones, \"Appendix A: Child Nutrition Programs , \" p. 41 . USDA provided t he h ighest reimbursement (up to 9 cents) for a \"complete\" Type A meal that was designed to provide one-third to one-half of a child's dai ly nutritional intake; up to 6 cents was pr ovided for an \"incomplete\" Type B meal; and up to 2 cents for a Type C meal , which was simply a half-pint of milk . To see the original nutritional requirements for each type of meal, see Gunderson, \" National School Lunch Pr...") Cash assistance could not be used for cafeteria equipment, and separate funds were authorized for this purpose ($10 million annually). However, Congress subsequently prohibited appropriations for equipment assistance from FY1948 to FY1967.[265](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn265 "Jones, \"Appendix A: Child Nutrition Programs ,\" pp. 59-61.")

NSLP remained relatively unchanged from 1946 to 1960. However, during this time frame, concerns emerged over the funding formula. One concern was that the formula prioritized funding for schools with large numbers of school-age children rather than actual participants in the program. There was also concern that schools with high proportions of needy children received the same amount of aid as those with wealthier families, even though they had to serve a larger number of meals for free or at a reduced-price.[266](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn266 "Jones, \"Appendix A: Child Nutrition Programs ,\" pp. 41, 63-64; Levine, School Lunch Politics , p. 128.") In 1962, P.L. 87-823 changed the funding formula to be based on the number of school lunches served in the state in the preceding school year instead of the number of school-age children. The law also authorized additional "special assistance" for state-selected schools in poor economic areas. (However, special assistance was not funded until 1966.[267](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn267 "CRS DL741517, \"Brief History of Child Nutrition Legislation,\" by Kathryn Michelman and Joe Richardson , 1974. According to Gunderson , \"The selection of the schools for receiving the special reimbursement from Section 11 funds was to be based upon five factors: The economic condition of the area from which the schools draw attendance; The need for free or reduced-price lunches; The percent of free or reduced-price lunches being served in such schools; The price of the lunch in such schools as..."))

Other notable changes to NSLP occurred in the 1970s. In 1970, P.L. 91-248 extended special assistance to all schools participating in NSLP.[268](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn268 "CRS Memorandum 831481, Chronology of Major Federal Food Assistance Legislation (Food Stamps, Child Nutrition Programs and Elderly Nutrition) 1932-1983 , by Jean Yavis Jones, November 1983 .") The law also reduced the state matching requirement and established the first national eligibility guidelines for free and reduced-price meals at 100% of the federal poverty guidelines (later in the decade increased to 125% for free lunches and 195% for reduced-price lunches). In 1971, another significant change occurred with the enactment of P.L. 92-153, which guaranteed states a certain level of federal cash assistance by specifying average per-meal reimbursement rates for free, reduced-price, and paid lunches.[269](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn269 "CRS Memorandum 831481 ; Jones, \"Appendix A: Child Nutrition Programs ,\" p. 43.")

The Addition of Other Child Nutrition Programs

In the 1960s, federal child nutrition efforts expanded beyond school lunches.[270](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn270 "Levine, School Lunch Politics , p. 127; Jones, \"Appendix A: Child Nutrition Programs ,\" p. 64.") On October 11, 1966, the Child Nutrition Act of 1966 (P.L. 89-642) was enacted.[271](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn271 "Section 2 provided the purpose of the act: \" In recognition of the demonstrated relationship between food and good nutrition and the capacity of children to develop and learn, based on the years of cumulative successful experience under the National School Lunch Program with its significant contributions in the field of applied nutrition research, it is hereby declared to be the policy of Congress that these efforts shall be extended, expanded, and strengthened under the authority of the Secre...") It formally authorized the SMP and authorized the SBP as a pilot program. The SMP was based on predecessor USDA school milk programs that had operated since the 1940s.[272](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn272 "Gunderson, \" National School Lunch Program .\"") SBP was a newer concept that USDA had piloted in the 1965-1966 school year.[273](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn273 "U.S. Congress, House Committee on Agriculture, Hearing on H.R. 13361 and Bills to Amend and Make Permanent the Special Milk Program for Children , committee print, 89 th Cong., 2 nd Sess., June 23 and June 24, 1966, H. Prt. 66-126 (GPO, 1966), p. 16.") In a House Agriculture Committee hearing on the Child Nutrition Act, then-Secretary of Agriculture Orville L. Freeman testified:

These proposals will permit us to begin a comprehensive effort to broaden child nutrition programs in this country. They are based on what we have learned in 20 years of administration of the National School Lunch Act, and they reflect a careful assessment of gaps which now exist in the nutritional needs of children in this country.[274](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn274 "Ibid . , p. 12.")

The SMP provided reimbursements for milk in schools, nonprofit child care centers, summer camps, and other nonprofit institutions. At the time, schools and institutions could participate in both SMP and NSLP. Meanwhile, SBP was authorized for two fiscal years and required states to prioritize funds for "schools drawing attendance from areas in which poor economic conditions exist and to those schools to which a substantial proportion of the children enrolled must travel long distances daily."[275](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn275 "P.L. 89-642.") (Congress later expanded priority to include "schools in which there is a special need for improving the nutrition and dietary practices of children of working mothers and children from low-income families."[276](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn276 "P.L. 92-32 .")) The Child Nutrition Act of 1966 also gave the Secretary of Agriculture the authority to provide higher reimbursements to schools with "severe need." As with NSLP, the law specified that breakfasts "meet minimum nutritional requirements prescribed by the Secretary on the basis of tested nutritional research" and be served for free or at a reduced price to children unable to pay the full price of a meal, as determined by local school authorities (P.L. 89-642).

In 1968, child nutrition efforts were further expanded with the authorization of the Special Food Service Program for Children (SFSPC), a pilot program to fund meals in summer and child care settings (P.L. 90-302). SFSPC provided the first federal assistance for summer meals for children and the first dedicated assistance for meals served in child care settings.[277](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn277 "Early appropriations for school lunches in 1944 and 1945 had allowed states to spend a small percentage of funds on food served in child care centers. The National School Lunch Act of 1946 made permanent support for meals served in residential child care institutions only.") Similar to SBP, SFSPC was targeted to areas with poor economic conditions and a high number of working mothers.

In 1975, the program was split into the separate Child Care Food Program (CCFP) and the SFSP ([P.L. 94-105](https://www.congress.gov/law/94/pub/105)). CCFP was open to public and nonprofit institutions that met child care licensing or other official child care standards, while SFSP retained a focus on institutions in low-income areas.[278](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn278 "Low-income areas was defined as areas in which at least one-third of children qualified for free or reduced-price meals.") Meals were provided for free to all children at SFSP sites, whereas CCFP conducted free and reduced-price eligibility determinations like NSLP.

1980-2010

The long-standing growth of child nutrition programs contrasted with budget cuts in the early 1980s, which were part of larger efforts to reduce federal domestic spending.[279](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn279 "Jones, \"Appendix A: Child Nutrition Programs ,\" p. 44.") The Omnibus Reconciliation Act of 1980 ([P.L. 96-499](https://www.congress.gov/law/96/pub/499)) reduced FY1981 funding for child nutrition programs by approximately $400 million (9%) of the child nutrition budget.[280](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn280 "CRS Memorandum 831481 ; Jones, \"Appendix A: Child Nutrition Programs ,\" p. 44.") The law achieved savings by lowering reimbursement rates in the programs and eliminating commodity assistance for breakfast, among other changes.[281](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn281 "CRS Memorandum 831481; Jones, \"Appendix A: Child Nutrition Programs ,\" p. 86.") Larger spending cuts followed with the Omnibus Reconciliation Act of 1981, which made changes that collectively cut $1.4 billion (25%) of the child nutrition budget (Title VIII of [P.L. 97-35](https://www.congress.gov/law/97/pub/35)).[282](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn282 "Jones, \"Appendix A: Child Nutrition Programs ,\" p. 44.") Many of the policy changes made by the law remain in place today. For example, the law restricted eligibility from 195% of the federal poverty guidelines to 185% for reduced-price meals and set eligibility at 130% for free meals in the NSLP, SBP, and CCFP. It also raised allowable charges for reduced-price lunches from 20 cents to 40 cents and for reduced-price breakfasts from 10 cents to 30 cents.[283](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn283 "CRS Memorandum 831481.") In a major change to SMP, the law excluded schools/institutions that participated in other child nutrition meals programs from participating in SMP—cutting SMP's budget by 77%.[284](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn284 "Jones, \"Appendix A: Child Nutrition Programs ,\" pp. 44-45.") In CCFP, the law restricted participation from children ages 18 and under to children ages 12 and under and reduced the maximum number of reimbursable meals from three meals and two snacks per child daily to two meals and one snack per child daily. The law also eliminated equipment assistance for school meals.

Child nutrition programs were subsequently excluded from budget deficit reduction measures in the late 1980s and 1990s, and new policies led to the expansion of the programs during this time frame.[285](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn285 "Jones, \"Appendix A: Child Nutrition Programs ,\" p. 45.") For example, amendments to the programs in these years authorized start-up grants for school breakfast programs, expanded CCFP to adult day care centers (and renamed it the Child and Adult Care Food Program, or CACFP), and provided new funding for after-school snacks through NSLP and CACFP.[286](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn286 "Jones, \"Appendix A: Child Nutrition Programs ,\" pp. 46-47 .") But what had potentially the longest-term impact on expansion was a policy change intended to reduce paperwork in the school meals programs: automatic (categorical) eligibility for free meals for children in food stamp (now SNAP) and Aid to Families with Dependent Children (now TANF) households, which was enacted in 1986—and direct certification of such children for free meals without household applications, which was enacted in 1989.[287](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn287 "FNS , Office of Research and Analysis, Direct Certification in the National School Lunch Program: State Implementation Progress Report to Congress , December 2008, p. 3, https://www.fns.usda.gov/direct-certification-national-school-lunch-program-state-implementation-progress .")

Other policies in the late 1980s and 1990s focused on improving program integrity. The 1989 child nutrition reauthorization ([P.L. 101-147](https://www.congress.gov/law/101/pub/147)) required USDA to create a standardized process through which states would review school food authorities' administration of NSLP and SBP (known as administrative reviews).[288](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn288 "Jones, \"Appendix A: Child Nutrition Programs ,\" p. 68 .") In CACFP, following USDA inspector general audits in the 1990s that found instances of abuse and mismanagement, the Agricultural Risk Protection Act of 2000 ([P.L. 106-224](https://www.congress.gov/law/106/pub/224)) made a number of changes aimed at improving program integrity in CACFP.[289](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn289 "See, for example, USDA , Office of Inspector General, Food and Nutrition Service: Child and Adult Care Food Program: National Report on Program Abuses , August 1999, https://usdaoig.oversight.gov/sites/default/files/reports/2023-07/27601-7-SF.pdf .") The act required CACFP sponsors to conduct more frequent and unannounced site visits of sponsored centers and homes, restricted nonprofit institutions' eligibility to those with tax-exempt status, and excluded institutions deemed ineligible to participate in any other public program based on violations of program requirements. Other legislation was aimed at improving program integrity in the school meals programs.

Program integrity continued to be a focus in the 2004 child nutrition reauthorization ([P.L. 108-265](https://www.congress.gov/law/108/pub/265)), which made changes to school food authorities' verification of household applications for free and reduced-price meals. Specifically, the law set a sample size of applications that schools must review, established a focus on "error-prone" applications (applications near the income eligibility thresholds), and authorized direct (automatic) household application verification processes.[290](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn290 "USDA , FNS , \" Verification of Eligibility for Free and Reduced Price Meals in the National School Lunch and School Breakfast Programs, \" 73 Federal Register 76847, December 18, 2008.") In addition, the law required states to conduct additional administrative reviews of school food authorities with a high level of administrative error or risk of error.[291](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn291 "For more information, see CRS Report RL33299, Child Nutrition and WIC Legislation in the 108th and 109th Congresses .")

The 2004 child nutrition reauthorization also continued the expansion of free school meals to new categories of children. Specifically, the law extended categorical eligibility and direct certification for free school meals to homeless children, migrant children, and children served under the Runaway and Homeless Youth Act.

The most recent child nutrition reauthorization as of the date of this report was the HHFKA ([P.L. 111-296](https://www.congress.gov/law/111/pub/296)), which continued the expansion of school meals in a few ways. It made foster children categorically eligible for free school meals, and allowed direct certification of such children. It also included a pilot project for direct certification (but not categorical eligibility) of children in Medicaid households for free and reduced-price meals based on an income test. In addition, the HHFKA created the Community Eligibility Provision, through which eligible schools can provide free meals to all students.

As discussed in this report, the HHFKA also made changes to nutritional requirements in the school meals programs and CACFP. Specifically, the law required USDA to update the nutrition standards for school meals within a certain time frame and align the standards with the Dietary Guidelines for Americans (per an existing statutory requirement).[292](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn292 "T he 1994 child nutrition reauthorization ( P.L. 103-448 ) required schools to serve breakfasts and lunches that were consistent with the Dietary Guidelines for A mericans.") The law also required USDA to issue new nutrition standards regulating all foods sold on school campuses during the school day ("competitive foods"). (Previous standards applied only to competitive foods sold during meal service.) In addition, the HHFKA required USDA to update the nutrition standards for CACFP meals and snacks within a certain time frame and align them with the Dietary Guidelines for Americans.[293](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R46234.html#fn293 "For more information, see CRS Report R45486, Child Nutrition Programs: Issues in the 115th Congress . Also see Janet Poppendieck, Free for All: Fixing School Food in America (Univer sity of California Press, 2010).")
