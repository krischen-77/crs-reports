Recent Changes to DOJ’s Interpretation of
Olmstead v. L.C. ex rel. Zimring


October 26, 2026

In 1999, the Supreme Court decided the landmark disability discrimination case Olmstead v. L.C. ex rel.
Zimring, which established that “unjustified institutional isolation of persons with disabilities is a form of
discrimination.” Olmstead applied Title II of the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA Title II), which
prohibits disability discrimination by state and local governments. Title II is often interpreted consistently
with Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 (Section 504), which prohibits disability
discrimination by recipients of federal funding and federal agencies. When Olmstead was decided, long-
standing Title II and Section 504 regulations from the Department of Justice (DOJ) required covered
entities to serve people with disabilities “in the most integrated setting appropriate to the[ir] needs.”
Section 504 regulations from other agencies, including the Department of Health and Human Services
(HHS), echoed this “integration mandate.”

On June 18, 2026, the DOJ Office of Legal Counsel (OLC) published an opinion (OLC Opinion)
narrowing DOJ’s interpretation of Olmstead. OLC determined that Olmstead, Section 504, and Title II do
not “impose[] an integration mandate.” Rather than requiring services in integrated settings, OLC
interpreted Olmstead, Title II, and Section 504 to prohibit only the irrational institutionalization of people
with disabilities. Subsequently, DOJ has withdrawn from or changed its position in several Olmstead
matters on grounds related to the OLC Opinion and for other cited reasons. These matters include a case
challenging HHS’s regulations implementing the integration mandate. As a result of DOJ’s change in
position, the HHS integration regulations have been vacated.

This Legal Sidebar reviews the background of the integration mandate and the Olmstead decision. It
discusses OLC’s new interpretation of Olmstead and certain recent DOJ activities related to Olmstead
enforcement. It concludes with some potential implications of these developments.

Section 504, Title II, and the Integration Mandate

Section 504 prohibits disability discrimination by federal funding recipients and federal agencies, and
Title II prohibits disability discrimination by state and local governments. The integration mandate has
been part of Section 504’s interpretation since its original regulations were promulgated, and it was
adopted into Title II’s regulations after the ADA’s passage as well. Relevant to the Supreme Court’s

holding in Olmstead, both statutes have also consistently been interpreted to require covered entities to
make reasonable modifications to accommodate people with disabilities.

Because Section 504 attaches conditions to federal funding, each federal agency promulgates Section 504
regulations governing its grantees. Responsibility for coordinating federal Section 504 enforcement
originally rested with the former Department of Health, Education, and Welfare (HEW) and was later
shifted to DOJ. HEW promulgated regulations coordinating executive agency implementation of Section
504 in 1978, and DOJ adopted those regulations in full in 1981. They have not been substantively
amended since. The Section 504 coordination regulations require recipients of federal funding to, among
other things, “administer programs and activities in the most integrated setting appropriate to the needs of
qualified handicapped persons.” At the time of promulgation, HEW described this requirement as
“intrinsic to section 504.”

When Congress passed the ADA in 1990, it included findings that “historically, society has tended to
isolate and segregate individuals with disabilities” and described “such forms of discrimination” as “a
serious and pervasive social problem.” It directed DOJ to issue Title II regulations “consistent with” the
1978 HEW Section 504 coordination regulations. Since 1991, DOJ’s Title II regulations have therefore
paralleled the Section 504 coordination regulations, requiring state and local governments to “administer
services, programs, and activities in the most integrated setting appropriate to the needs of qualified
individuals with disabilities.” Under the regulations, the “most integrated setting” is the “setting that
enables individuals with disabilities to interact with nondisabled persons to the fullest extent possible.” In
1991, DOJ stated: “Integration is fundamental to the purposes of the [ADA].”

In 2011, DOJ published guidance on the integration mandate. That guidance affirmed DOJ’s perspective
at the time that the ADA required covered entities to “provide individuals with disabilities opportunities to
live, work, and receive services in the greater community” and that people “at serious risk of
institutionalization or segregation” could bring ADA claims. The guidance rejected the notion that costs to
state programs could, on their own, justify serving people with disabilities in more segregated settings.

The Supreme Court and federal agencies have also interpreted Section 504 and Title II to require covered
entities to make reasonable modifications to allow people with disabilities to access their programs and
services. In Alexander v. Choate, a Section 504 case, the Court held that “while a grantee need not be
required to make ‘fundamental’ or ‘substantial’ modifications to accommodate the handicapped, it may be
required to make ‘reasonable’ ones.” Both Title II’s statutory text and its regulations require reasonable
modifications. Like Section 504, Title II does not require covered entities to make changes that “would
fundamentally alter the nature of the service, program, or activity.”

Olmstead v. L.C. ex rel. Zimring

In Olmstead, two women with intellectual disabilities and serious mental illnesses sued Georgia health
officials for allegedly keeping them institutionalized for years after their physicians agreed that they could
be served in community-based programs. The plaintiffs argued that Georgia violated the Title II
integration mandate by failing to place them in such programs. Georgia countered that the plaintiffs had
not shown disability discrimination because they could not identify a group of similarly situated
nondisabled people who were treated better. Georgia contended that it was keeping plaintiffs out of
community-based services not because of their disabilities but because of costs, and requiring the state to
prioritize particular services for these two individuals would fundamentally alter its health program by
forcing it to divert funds from serving other people with medical needs.

Majority Opinion

A splintered Supreme Court largely sided with the plaintiffs. In the majority opinion authored by Justice
Ginsburg, the Court held that “unjustified institutional isolation of persons with disabilities is a form of
discrimination” prohibited by Title II. The Court relied both on Congress’s findings in the ADA, which
“explicitly identified unjustified ‘segregation’ of persons with disabilities as a ‘for[m] of discrimination,’”
and DOJ’s Title II regulations requiring integrated services. The Court reasoned that unjustified
institutionalization was a form of discrimination for at least two reasons: First, it “perpetuates”
stereotypes that people with disabilities “are incapable or unworthy of participating in community life.”
Second, it results in disparate treatment of people with disabilities because it requires them to “relinquish
participation in community life they could enjoy given reasonable accommodations, while persons
without mental disabilities can receive the medical services they need without similar sacrifice.”

A majority of the Court also agreed that a state could defer to the “reasonable assessments of its own
professionals” as to appropriate medical placements and that a state did not need to put unwilling patients
in community-based services. In other words, to be eligible to transfer to a more integrated setting, a
person with a disability would need to consent and obtain a doctor’s approval.

How much Olmstead’s holding depended on DOJ’s Title II regulations is not clear. One could read the
opinion as an interpretation of DOJ’s regulations. The Court characterized the dispute between the parties,
at least in part, as one over “the proper construction and enforcement of the regulations.” The Court
expressly declined to consider whether the regulations validly construed Title II, and it therefore may
have based its conclusion on the assumption that they did. On the other hand, the Court also characterized
the case as “concern[ing] the proper construction” of Title II itself, not just the regulations, and it
appeared to treat the regulations as one of several sources of support for its holding. It declined to decide
whether it should defer to DOJ’s regulations under the then-prevailing Chevron doctrine, holding only
that DOJ’s “views warrant respect.” The Court also looked to congressional findings and its own
reasoning about what discrimination means, in addition to its references to the Title II regulations.

Plurality Opinion

As discussed above, a majority of the Court in Olmstead agreed that unjustified institutionalization can be
disability discrimination and that states need not provide community-based services against a doctor’s
advice or a patient’s wishes. The Court did not reach a majority to further explain when public entities
must provide community-based services. A four-member plurality viewed the matter as a question of what
reasonable modifications states would need to make to provide community-based care to participants in
state medical programs. The plurality sought to balance the needs of people in institutions seeking more
integrated settings with “the responsibility the State has undertaken for the care and treatment of a large
and diverse population of persons with mental disabilities.” In addition to a doctor’s approval and

patient’s consent, the plurality thought that a state would have to provide community-based services when
“the placement can be reasonably accommodated, taking into account the resources available to the State
and the needs of others with mental disabilities.” Generally, the plurality thought that a state could meet
its obligations by showing that it had a “working plan for placing qualified persons with mental
disabilities in less restrictive settings, and a waiting list that moved at a reasonable pace not controlled by
the State’s endeavors to keep its institutions fully populated.”

Justice Kennedy’s Concurrence

Justice Kennedy concurred in the judgment. While he did not join the majority opinion, he agreed that
institutionalization could constitute disability discrimination. Justice Kennedy proposed, however, that
plaintiffs pleading a Title II claim based on unjustified institutionalization would have to show that the
state served another group of people with “medical problems of comparable seriousness” in the
community but denied community-based services to the plaintiffs because of their disabilities. Justice
Kennedy emphasized his concerns that courts respect doctors’ treatment recommendations, that federal
courts not overly intrude into state health policy decisions (including by managing trade-offs between
competing interests), and that people with disabilities not be forced out of institutions without proper
treatment or placements available in the community.

Dissent

Justice Thomas, joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justice Scalia, dissented. The dissent would have
found discrimination only if the state treated a person in a protected group—in this case, people with
disabilities—differently than people outside that group. Because the plaintiffs in Olmstead sought services
given only to people with disabilities, in the dissent’s view they could not identify anyone outside of their
protected class who was treated better. The dissent saw the plaintiff’s claim as one for a certain “standard
of care,” rather than nondiscriminatory treatment. The dissent also echoed Justice Kennedy’s concerns
about the “federalism costs” of “directing States how to make decisions about their delivery of public
services” and about undue court interference in state decisions about the allocation of “limited resources.”

DOJ’s Reinterpretation of Olmstead

The 2026 OLC Opinion significantly changes DOJ’s interpretation of Olmstead, Section 504, and Title II.
As described above, since 1981, DOJ has consistently interpreted Section 504 and Title II to require
covered entities to maximize integration when serving people with disabilities. The OLC Opinion
concludes that federal agencies “incorrectly interpret [the laws] to impose an integration mandate.” OLC
acknowledges that most circuit courts treat the Olmstead plurality’s framework “as binding.” OLC,
however, emphasizes what it describes as the majority’s “narrow” holding: that Title II prohibits only
“unjustified institutional isolation.” Further, OLC interprets the majority to say “remarkably little” about
when institutionalization is “unjustified.” OLC interprets Section 504, Title II, and Olmstead to prohibit
institutionalization “only when there are no legitimate, nondiscriminatory factors that weigh in favor of
it.” In OLC’s view, legitimate justifications for institutionalization include anything other than the “mere
fact of disability” itself. OLC elaborates that this may “include concerns about the safety of the individual
or the community,” such as that disabled individuals will not take “necessary medication”; “[r]esource
and space constraints in community-based treatment facilities”; or any “special needs resulting from” a
person’s disability.

In many ways, the OLC Opinion echoes the concerns of Justice Kennedy’s concurrence and the Olmstead
dissent. OLC opines that requiring states to provide medical services in integrated settings would be
constitutionally suspect for a number of reasons, including that it would “upset the usual constitutional

balance of federal and state powers.” It contends that the fundamental meaning of discrimination is to
treat similarly situated people differently and that institutionalization is therefore discriminatory only
when a covered entity makes “arbitrary distinctions between individuals with similar medical needs.”
Beyond some of the reasoning supported by the concurring and dissenting Justices in Olmstead, OLC
doubts whether Congress has the constitutional authority to regulate where states provide medical
services outside of attaching conditions to federal funds, and it does not think that Section 504 speaks
clearly enough to the integration mandate to give recipients the necessary “clear notice” of what is
required.

On July 20, 2026, DOJ published a Federal Register notice to “clarify” that certain DOJ Olmstead
guidance, including the 2011 guidance, is “not enforceable” and that DOJ would not rely on it. According
to the notice, DOJ intends to “revisit” whether its Olmstead guidance is “consistent with the ‘single, best
meaning’” of the ADA.

Recent DOJ Activities Related to Olmstead Enforcement

DOJ and HHS have cited the OLC Opinion and July 20, 2026, notice to explain changes in their litigating
positions in ongoing Olmstead matters. In one such case, Texas v. Kennedy, HHS faced a suit from several
states challenging its Section 504 regulations implementing the integration mandate. The states claimed
that HHS had exceeded its statutory authority by forcing states to “redesign their entire systems of care”
to provide HHS’s preferred services, rather than requiring only that states not discriminate in the services
they did provide. In addition, the states claimed HHS violated Spending Clause requirements by attaching
“new conditions” to federal funds that were coercive and not clearly statutorily mandated.

Citing the OLC Opinion, HHS and the plaintiffs in Texas v. Kennedy agreed to settle the case by jointly
asking the court to vacate HHS’s integration-related Section 504 regulations. On September 23, 2026, the
court obliged. The court’s order vacates the challenged regulations nationwide. The court observed that
DOJ takes the position that courts cannot vacate rules and that any vacatur “must at least be party-
specific” but stated that DOJ agreed not to “pursue [that] position . . . in this case.”

DOJ cited its July 20, 2026, notice to explain its request to the Eleventh Circuit to vacate a recent decision
in DOJ’s favor in another case, United States v. Florida, which DOJ had been litigating since 2013.
United States v. Florida involved claims that Florida administered its Medicaid program in ways that put
children with “complex medical needs” in nursing homes or “at serious risk” of institutionalization. The
suit addressed “approximately 140 Institutionalized Children” and “more than 1,800” children at risk of
institutionalization. Ultimately, DOJ prevailed on a number of significant legal issues, among them that
people “at risk of institutionalization” could bring claims under Title II. In March 2026, the Eleventh
Circuit largely upheld an injunction requiring Florida to make changes to its Medicaid administration.

In its motion to vacate, DOJ cited the July 20, 2026, notice and asked the appellate court to withdraw its
decision. It stated that DOJ had “decided to ‘revisit’” the position that people at risk of institutionalization
could pursue Title II claims and that “the United States no longer seeks or wishes to enforce liability in
this case based on the at-risk theory.” It further stated that it no longer seeks to “uphold or enforce” the
lower court’s injunction. In response, the Eleventh Circuit referred the case to mediation and invited
“counsel representing the interests of at-risk children” to join.

In addition to changing its positions in Texas v. Kennedy and United States v. Florida, DOJ has recently
withdrawn from or terminated some additional Olmstead matters. For example, on August 6, 2026, DOJ
withdrew as plaintiff-intervenor and stipulated to dismissal of its claims in Steward v. Abbott. In 2025, the
district court in that case held that Texas violated the ADA and Section 504 by failing to provide certain
services to nursing home residents with intellectual disabilities, leaving many “languishing in nursing
facilities for years when they could have been living in a community setting.” In another example, on

Implications of DOJ’s Recent Approach to Olmstead and
Considerations for Congress

Both Title II and Section 504 may be enforced via a private right of action, and neither private plaintiffs
nor courts are bound by DOJ’s interpretation of Olmstead. Private plaintiffs may still seek to enforce an
interpretation of Olmstead that requires greater access to community care, and they may seek judicial
relief for people with disabilities at risk of institutionalization.

That said, Congress envisioned that the federal government would “play[] a central role in enforcing” the
ADA, and DOJ is a major player in Olmstead matters. Olmstead matters can be “highly resource- and
personnel-intensive.” As in United States v. Florida, they can span years (sometimes decades), require
expertise in law, health care policy and administration, medicine, and other fields, and involve dozens to
thousands of plaintiffs or class members. DOJ has at times taken a “sweeping” “enforcement approach,”
addressing “all of a state’s institutions” rather than individual facilities. Private litigants may lack the
resources or expertise to pursue Olmstead cases at the scale at which DOJ is capable. Resource
constraints aside, as commentators observe, privately litigated Olmstead matters often proceed as class
actions, and class certification presents significant challenges that DOJ does not face.

DOJ’s position has directly affected individual cases and could lead to more changes in ongoing Olmstead
matters. The OLC Opinion states that DOJ “has successfully elicited consent decrees, remedial orders, or
out-of-court agreements in nearly a dozen states.” As seen in the cases described above, DOJ may seek to
terminate or modify other injunctions, orders, or consent decrees. DOJ’s position may enable states to
exercise more flexibility in how they administer their health care programs and to allocate resources
differently than they would under a more aggressive interpretation of the ADA and Section 504.

While Olmstead and the OLC Opinion focus on the integration mandate in health care settings, they each
have broader implications. Olmstead’s reach stretches into other areas. DOJ has pursued Olmstead cases
involving segregated employment and segregated educational services for people with disabilities, for
example. Policies in these domains may be affected by the OLC Opinion. The OLC Opinion may also
signal a narrower approach to Title II enforcement beyond matters involving segregation. The OLC
Opinion questions Congress’s Commerce Clause authority to regulate state activity that DOJ views as
noneconomic, as well as Congress’s Fourteenth Amendment authority to address disability discrimination
by states except where such discrimination is irrational. One possible conclusion of this reasoning may be
that Congress cannot use these constitutional authorities, which underpin Title II, to compel states to
reasonably accommodate people with disabilities in all state services. Whether DOJ will extend its
reasoning so far, and change its enforcement practices in other ADA cases, remains to be seen.

Olmstead is a statutory opinion. Congress can clarify its position on the integration mandate. If Congress
agrees with DOJ’s approach and seeks to rein in private litigation, for example, it can amend Title II and
Section 504 to align with the OLC Opinion. Congress can also consider codifying the Olmstead plurality
or other approaches to a broader integration mandate. While DOJ posits certain constitutional limits on
Congress’s power to require states to provide integrated services, those views have not been tested at the
Supreme Court. Moreover, DOJ agrees that Congress could require integration through conditions on
federal funds, so long as those conditions are clear and the law satisfies other constitutional constraints.

All Justices in Olmstead recognized that states have competing interests when serving people with
disabilities and that states may lack the resources to both maintain institutions for those who need them
