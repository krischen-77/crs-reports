Reserve Component Personnel Issues: Questions and Answers


Summary

Congress has historically shown interest in various reserve component (RC) personnel issues,
such as pay and benefits, training and readiness, and career management. The term reserve
component refers collectively to the seven individual RCs of the Armed Forces: the Army
National Guard of the United States, the Army Reserve, the Navy Reserve, the Marine Corps
Reserve, the Air National Guard of the United States, the Air Force Reserve, and the Coast Guard
Reserve. The purpose of these seven reserve components, as codified at Title 10, Section 10102,
of the U.S. Code, is to “provide trained units and qualified persons available for active duty in the
armed forces, in time of war or national emergency, and at such other times as the national
security may require, to fill the needs of the armed forces whenever more units and persons are
needed than are in the regular components.” The Army National Guard and the Air National
Guard also have a state role: When operating under the control of their governors, they respond to
various domestic emergencies, such as disasters and civil disorders.

This report addresses how RC personnel are organized, compensated, and mobilized. It concludes
with a discussion of potential issues for Congress. This report covers the military services
exclusively and generally does not discuss the Coast Guard Reserve.

1. What Is the Reserve Component? What Is
Its Purpose?

The Armed Forces of the United States consist of six services:

    1.  The Army, under the Department of the Army
    2.  The Navy under the Department of the Navy
    3.  The Marine Corps under the Department of the Navy
    4.  The Air Force, under the Department of the Air Force
    5.  The Space Force, under the Department of the Air Force

    2.  the Army Reserve,
    3.  the Navy Reserve,
    4.  the Marine Corps Reserve,
    5.  the Air National Guard of the United States,
    6.  the Air Force Reserve, and
    7.  the Coast Guard Reserve.3

Ready Reserve

The Ready Reserve is the primary personnel pool of the RCs. Members of the Ready Reserve are
typically called to active duty before members of the Standby Reserve15 or the Retired Reserve.
The Ready Reserve comprises the Selected Reserve, the Individual Ready Reserve, and the
Inactive National Guard, each of which is described below.

Selected Reserve

The Selected Reserve contains those units and individuals within the Ready Reserve designated
as “so essential to initial wartime missions that they have priority over all other Reserves.”16
Members of the Selected Reserve are generally required to perform one weekend of training each
month (inactive duty for training or IDT, also known colloquially as weekend drill) and two
weeks of training each year (annual training or AT) for which they receive pay and benefits.17
Some members of the Selected Reserve may be required only to complete their IDT and AT,
while others may perform additional periods of service.

The IRR is primarily composed of individuals who have completed training or some period of
service in the AC or Selected Reserve and “have some period of their military service obligation
(MSO) or other contractual obligation remaining.”18 By law, these personnel may be required to

Inactive National Guard

The ING is, for practical purposes, the National Guard equivalent to the IRR. It comprises those
members of the Army National Guard who are in an inactive status. The Air National Guard does
not maintain an ING.20 Members do not participate in training as do members of the Selected
Reserve. However, ING members are attached to specific National Guard units and “must muster
once a year with their assigned unit, but they do not participate in training activities.”21 Members
of the ING can be involuntarily ordered to active duty if their units are activated under Partial
Mobilization or Full Mobilization.22

Standby Reserve

The Standby Reserve comprises those individuals who maintain a military affiliation but are
generally not required to participate in military training. These individuals may transfer from the
Ready Reserve to the Standby Reserve due to temporary disability or hardship, to ensure
continuity in key federal or private civilian jobs, or for other administrative reasons.23 Members
of the Standby Reserve are subject to involuntary activation only in the case of Full
Mobilization.24

Retired Reserve

The Retired Reserve includes (1) reserve personnel who are receiving retired pay as a result of
their reserve and/or active service and (2) reserve personnel who transfer into the Retired Reserve
after qualifying for reserve retirement but before becoming eligible to receive retired pay (which
typically occurs at age 60).25 Members of the Retired Reserve may be involuntarily ordered to
active duty in the event of a Full Mobilization, and some members of the Retired Reserve may be
ordered to active duty in the event of a recall of retirees.26 Active component personnel who are
receiving retired pay are not included in the Retired Reserve.27

5. Does the Space Force Have a Reserve Component?

The Space Force does not have an RC. Current law requires that full-time and part-time Space
Force personnel be managed as part of a single component.28 By contrast, the other services
generally manage full-time and part-time personnel in separate components.

Current statute authorizes a category of Space Force personnel to serve in an “active status” but
not on “sustained duty.” These personnel may serve in a part-time capacity and may be required
to drill and train periodically, in a manner analogous to members of the Selected Reserve.29 The
Secretary of the Air Force may also “enter into a written agreement with a member of the Space
Force not on sustained duty” that “shall specify the conditions of service.”30 This statutory
language may allow for individual Space Force members (known as Guardians) to transfer
between full-time and part-time duty over the course of their careers. It may also allow the Space
Force to rapidly adjust its force structure.

The Secretary of the Air Force may also make service agreements with Guardians serving in
“inactive status.”31 Such arrangements may create a category of Guardians analogous to the IRR
(see “Individual Ready Reserve”).

6. What Does “Full-Time Support” Mean? What Are
the Different Categories of Full-Time Support for
the Reserve Components?

Reserve units are primarily filled by traditional reservists: members of the Selected Reserve who
are usually required to serve one weekend a month and two weeks a year.32 However, some
reserve units are also staffed by one or more full-time civilian and/or military employees. These
employees, known as full-time support (FTS) personnel, are “assigned to organize; administer;
instruct; recruit and train; maintain supplies, equipment and aircraft; and perform other functions
required on a daily basis in the execution of operational missions and readiness preparations” as
authorized in Title 5, Title 10, and Title 32.33

DOD regulations identify five categories of FTS personnel: active guard and reserve (AGR),
military technicians (MTs), AC, Navy full-time support, and civilians.34 The distinctions among
each of these categories are outlined below and in Table 5. The mix of FTS personnel in each of
the RCs is intended “to optimize consistency and stability for each RC to achieve its assigned
missions.”35

Active Guard and Reserve

AGR personnel are members of an RC who are placed on active duty or full-time National Guard
duty orders for a period of 180 consecutive days or more for the purposes of “organizing,
administering, recruiting, instructing, or training the reserve components.”36 They may also
perform certain operational support duties (such as support for emergency preparedness
programs) and may provide training to AC personnel, DOD contractors, DOD civilians, and
foreign military personnel.37 Although they are serving full-time, AGR personnel are still
considered members of the Selected Reserve.38

Military Technicians

MTs, referred to in statute as “military technicians (dual status),” are federal civilian employees
who provide support to reserve units either in the “organizing, administering, instructing or
training of the Selected Reserve” or “in the maintenance and repair of supplies or equipment.”39
Some MTs may also perform certain operational support duties and provide training to AC
personnel, DOD contractors, DOD civilians, and foreign military personnel.40

MTs are generally required to maintain membership in the Selected Reserve as a condition of
their employment.41 They are required to attend IDT and AT with their reserve units.42 MTs can be
involuntarily ordered to active duty in the same way as other members of the Selected Reserve.

RC, AC personnel may mobilize as part of the reserve units to which they are assigned.44

Navy Full-Time Support

The Navy Reserve employs a cadre of FTS personnel who are members of the Navy Reserve but
typically serve full-time in a manner analogous to their AC FTS counterparts. These personnel are
comparable to AGR but are identified as a separate category in DOD regulations.45 Navy
personnel and administrative regulations sometimes refer to sailors in this program as “TARs”
(Training and Administration of the Reserves).46 These personnel incur an eight-year obligation,
of which 48 months are “normally” served on active duty, and are administered as full-time
active-duty personnel.47 Their duties involve “organizing, administering, recruiting, instructing, or
training” the reserves.48

Civilians

Civilians are federal civil service employees who “provide administration, training, maintenance,
and recruiting support to the RCs.” They are not required to hold membership in the Selected
Reserve as a condition of their employment.49

Basic Pay

Reservists activated for full-time duty under Title 10 or Title 32 generally receive one day of
basic pay for each day of duty in a manner analogous to their AC counterparts. During their AT—
a period of sustained training typically lasting two weeks—reservists are also usually paid for
each day of duty performed.

The pay policy for reservists’ IDT is more complex. When performing IDT, reservists receive the
equivalent of one day of basic pay for each unit training assembly (UTA) they attend. A UTA is
generally a four-hour period of instruction, and there are typically four UTAs in a weekend.53
Reservists would therefore receive four days’ basic pay for two calendar days of IDT. During a
typical year, a reservist who worked 38 days (14 days of AT and 24 calendar days of IDT) would
receive the equivalent of 62 days of basic pay (14 days of pay for AT and 48 days of pay for IDT).

Special and Incentive Pays

Military personnel may be eligible for special and incentive pays for performing certain types of
hazardous or arduous duty, for serving in certain assignments, or for possessing certain skills.54
Reserve personnel are generally eligible for these pays when performing qualifying duty,
including AT.55 They may receive a pro-rated portion of the full monthly amount corresponding to
the number of days served. Reservists may also be eligible for special and incentive pays during
IDT and typically receive such compensation at a rate proportional to the amount of inactive duty
compensation they receive (i.e., one-thirtieth of the monthly rate for each UTA).56

Allowances

Military personnel may be eligible for a housing allowance, called Basic Allowance for Housing.
During AT, but not during IDT, reservists may be eligible for a housing allowance known as Basic
Allowance for Housing-Reserve Component/Transit and for a subsistence allowance known as
Basic Allowance for Subsistence.57 Reserve officers are also entitled to a $400 clothing allowance
at the beginning of their reserve service to assist them in purchasing necessary uniform items.58
Furthermore, if they are called to active duty for more than 90 days, they are usually entitled to an
additional $200 clothing allowance.59 Reserve enlisted personnel are typically issued all their
uniforms, shoes, boots, and insignia and therefore do not receive a clothing allowance; however,
they may be eligible for a clothing allowance if required uniform items are not provided to
them.60

Retirement
Reservists become eligible for retirement after 20 years of qualifying service.71 A year of
qualifying service is defined as a year in which a reservist has earned at least 50 retirement points,
the sum of which is used to calculate retired pay (see below).72 A reservist earns 15 retirement
points per year for being a member of the Ready Reserve, one point for each UTA, one point for
each day of AT, and one point for each day of active duty.73 Points can also be earned for
completing certain military courses and for performing funeral honors duty.74

Earning 50 points in a given year is generally feasible for members of the Selected Reserve,
because attending a full year of IDT and two weeks of AT generates 77 retirement points.75
Excluding points earned while in an active duty status (which includes AT), reservists may not
earn more than 130 points per year.76 Additionally, including points earned while in an active duty
status, reservists may not earn more than 365 points in a year (366 in a leap year).77
After completing 20 years of qualifying service, a reservist may apply for retirement.78 Upon
reaching the retired pay eligibility age—which typically ranges between 50 and 60 depending on
how many days of certain types of duty the reservist performed during his or her career79—the
retired reservist is eligible to receive retired pay.

Retired pay is calculated by totaling all the points earned during all the years of service, then
dividing this sum by 360. This calculation produces the number of equivalent years of active-duty
service the reservist has performed. The number of equivalent years is then multiplied by 2.5% or
2.0% (depending on the date of entry to military service) to determine the retirement benefit
multiplier.80 This multiplier is then applied to an amount based on the highest of the last three
years of paid service, sometimes called the high-three average.81

Potential Issues for Congress

Do the personnel policies of the Reserve Component support the missions of the
Reserve Components?

Congress may consider how it expects the National Guard and reserves to be employed and if the
force structure and personnel policies of the reserves support Congress’s intent.

Congress could evaluate the balance between the RC’s strategic and operational roles. In its
strategic role, the RC provides additional forces for mid- to large-scale contingency operations or
other unanticipated national crises. In its operational role, the RC participates regularly in force
rotations and military operations.
DOD policy indicates that the reserves maintain both operational and strategic roles.108 Congress
may consider the appropriate balance between these mission sets. It may further consider whether
the force structure and personnel policies of the RC supports the missions Congress intends the
RC to perform.

Do the personnel policies of the Reserve Component support Congress’s military
end-strength goals?

Congress sets the authorized number of military personnel (also called the end-strength) of the
Armed Forces, historically in an annual National Defense Authorization Act. Congress may
consider whether RC personnel policies help or hinder the services’ efforts to recruit and retain
personnel to meet these end-strength goals.109 Generally, more generous RC pay and benefits may
help the reserves attract recruits and retain personnel to meet end-strength goals. Generous RC
benefits may also cause prospective recruits to choose the reserves over the AC—or AC personnel
to switch to the reserves. This dynamic may hinder the active forces’ efforts to meet their end-
strength goals. Congress may consider the appropriate balance of AC and RC benefits with the
structure of the total force in mind.

Do the personnel policies of the Reserve Component support a cost-effective
military force?

Congress may consider whether RC personnel policies are cost-effective, by evaluating the
absolute cost of employing reserve personnel, or comparing the costs of employing active and
reserve personnel.

Should Congress choose to reduce the costs of employing reserve personnel, it may adjust RC
pay and benefits to achieve this goal. Congress may also adjust the AC/RC force mix for a range
of reasons, including cost savings.110
