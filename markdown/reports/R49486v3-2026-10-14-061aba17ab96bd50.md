WOTUS. Although Section 401 water quality certifications are not permits, they are a type of approval required for some
projects to move forward and have implications for issuance of some federal permits and licenses. Examples of activities that
may require a CWA Section 401 water quality certification include activities requiring a USACE-issued CWA Section 404
permit, projects requiring USACE Rivers and Harbors Act permits, hydropower projects licensed by the Federal Energy
Regulatory Commission (FERC), and certificates of public convenience and necessity issued by FERC.

The second Trump Administration has issued proposed regulations that, if finalized, would affect some aspects of CWA
permitting and 401 certifications. EPA and USACE have issued a proposed rule and a proposed supplemental rule to update
the definition of WOTUS. If finalized, the rule would likely narrow the scope of waters and wetlands considered WOTUS,
and therefore subject to the CWA’s permitting programs and water quality certification requirements. EPA has also issued a
proposed rule to update the Section 401 implementing regulations, which, if finalized, would likely narrow the scope of what
states may consider in issuing water quality certifications, among other changes. These changes could have implications for
the issuance of certain federal licenses and permits, including Section 404 permits.

Many Members have shown interest in policy issues pertaining to CWA permitting—especially permitting reform—in recent
Congresses. Congressional committees have held hearings that have included discussions of CWA permitting, and some
Members have introduced legislation that either focuses specifically on CWA permitting or would amend CWA permitting
within broader permitting reform bills. Of the over 50 bills introduced during the 119th Congress that propose to address some
aspect of CWA permitting, more than half include changes to NPDES permitting, more than half include changes to Section
404 permitting, and over a quarter would make changes to Section 401 certification. A few of these bills propose changes to
the scope of WOTUS.

Congress’s interest in CWA permitting, as demonstrated through hearings and legislative efforts, can be grouped under a few
broader policy areas: the balance of federal versus state authority for permitting-related programs and decisions; regulatory
certainty; and timelines for permit issuance, particularly for certain energy, mineral, and other projects identified as being of
national importance. Congress may consider proposing or supporting legislation that addresses these issues, may consider
overseeing the Administration’s ongoing regulatory efforts; may consider oversight of existing permitting processes
generally, or specific to certain industries or project types; or may opt not to act and leave implementation of these issues to
EPA, USACE, and states.

Introduction

The Federal Water Pollution Control Act, commonly referred to as the Clean Water Act (CWA), is
the principal federal law in the United States governing water pollution.1 Congress passed major
amendments to the statute in 1972, which significantly reorganized and expanded it to establish a
new framework for controlling water pollution.2 Among the many revisions, the amendments set
ambitious goals for water quality and established the basic structure for regulating pollutant
discharges into the nation’s surface waters.

The CWA’s objective, as stated in the 1972 amendments, is “to restore and maintain the chemical,
physical, and biological integrity of the Nation’s waters.”3 The CWA also established two goals:
to eliminate the discharge of pollutants into navigable waters by 1985; and as an interim goal,
wherever attainable, to achieve water quality that is “fishable” and “swimmable” by July 1,
1983.4 Although much progress has been made, those goals have not been met fully in many
places. While those dates have long passed, the goals remain, and efforts to attain them continue.

To help achieve the CWA’s objective and goals, the statute prohibits the discharge of pollutants
from any point source (e.g., a discrete conveyance such as a pipe) to waters of the United States
(WOTUS) without a permit.5 The CWA also establishes two permitting programs that may be
used to authorize certain discharges. These are the Section 402 National Pollutant Discharge
Elimination System (NPDES) permitting program and the Section 404 permitting program for
dredged or fill material.6 NPDES permits are issued by 47 states and the U.S. Environmental
Protection Agency (EPA) for discharges of pollutants from point sources, such as municipal and
industrial sources. These sources include wastewater treatment facilities, municipal stormwater
collection systems, and a wide variety of industrial dischargers. Section 404 permits are issued
primarily by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (USACE) and two states (Michigan and New
Jersey) for discharges of dredged or fill material. Activities that may require a Section 404 permit
include pipeline projects, water resource projects, mining projects, transmission line projects, or
other development projects.

In recent Congresses, some Members have shown interest in a wide range of policy issues
pertaining to CWA permitting and reform. One issue of interest has been the definition of waters
of the United States (WOTUS), which determines the scope of waters subject to the CWA’s
permitting provisions. Some Members have also sought to find ways to streamline the CWA
permitting process to help expedite the approval of certain projects—including linear
infrastructure and other projects that they assert are critical to national interests. Other policy
topics related to CWA permitting that some Members have sought to address include permit term
lengths; permit requirements for emerging contaminants such as per- and polyfluoroalkyl
substances (PFAS); permit requirements for aerial discharges including pesticides and fire

under the statute. Broadly, CWA Section 301 specifies that the discharge of any pollutant by any
person is unlawful without a permit.9 CWA Section 502, the statute’s definitions provision,

    •   Pollutant broadly includes “dredged spoil, solid waste, incinerator residue,
       sewage, garbage, sewage sludge, munitions, chemical wastes, biological
        materials, radioactive materials, heat, wrecked or discarded equipment, rock,
        sand, cellar dirt and industrial, municipal, and agricultural waste discharged into
        water.”11

The scope of waters included in the definition of WOTUS has been, and continues to be, a matter
of debate. Because the CWA does not define WOTUS, USACE and EPA—the two agencies that
administer the statute—have defined the term in regulations. For much of the past several
decades, regulations promulgated by USACE and EPA in the 1980s have been in effect. However,
Supreme Court rulings, stakeholder interest, and congressional interest prompted the last three
presidential Administrations (the Obama Administration, the first Trump Administration, and the
Biden Administration) to promulgate revised definitions of WOTUS. Each of these regulatory
efforts prompted reactions from a variety of stakeholders with numerous groups filing lawsuits to
challenge the rules. In addition, in 2023, the Supreme Court issued a decision construing WOTUS
more narrowly than previous regulatory interpretations.22 EPA and USACE have since attempted
to align the regulatory definition of WOTUS with the Court’s ruling.

In March 2025, the Trump Administration announced its intent to revise the definition of
WOTUS again through new rulemaking.23 On November 20, 2025, EPA and USACE published a
proposed rule to revise the definition of WOTUS for public comment.24 On September 9, 2026,
EPA and USACE issued a supplemental proposed rule, which supplements the initial proposed
rule with additional regulatory options on certain aspects of the proposed rule.25 If finalized, the
updated regulations would likely result in a narrower scope for WOTUS compared to the existing
regulations, meaning that fewer waters and wetlands would be subject to CWA permitting
requirements.26

Given that the scope of WOTUS determines which waters are subject to CWA permitting
requirements, the definition continues to be a key policy issue in the CWA permitting space. For
more information on this issue, see CRS Report R47408, Waters of the United States (WOTUS):
Frequently Asked Questions About the Scope of the Clean Water Act, by Kate R. Bowers and
Laura Gatz.

Who Needs an NPDES Permit?

Any person who discharges a pollutant from a point source to WOTUS needs an NPDES permit
unless the activity is specifically exempt under Section 402.28 This includes a wide range of
potential dischargers—typically the owners or operators of a facility or activity subject to
regulation under the NPDES program.29 These facilities/activities include publicly owned
treatment works (i.e., municipal wastewater treatment facilities), municipal separate storm sewer
collection systems (MS4s), a wide range of industrial facilities that discharge wastewater and/or
stormwater, construction activities that discharge stormwater, certain pesticide applications, and
certain fire-retardant applications.30 Any owner or operator of a point source other than a vessel or
floating craft that discharges a pollutant into the contiguous zone or the ocean also needs an
NPDES permit. The CWA identifies specific activities that do not require NPDES permits in
Section 402(l).31

How Are Permit Limitations Established?

When a state or EPA establishes effluent (i.e., discharge) limitations in a permit, it must consider
limitations based on both the technology available to control the pollutants (i.e., technology-based
effluent limitations) and what is needed to attain or maintain the water quality standards of the
receiving waterbody (i.e., water-quality-based effluent limitations). The permitting authority must
also consider antibacksliding requirements that aim to prohibit the renewal, reissuance, or
modification of an existing NPDES permit that contains effluent limitations, permit conditions, or
standards less stringent than those established in the previous permit.38

Technology-Based Effluent Limitations (TBELs)

TBELs are specific numerical limits (i.e., maximum allowable levels of specific pollutants), or in
some cases, best management practices, that represent the minimum level of control that must be
established in a permit.39 The CWA requires EPA to establish TBELs for various categories of
point sources/dischargers.40 Technology-based requirements consider the performance of specific
technologies as well as economic achievability. These limits do not specify what technologies
must be employed; rather, they establish the levels of specific pollutants that are allowable in the
discharge based on the performance of technologies identified as representing specified levels of
control (e.g., best available technology economically achievable, best conventional pollutant
control technology). The CWA prescribes levels of control for various dischargers, as described
below.

Water-Quality-Based Effluent Limitations

In cases where technology-based effluent limitations are not adequate to meet applicable water
quality standards, permits also incorporate water-quality-based effluent limitations.51 Water-
quality-based effluent limitations are specific limits established in a permit (numeric or narrative)
that, if not exceeded in the discharge, allow for attainment of water quality standards in the
receiving water.52 Water quality standards—established by states, territories, tribes, and EPA—
define the desired condition or level of protection of a water body and what is needed to achieve
or protect that condition.53

A 2025 Supreme Court decision—City and County of San Francisco, California v. EPA—ruled
that the CWA does not authorize EPA (or states) to impose what the Court called “end-result”
provisions in NPDES permits.54 Such “end-result” requirements condition a permittee’s
compliance on the quality of the receiving water rather than on adherence to specific effluent
limitations. Examples include certain narrative conditions added to permits to prohibit the
permittee from creating nuisance conditions or causing or contributing to a violation of water
quality standards in the receiving water body. Prior to the Supreme Court ruling, such provisions
were commonly used by permit writers.55 As a result of the ruling, permit writers will need to
specify specific, measurable discharge limits, or will need to include specific actions a permittee
must take to comply with its permit.

Permitting Time Frames and the NPDES Permit Backlog

EPA recognizes the importance of timely issuance and reissuance of NPDES permits, noting that
timeliness provides greater regulatory certainty to the business community and ensures that
permits improve environmental protection by reflecting the most recent scientific information.64
However, EPA and some states have permitting “backlogs” for both individual and general
permits. EPA notes that it “continuously works to increase efficiencies in permitting processes to
decrease the NPDES permit backlog and encourages authorized NPDES permit programs to do
the same.”65

EPA regulations require any person proposing a new discharge to apply for a permit at least 180
days before the discharge is to commence.66 EPA considers applications for new EPA-issued
permits that are not issued or denied within 365 days of receipt of the application to be
backlogged.67

Permittees that want to renew their permit must submit a complete application for permit renewal
at least 180 days prior to their permit expiration date.68 If the permitting authority receives the
complete application but fails to reissue the permit prior to the expiration date, the permit is
“administratively continued” and operates under the same terms and conditions until the new
permit is issued.69 EPA considers permits that are administratively continued for 180 days or more
to be backlogged.70

EPA tracks the NPDES permit backlog for both EPA-issued and state-issued permits, including
individual and general permits.71 Table 1 provides a summary of the NPDES permit backlog for

date, EPA had a backlog for new individual permits—10 for permits on Tribal land and 14 for
other EPA-issued permits.73 EPA’s NPDES backlog data do not provide information on the
backlog for new state-issued individual permits. Table 2 provides a summary of the NPDES

Enforcement83

The CWA authorizes EPA and delegated states to use a range of enforcement tools—
administrative, civil judicial, and criminal actions—to address noncompliance with NPDES
requirements.84 EPA retains enforcement authority in delegated states, although such states
generally take the lead on enforcement.85 EPA and states will sometimes undertake joint
enforcement proceedings where violations of both federal and state law are alleged. EPA and
states may seek voluntary compliance with violations or may use a range of tools authorized
under CWA Sections 309 and 402. The CWA authorizes EPA and states to issue administrative
compliance orders (e.g., to correct violations, comply with permit conditions or limitations) and
assess administrative penalties.86 The statute also provides judicial enforcement tools. EPA and
states may seek civil judicial enforcement actions and penalties, as well as criminal judicial
enforcement actions (e.g., fines, imprisonment, or both) for knowing or negligent violations.87

Section 404 Dredge and Fill Permits

The other permitting program authorized under the CWA is the CWA Section 404 permitting
program for the discharge of dredged or fill material into WOTUS.88 Dredged material includes
material dredged or excavated from waterbodies, such as coastal waters, lakes, rivers, and stream
beds.89 These materials can contain pollutants that may affect water quality when discharged into
receiving waters or wetlands. Regardless of the presence of pollutants in the dredged material,
when it is used to fill wetlands or other receiving waters for purposes of development, the
discharge can negatively affect aquatic ecosystems and the functionality of wetlands, among other
outcomes. Fill material—including rock, sand, soil, construction debris—may have similar
consequences when used to fill wetlands or other receiving waters.90

Types of Section 404 Permits, Term Lengths, and Permit Processes

Similar to NPDES permits, there are two main types of Section 404 permits: individual permits
and general permits. However, in contrast to NPDES permits, there are multiple types of
individual and general permits under Section 404, which have different permit terms. Section 404
establishes requirements for some aspects of the permits, and USACE and EPA have promulgated
regulations to implement these requirements.105 States with delegated authority also have their
own state laws and regulations that apply to the permits they issue.106

Individual Permits

USACE or states issue individual permits for individual projects or applicants, typically when the
project’s discharge may cause more than minimal individual or cumulative impacts.107 There are
two types of individual permits: standard individual permits and letters of permission (LOPs).
The term lengths for individual permits issued by USACE vary based on the project. If the
discharge is of a more permanent nature, the permit may include timelines for completing the
work or activity or may specify a date by which the work must be started.108 If the discharge is of
a temporary nature, the permit would include an expiration date.109 The CWA limits permit terms
for state-issued individual permits to five years.110

Standard individual permits authorize discharges following a comprehensive case-specific review,
including a public notice period, public interest review, an option for a public hearing, activity-
specific environmental documentation, and a 404(b)(1) guidelines analysis.111 See Figure 3 for a
general overview of the procedure USACE uses to evaluate a standard individual permit

General Permits

USACE (and authorized states) may issue general permits for proposed activities that are similar
in nature and will result in only minimal individual and cumulative adverse environmental

terms to no more than five years.124

General permits are a key means by which USACE streamlines the requirements of its permitting
process: they essentially preauthorize categories of activities USACE has identified as being
similar in nature and causing only minimal environmental impacts. The issuance process for a
general permit closely parallels the issuance process for individual permits, with a public notice,
opportunity for a public hearing, and detailed decision documentation.125 Once a general permit is
issued, activities that qualify for the general permit may proceed, provided the terms and
conditions of the general permit are met, without the project proponent having to obtain an
individual, site-specific permit.

a preconstruction notification, or PCN), USACE review of the proposed work, and USACE’s
written verification of compliance with the general permit before the project can commence.126
However, in some cases, activities that qualify for a general permit can proceed without

permit.127

Regional General Permits

USACE district offices may issue regional general permits (RGPs) for their geographic area of
regulatory jurisdiction. RGPs authorize specific activities within WOTUS that are typically
common for that geographic area, are more minor in scope, and result in no more than minimal
individual and cumulative impacts to WOTUS.134

Monitoring, Reporting, and Inspections

CWA Section 404 permits may include special conditions when necessary to satisfy legal
requirements or ensure compliance with permit terms and conditions.140 Such conditions may
include monitoring, reporting, and inspection requirements. The specific monitoring that may be
required depends upon the site-specific activity, but may include, for example, monitoring of
wetlands, streams, or other aquatic resources to assess impacts from the discharge, monitoring to
ensure that any compensatory mitigation measures are meeting performance standards, or water
quality monitoring to assess effects on receiving waters.141 The permit may also require the
permittee to submit periodic reports to USACE, which may detail the project’s progress,
compliance status, or any monitoring results.142 USACE permits also require that the permittee
must allow USACE representatives to inspect the authorized activity at any time to ensure it is in
compliance with the permit terms and conditions.143

Clean Water Act Section 401

Separate from, but related to permitting, CWA Section 401 requires a type of approval, called a
water quality certification, for any federal permit or license that may result in a discharge to
WOTUS.150 A water quality certification, issued by the state (or other certifying authority)151 in
which the discharge originates, attests that the discharge will comply with applicable provisions
of certain enumerated sections of the CWA (301, 302, 303, 306, 307) and may include
consideration of other water quality requirements.152 Examples of activities that may require a
CWA Section 401 water quality certification include activities requiring a USACE-issued CWA
Section 404 permit, projects requiring USACE Rivers and Harbors Act permits, hydropower
projects licensed by the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC), and certificates of
public convenience and necessity issued by FERC.

A certifying authority may grant, grant with conditions, deny, or waive certification of proposed
federal licenses or permits.153 If a certifying authority grants a certification with conditions, those
conditions must be included in the final license or permit.154 If a certifying authority denies
certification, the federal license or permit may not be granted.155 If a certifying authority does not
act on a certification request in a reasonable period of time (which per the statute is not to exceed
one year), the certification is waived.156

WOTUS-Related Legislation
Three of the bills introduced during the 119th Congress (S. 795, H.R. 3898, and S. 5262) would
amend the statutory definition of navigable waters to provide a new definition of the term or
specify and/or clarify certain exclusions from the scope of WOTUS. One bill (S. 5653) would
specify the judicial review provision applicable to regulations defining the scope of WOTUS.

Section 401-Related Legislation
Over a quarter of the bills introduced during the 119th Congress that propose to address some
aspect of CWA permitting would make changes to some aspect of CWA Section 401 and/or the
CWA Section 401 water quality certification process.

Some of these bills would amend Section 401 directly to address policy issues such as the scope
of what states may consider in making certification determinations; the scope of what conditions
they may include when granting certifications with conditions; the enforcement roles of federal
agencies versus states; the timelines associated with certifications; and/or judicial review for 401
certifications (e.g., H.R. 3928, H.R. 3898, S. 4475, S. 4755, S. 4944, H.R. 9855, S. 5653).

Some bills (e.g., H.R. 3668, S. 4300, S. 4944, S.Amdt. 5945) would exempt certain projects (i.e.,
certain pipeline projects requiring authorizations under the Natural Gas Act) from 401
certification requirements. These bills would instead designate the state that would have been the
401-certifying authority as a participating agency in any FERC-led National Environmental
Policy Act review process.

Section 402-Related Legislation
More than half of the bills introduced during the 119th Congress that propose to address some
aspect of CWA permitting would make changes to CWA Section 402 NPDES permitting. Some of
these bills would extend permit term limits from 5 years to 10 years (e.g., H.R. 2093, H.R. 3898,
S. 4944, S.Amdt. 5945, S. 5653). Some would formally authorize the use of general permits
under CWA Section 402 (which EPA and states already use in practice) and/or would specify
requirements for instances when either a general permit expires before a new one is issued or if
EPA decides not to issue a new one (e.g., H.R. 3041, H.R. 3898, H.R. 3899, S. 4944, S.Amdt.
5945).

Several bills would exempt certain activities from Section 402 permitting requirements. Examples
include exemptions for certain pesticide discharges (e.g., H.R. 3824, H.R. 3898); aerial
discharges of products used for fire control and suppression (e.g., H.R. 3300, H.R. 3898, S. 1672,
H.R. 7567); and discharges from portable water treatment and filtration facilities following
certain disaster or emergency declarations (H.R. 4879). One bill would remove an existing
Section 402 permitting exemption for certain discharges of stormwater runoff from oil, gas, and
mining operations (H.R. 6090).161

Policy Issues and Options for Congress

Congress’s interest in CWA permitting, as demonstrated through hearing discussions and
legislative efforts, can be grouped under a few broader policy areas: the balance of federal versus
state authority for permitting-related programs and decisions; regulatory certainty; and timelines
for permit issuance, particularly for certain energy, mineral, and other projects identified as being
of national importance.

Federal Versus State Authority

Cooperative federalism—a model in which federal and state governments collaborate and share
responsibilities in governing—is a core principle of the CWA. Section 101(b) states that “it is the
policy of the Congress to recognize, preserve, and protect the primary responsibilities and rights
of States to prevent, reduce, and eliminate pollution, to plan the development and use (including
restoration, preservation, and enhancement) of land and water resources, and to consult with the
Administrator in the exercise of his authority under this chapter.”163 The statute aims to balance
the goals of preserving states’ sovereignty over their waters with the need for national consistency
in regulations. Section 510 provides that the CWA’s requirements generally set a floor above
which individual states may impose more stringent requirements.164 Stakeholders, as well as some
Members of Congress, have often debated the appropriate balance between federal and state
authority on certain elements of the CWA. Two such elements include the scope of waters subject
to federal jurisdiction under the CWA (i.e., which waters are considered WOTUS) and the scope
of states’ water quality certification authority under Section 401.

groups, agricultural stakeholders, and some states) argue for a narrower definition of WOTUS,
under which a narrower scope of waters would be subject to the CWA’s federal regulations and
permitting programs. Some of these stakeholders point to the CWA Section 101(b) goal and its
aim to ensure that states have primary responsibilities and rights to manage their water
resources.165 Some of these stakeholders also reason that states are able to regulate
nonjurisdictional waters through their own state laws, noting that some states have already done
so.166 Other stakeholders (including environmental groups and some states) and Members of

Regulatory Certainty

Another policy issue central to CWA-permitting reform discussions is regulatory certainty (i.e.,
the aim to establish a more stable and predictable legal and regulatory environment). Some
Members have introduced legislation intended to improve regulatory certainty. Examples include
bills related to permit terms, bills to clarify what it means to be in compliance with a permit, and
bills to establish limits for the time period during which EPA may “veto” a Section 404 permit.

Permit Term Length
Several bills introduced in the 119th Congress include changes to permit term limits. Most permit
terms under the CWA currently have 5-year term limits, as established in statute. Some Members
have introduced bills that would extend some permit term limits to 10-year terms. Some bills
focus on NPDES permit term limits, some focus on certain Section 404 permit term limits, and
some would extend both. Both the House-passed PERMIT Act (H.R. 3898) and BAAJA (S. 5653)
would extend permit term limits for both NPDES and Section 404 permits.

Timing of EPA’s 404(c) “Veto” Authority

As discussed above, EPA has authority, under Section 404(c) to prohibit, deny, restrict, or
withdraw the use of a specified area as a disposal site for dredged or fill material whenever the
Administrator determines that the discharge will have an unacceptable adverse effect on
municipal water supplies, shellfish beds and fishery areas, wildlife, or recreational areas. This
authority is commonly referred to as EPA “veto” authority. To date, EPA has issued final
determinations under its Section 404(c) authority 14 times.

Two of EPA’s most recent uses of Section 404(c) authority drew attention from stakeholders for
the timing of the decisions. In one instance, EPA first initiated the Section 404(c) process for the
Pebble Deposit area in Alaska prior to the permittee submitting a Section 404 permit
application.193 In the other instance, EPA issued its 404(c) “veto” years after USACE issued a
Section 404 permit for the Spruce No. 1 Mine Project in West Virginia.194 These EPA actions

Permit Issuance Timelines

Congress also continues to be interested in CWA permit issuance timelines, particularly for
certain energy, mineral, and other projects identified as being of national importance. Some
Members of Congress and stakeholders continue to debate the balance between streamlining
permitting processes and timelines to facilitate projects of national importance and protecting
water quality and aquatic resources.
Several bills introduced in the 119th Congress aim to shorten permitting timelines or streamline
permit issuance. For example, some bills, including the House-passed PERMIT Act and BAAJA
(S. 5653), would make it easier for certain projects to be eligible for NWPs by, for example,
increasing the threshold for allowable impacts to wetlands or other waters. Other bills would add
permitting exemptions, and still others would add permitting deadlines for certain project types.

Some stakeholders and Members of Congress support efforts to expedite or streamline permitting
processes and assert these changes would help improve efficiency and predictability.197 They
argue that some projects that are critical to national security or economic competitiveness need to
be expedited. Some also argue that long permitting times can lead to delays in project
implementation or decisions to cancel projects.198
