## Summary

The National Park Service (NPS) has a multibillion-dollar backlog of *deferred maintenance*—maintenance that was not performed as scheduled or as needed and was put off to a future time. NPS's deferred maintenance, also known as *deferred maintenance and repairs* or the *maintenance backlog*, was estimated for FY2025 at $24.237 billion. Other federal land management agencies also have deferred maintenance, but NPS's backlog is the largest and has drawn the most congressional attention.

During the past decade (FY2016-FY2025), NPS deferred maintenance grew by $12.906 billion (+114%) in nominal dollars and $7.263 billion (+43%) in inflation-adjusted dollars. Many factors might contribute to the growth or reduction of deferred maintenance, including the aging of NPS assets, availability of funding for maintenance activities, acquisition of new assets, agency management of the backlog, completion of individual projects, changes in construction and related costs, and alterations in the methods used to estimate deferred maintenance. NPS reported that a 60% nominal-dollar increase in the backlog between FY2020 and FY2021 was due in part to changes in how the agency estimates deferred maintenance. NPS's deferred maintenance is distributed unevenly among states and territories, with California, the District of Columbia, Arizona, and Wyoming having the largest amounts. The amounts also vary among individual park units. Among the various types of assets that NPS maintains, deferred maintenance is highest for the agency's paved roads and buildings.

Sources of funding to address NPS deferred maintenance have included discretionary and mandatory appropriations, allocations from the Department of Transportation, park entrance and concessions fees, and donations, among others. Often it is not possible to determine the total amount of funding from these sources that NPS has allocated each year to address deferred maintenance, because NPS does not aggregate these amounts in its budget reporting. In 2020, the Great American Outdoors Act (GAOA; P.L. 116-152) provided a major new source of mandatory funding to address deferred maintenance of NPS and other federal land management agencies. The GAOA's National Parks and Public Land Legacy Restoration Fund (LRF) received $1.9 billion annually for five years (FY2021-FY2025), with 70% of the funds—approximately $1.3 billion annually—going to NPS. In the 119th Congress, H.R. 9250 and S. 1547 would reauthorize deposits to the fund for FY2027-FY2031. For more information, see CRS Insight IN12729, *Legacy Restoration Fund for Deferred Maintenance: 119th Congress Reauthorization Bills*.

Congressional attention in the 118th and 119th Congresses has focused on NPS's use of the LRF funding provided under the GAOA, including the agency's project selection priorities, the funds' impact in addressing the backlog, the reasons for changes in NPS's deferred maintenance estimates, and proposals to reauthorize deposits to the LRF. Separately from the LRF, Congress also has debated the sufficiency of ongoing annual appropriations for NPS deferred maintenance, as well as appropriations for preventive maintenance that could keep assets from deteriorating to the point that deferred maintenance is accrued. Some in Congress also have expressed interest in potential actions to reduce the backlog without additional funding, such as through public-private partnerships or disposal of unneeded assets.

The National Park Service's (NPS's) backlog of *deferred maintenance* (DM)—maintenance that was not performed as scheduled or as needed—is of ongoing interest to Congress. The agency estimated its DM needs for FY2025 at $24.237 billion.[1](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn1) Although other federal land management agencies also have DM backlogs, NPS's backlog is the largest.[2](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn2) Because unmet maintenance needs may damage park resources, adversely affect visitors' enjoyment of the parks, and jeopardize safety, NPS DM has been a topic of concern for Congress and for nonfederal stakeholders. Potential issues for Congress include, among others, how to weigh NPS's maintenance needs against other financial demands within and outside the agency, how to prioritize limited DM funding among multiple parks and types of infrastructure, and how to ensure that NPS is efficiently and successfully managing funds to reduce the backlog. This report provides an overview and discussion of issues related to NPS DM. The report begins with general background and then contains more detailed sections on estimates of NPS DM, agency funding to address DM, and issues under consideration in the 119th Congress.

## General Background

### What Is Deferred Maintenance?

The Federal Accounting Standards Advisory Board (FASAB) defines *deferred maintenance* *and repairs* (DM&R) as "maintenance and repairs that were not performed when they should have been or were scheduled to be and which are put off or delayed for a future period."[3](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn3) Federal agencies and Members of Congress also refer to DM&R as *deferred maintenance* (DM, the term used in this report) or as the *maintenance backlog*.[4](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn4) The term DM encompasses both maintenance and repair needs.

As suggested by the above definition, DM does not include *all* maintenance and repairs, only those that were not accomplished when scheduled or needed and were put off to a future time. Another type of maintenance is *cyclic maintenance*—that is, maintenance performed at regular intervals to prevent asset deterioration, such as to replace a roof or upgrade an electrical system at a scheduled or needed time.[5](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn5) Although NPS considers cyclic maintenance separately from DM for budgeting purposes, NPS has emphasized the importance of cyclic maintenance for preventing accrual of DM.[6](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn6) NPS also performs routine, day-to-day maintenance as part of its *facility operations* activities; such activities include, for example, mowing and weeding landscapes and trails, conducting custodial and janitorial functions, and removing litter.[7](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn7)

### NPS's Deferred Maintenance Backlog

NPS estimated its total DM for FY2025 (the most recent year available) at $24.237 billion ([**Table 1**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#_Ref476756993)).[8](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn8) Although all four major federal land management agencies—NPS, the Bureau of Land Management (BLM), the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS), and the U.S. Forest Service (FS)—have DM backlogs, NPS's backlog is the largest. For FY2025, NPS's DM was more than twice that reported by FS, more than three times that reported by BLM, and nearly seven times that reported by FWS.[9](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn9) One factor in the difference may be that NPS has more infrastructure assets (over 70,000 assets) than the other land management agencies.[10](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn10) DM for the four agencies and agency asset comparisons are discussed further in CRS Report R43997, *Deferred Maintenance of Federal Land Management Agencies: FY2013-FY2022 Estimates and Issues*.

Multiple factors may contribute to growth or reduction of NPS DM. One key driver of growth in NPS maintenance needs has been the increasing age of agency infrastructure. Many NPS assets—such as visitor centers, roads, and other assets—were constructed by the Civilian Conservation Corps in the 1930s or as part of NPS's Mission 66 infrastructure initiative in the 1950s and 1960s.[11](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn11) As these structures have reached or exceeded the end of their anticipated life spans, unfunded costs of repair or replacement have contributed to the DM backlog. Further, observers point out that as time goes by and needed repairs are not made, the rate at which such assets deteriorate accelerates, potentially leading to higher maintenance costs.[12](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn12)

A related key factor is the amount of funding available to NPS to address DM. The sources and amounts of agency funding for DM have fluctuated over time and are discussed in greater detail below, in the section on "[Funding for NPS Deferred Maintenance](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#_Ref157417989)." In addition to funding for DM specifically, funding for routine and cyclic maintenance can affect DM, if a lack of available funds results in scheduled maintenance being deferred.

Some observers have expressed concern that growth in NPS DM may be at least partly due to inefficiencies in the agency's asset management strategies and/or the implementation of these strategies. NPS has taken steps over the past decade to improve its asset management systems and strategies. The Government Accountability Office (GAO) and the Department of the Interior's (DOI's) Office of Inspector General (OIG) have recommended further improvements.[13](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn13) Some aspects of NPS management of the DM backlog are discussed further in the report sections below.

Another issue is that the methods used by NPS to estimate DM have changed over time. NPS's methods are discussed in the section on "[Estimating NPS Deferred Maintenance](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#_Ref157169938)." In particular, NPS attributed a large jump in estimated DM between FY2020 and FY2021 partly to changes in its estimation methods, and additional changes have been implemented in other years. Also, NPS has worked to build and maintain a complete and accurate data set during the past decade.[14](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn14) What portion of the overall change in NPS DM over the decade may be attributable to changes in methodology or data completeness, rather than to other factors, remains unclear.

From year to year, the completion of individual projects, changes in construction and repair costs, and similar factors play a role in the growth or reduction of NPS DM. The DM amounts reported by NPS for each fiscal year represent a snapshot of the backlog on the applicable date. The amount of DM changes frequently as NPS managers and staff complete or cancel maintenance work, recalculate costs, and record new maintenance and repair needs.

The states and territories with the highest NPS DM are not necessarily those with the most park acreage. For example, Alaska contains almost two-thirds of the total acreage in the National Park System but accounts for 1% of the agency's DM backlog. Instead, the amount, type, and condition of infrastructure in the NPS units, along with other factors, combine to determine the NPS DM in each state or territory. For example, paved roads are a major component of NPS DM, and the states and territories with NPS-administered parkways—such as the George Washington Memorial Parkway (Virginia, Maryland, and Washington, DC), the Natchez Trace Parkway (mainly in Mississippi and Tennessee), and the Blue Ridge Parkway (North Carolina and Virginia)—are all among the 20 states and territories with the highest DM.

Various factors may contribute to the relatively high DM estimates for these park units as compared with others. The costs of labor and materials to execute DM projects also may be higher in these urban areas. ## Estimating NPS Deferred Maintenance

NPS estimates the DM needs of park assets through a multi-step process. Park staff record data about the condition of individual assets in a software system, which generates estimates of each asset's maintenance and repair needs.[16](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn16) Over the years, NPS has evolved its methods for estimating DM.[17](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn17) The changes have included, among others, redefining the set of assets for which DM is tracked and changing the inputs and formulas that generate financial estimates of DM needs. NPS has stated that such changes aim to bring estimation methods into line with industry standards and provide "a more comprehensive understanding of facility conditions."[18](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn18) In a September 2023 report, the DOI OIG found that despite the changes, the data in NPS's software system remained "inaccurate and unreliable ... possibly underestimating the figures in some cases and overestimating in others."[19](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn19) NPS has taken several steps in response to the DOI OIG report.[20](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn20)

In recent years, several methodological changes have affected the agency's DM estimates. According to NPS and the DOI OIG, such changes include the following:

* Starting with its DM estimates for FY2021, NPS changed its DM cost estimating formula by adding a "blanket" 35% markup to DM for non-transportation assets.[21](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn21) NPS stated that this markup was equivalent to a markup used by the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) in estimating DM needs for NPS transportation assets (e.g., park roads) and that the markup thus created "consistency" between transportation and non-transportation assets.[22](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn22) Similar to FHWA's markup, NPS's markup is intended to reflect costs of compliance, design, construction management, and project management, which were not accounted for in earlier estimates.[23](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn23)
* Starting with its DM estimates for FY2022, NPS changed its process for calculating park assets' DM needs. NPS had previously calculated DM for its assets based on the estimated costs from open DM work orders at the end of each fiscal year. As of FY2022, NPS shifted to a method based on its visual condition assessments of assets, which are considered in conjunction with the current replacement value for an asset.[24](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn24) NPS stated that this "parametric condition assessment" method is "an accepted industry standard" that will "improve consistency across reporting cycles, and enhance comparability of DM&R [deferred maintenance and repairs] data over time."[25](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn25) As of its FY2027 budget justification, NPS stated that it had completed "the majority of validation & verification" activities associated with the shift to this method.[26](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn26)
* Starting with its DM estimates for FY2023, NPS changed the set of assets for which it tracks and reports DM.[27](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn27) Prior to this time, NPS tracked a set of approximately 75,000 assets to produce its DM estimates.[28](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn28) However, NPS reported on somewhat different groupings of NPS assets to two other entities—the FASAB, for annual DOI agency financial reports, and the General Services Administration (GSA), for its Federal Real Property Profile (FRPP)—owing to specific reporting requirements of these two entities.[29](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn29) Starting with its reporting of FY2023 DM, NPS aligned its base asset portfolio with the portfolio reported to the GSA for the FRPP. As a result, NPS now reports a portfolio of approximately 71,000 assets (rather than the 75,000 assets used earlier).[30](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn30)
* For DM estimates starting in FY2023, NPS incorporated a new "road model update" from FHWA to estimate DM for NPS transportation assets. NPS stated that the new financial model "cumulatively account[s] for general inflation from recent years" and was responsible for a 36% increase in estimated DM for NPS paved roads between FY2022 and FY2023.[31](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn31)

It can be difficult to determine to what extent methodological changes such as these have been responsible for variations in NPS's estimated DM over the years. The DOI OIG found that of the approximately $8.8 billion increase in NPS DM between FY2020 and FY2021, approximately $3.7 billion was attributable to the new 35% markup for non-transportation assets.[32](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn32) NPS stated that other factors in the FY2020-FY2021 increase included insufficient funding for annual maintenance needs (thus creating more DM) and increased construction and maintenance costs due to inflation.[33](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn33)

In other cases, such as with the shift to estimating DM through condition assessments rather than work orders, the effects of the change on DM estimates are unclear. Also unclear are the impacts, if any, of the FY2023 change in the portfolio of assets NPS tracks, as compared with the set of assets tracked in FY2022 and previous years. To the extent that NPS is tracking DM for fewer assets than it previously did, this could lower estimated DM compared with earlier years, although any such effects would be difficult to measure given other factors also affecting DM.

In general, the DOI OIG found that NPS "does not have a monitoring mechanism to ensure the accuracy and completeness of its … data" and that "without reliable data, the NPS cannot make informed decisions on how to manage its deferred maintenance, improve program effectiveness and accountability, and potentially enhance decision making."[34](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn34) However, the OIG noted steps NPS has taken to improve data quality and monitoring, such as directing park staff to rectify data anomalies and to annually review the accuracy of condition assessment scoring.[35](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn35)

## Funding for NPS Deferred Maintenance

NPS has used discretionary and mandatory appropriations, allocations from the Department of Transportation, park entrance and recreation fees, donations, and other funding sources to address the DM backlog. Often it is not possible to determine the total amount of funding from these sources that NPS has allocated each year to address DM, because NPS does not specifically aggregate these amounts in its budget reporting.[36](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn36) From FY2021 to FY2025, NPS received a notable increase in its financial resources to address DM through the National Parks and Public Land Legacy Restoration Fund (Legacy Restoration Fund, or LRF; 54 U.S.C. §§200401-200402), a five-year fund for DM of multiple land management agencies.

### The Legacy Restoration Fund

In 2020, the Great American Outdoors Act (GAOA) established the LRF to address DM of five agencies (NPS, FS, BLM, FWS, and the Bureau of Indian Education).[37](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn37) The fund received annual deposits over five years (FY2021-FY2025) of amounts equal to 50% of all federal energy development revenues—from oil, gas, coal, or renewable energy—that were credited in the preceding fiscal year as miscellaneous receipts to the Treasury, up to a cap of $1.900 billion annually. NPS received a 70% share, with the remainder going to the other agencies at specified percentages. The LRF received the maximum allowable deposit of $1.900 billion each year, with 70% of this total ($1.330 billion annually) going to NPS, for a cumulative total of $6.650 billion for DM over the five-year period. Bills in the 119th Congress would reauthorize deposits to the LRF for FY2027-FY2031 and make other changes; for discussion, see CRS Insight IN12729, *Legacy Restoration Fund for Deferred Maintenance: 119th Congress Reautho**r**ization Bills*.

The GAOA contained provisions for allocating LRF funding to individual DM projects. The GAOA directed the President to submit lists of projects to be addressed with LRF funds in agencies' annual budget submissions to Congress.[38](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn38) Congress, in appropriations acts, could accept the President's proposals or provide alternate allocations (while adhering to the overall funding percentages defined for each agency).[39](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn39) For FY2021-FY2025, appropriators generally, but not always, allocated the NPS funds as proposed by NPS.

The GAOA required that NPS and other agencies use the LRF funding for "priority deferred maintenance projects," and that at least 65% of each agency's monies over the term of the fund be used for non-transportation projects.[40](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn40) DOI and NPS developed additional selection criteria for GAOA projects.[41](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn41) In early 2024, GAO examined the land management agencies' processes for selecting LRF projects and found that the agencies, including NPS, "generally followed leading practices for managing deferred maintenance in their processes for selecting projects for LRF funding."[42](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn42)

### Annual Appropriations for NPS Deferred Maintenance

Outside of the LRF, much of the funding used to address NPS DM has come from annual appropriations, primarily through the agency's Operation of the National Park System (ONPS) and Construction accounts. Because both accounts fund other activities in addition to DM, it is unclear how much funding under these programs has gone directly to address the DM backlog.

Within the ONPS account, annual appropriations reports have identified funding for NPS "maintenance, repair, or rehabilitation projects," and NPS uses these amounts for both DM and other types of maintenance.[43](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn43) NPS has historically used this funding for projects with anticipated costs of less than $2 million each.[44](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn44) For FY2026, Congress provided $157.2 million for maintenance, repair, or rehabilitation projects.[45](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn45)

Within the Construction account, NPS has historically used funding in the Line-Item Construction program for DM projects (among other projects) expected to cost $2 million or more.[46](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn46) For FY2026, Congress did not provide funding for Line-Item Construction projects, except for one project designated as community project funding/congressionally directed spending. The joint explanatory statement for P.L. 119-74 stated that this activity was not funded because NPS did not submit a list of line-item project funding requests.[47](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn47)

Portions of other NPS discretionary budget activities and accountsalso are sometimes used for DM. These include other programs within the ONPS and Construction accounts as well as NPS's Centennial Challenge account (discussed below under "[Other NPS Funding for Deferred Maintenance](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#_Ref167896273)").

Combined discretionary appropriations for maintenance, repair, or rehabilitation projects within the ONPS account and Line-Item Construction within the Construction account—the two primary sources of annual appropriations for NPS DM—declined after the LRF funding became available. Comparing FY2020 combined funding for the two programs (prior to establishment of the LRF) with FY2026 combined funding, the appropriations decreased by 62% in nominal dollars.[48](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn48)

### Transportation Funding from the Highway Trust Fund

Like other federal land management agencies, NPS receives mandatory appropriations from the Highway Trust Fund to address transportation needs, including transportation-related DM.[49](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn49) As with discretionary appropriations, NPS uses these funds for both DM and other work on transportation assets (e.g., preventive maintenance), and does not record the portion of funding that is used specifically to address DM.[50](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn50)

Funds are provided to NPS by FHWA, primarily through the Federal Lands Transportation Program (FLTP), which was most recently reauthorized by the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (IIJA).[51](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn51) Under the IIJA, NPS's annual FLTP allocation grew from $332.4 million in FY2022 to $360.0 million in FY2026. Through related federal highway programs in the IIJA, such as the Federal Lands Access Program and others, NPS could receive additional funding.[52](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn52)

In FY2023 (the most recent year reported), the federal highway programs funded more than half of NPS's spending on transportation maintenance and improvements.[53](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn53) NPS has noted, however, that its transportation projects may often require greater investments than can be supported by the available funding from the FLTP and related programs.[54](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn54) NPS has stated that the cost of major transportation projects can range from $25 million to nearly $1 billion, with the costs of some individual projects exceeding NPS's entire FLTP allocation for a given year.[55](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn55) Mandatory appropriations from the LRF—the GAOA's DM fund—also were available for transportation projects for FY2021-FY2025, although the GAOA funding for transportation projects was capped at no more than 35% of NPS's total LRF allotment over the five-year life of the fund.[56](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn56)

### Park Entrance and Recreation Fees

Park entrance and recreation fees collected under the Federal Lands Recreation Enhancement Act may be used for DM, among other purposes.[57](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn57) The fees are available for use without further appropriation, and most are retained at the collecting parks.[58](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn58) Fee collections may be used for purposes benefiting visitors, including facility maintenance and repair, interpretation and visitor services, law enforcement, and others. NPS policies require parks to obligate at least 55% of the collections to "deferred and preventive facility maintenance projects."[59](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn59) In its FY2027 budget justification, NPS estimated FY2026 entrance and recreation fee collections at $596.0 million.[60](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn60)

### Concessions Franchise Fees

NPS collects concessions franchise fees from park concessioners that provide services such as lodging and dining at park units. The fees, collected under the National Park Service Concessions Management Improvement Act of 1998, are typically set as a percentage of a concessioner's gross receipts.[61](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn61) The fees are available for use without further appropriation and are mainly retained at the collecting parks.[62](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn62) They may be used to reduce DM (including concessions-related DM) and are also used for other NPS concessions-related activities, such as contract development and payment of leasehold surrender interest (an amount owed to concessioners that have invested in improvements to NPS infrastructure).[63](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn63) In its FY2027 budget justification, NPS estimated concessions fee collections of $206.1 million for FY2026.[64](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn64)

### Other NPS Funding for Deferred Maintenance

The National Park Service Centennial Act (Centennial Act) established the NPS Centennial Challenge Fund.[65](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn65) The fund provides a federal match to private-sector donations for NPS projects and programs that "further the mission of the Service and … enhance the visitor experience in System units."[66](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn66) The fund receives both discretionary appropriations and offsetting collections made up of certain amounts from sales of entrance passes to seniors.[67](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn67) In selecting projects and programs to receive the funding, the Secretary of the Interior is to prioritize DM, physical improvements to visitor service facilities, and trail maintenance.[68](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn68) The FY2026 appropriation for the Centennial Challenge Fund in P.L. 119-74 was $5.0 million.[69](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn69) NPS estimated that senior pass sales would provide $8.5 million for the fund in FY2026.[70](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn70)

The Centennial Act also established the NPS Second Century Endowment and directed that the endowment receive revenues from senior pass sales totaling $10 million annually.[71](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn71) In addition, the endowment is authorized to receive gifts, devises, and bequests from donors. The funds may be used for projects approved by the Secretary of the Interior that further the mission and purposes of NPS, including projects on the maintenance backlog. For FY2025 (the most recent year available), the National Park Foundation, which administers the endowment, reported spending $1.0 million from the endowment on unspecified projects.[72](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn72)

Other NPS mandatory appropriations also have been partially used for DM. These include donations, monies collected under the Park Building Lease and Maintenance Fund, transportation fees collected under the Transportation Systems Fund, and rents and payroll deductions for the use and occupancy of government quarters, among others. NPS estimated varying amounts for these mandatory appropriations for FY2026.[73](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn73)

## Issues for Congress

In the 119th Congress, debate about NPS DM has focused on potential reauthorization of deposits to the LRF, the DM fund established by the GAOA. H.R. 9250, as ordered reported by the House Committee on Natural Resources, and S. 1547, as reported by the Senate Committee on Energy and Natural Resources, would reauthorize deposits to the fund for FY2027-FY2031 and make other changes related to the distribution and use of monies, streamlining project implementation, and ensuring the accuracy of DM data, among other matters. For further discussion, see CRS Insight IN12729, *Legacy Restoration Fund for Deferred Maintenance: 119th Congress Reauthorization Bills*.

Congressional oversight of the LRF after its 2020 establishment raised multiple issues that have informed legislation in the 119th Congress. Some Members questioned whether the fund was an effective tool for NPS, given that the NPS backlog grew rather than decreased after FY2020 ([**Figure 1**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#_Ref480469297)). NPS has emphasized that despite factors leading to growth in the DM estimates—changes in DM estimation methods, inflation in the costs to execute projects, and new maintenance needs—the LRF has played a major role in addressing the backlog. NPS estimated in August 2025 that the FY2021-FY2025 LRF funding would address $4.7 billion in DM upon completion of the funded projects.[74](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn74) GAO has stated that another positive impact of the LRF is that it has "fostered a cultural change [at the land management agencies] toward maintaining better data on deferred maintenance."[75](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn75) Nonetheless, GAO still noted issues with unclear reporting by NPS and other DOI agencies on reasons for changes in DM estimates, which have compromised the availability of information Congress may need for its decisionmaking.[76](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn76) GAO stated that, "in the absence of additional information on agencies' DM&R estimates, Congress and the public are limited in their ability to determine the true impact of DM&R on agency operations or the actual amount of funding needed to address the DM&R backlog."[77](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn77) H.R. 9250, as ordered reported, and S. 1547, as reported, would require NPS and other agencies to establish "clear, quantifiable, and standardized metrics" for calculating DM and to meet other data criteria.

Some Members also questioned the geographic distribution of NPS's LRF projects, expressing concern about uneven distribution of the funding across states and regions.[78](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn78) NPS's criteria for LRF project selection did not explicitly include geographic distribution; in contrast, FS's criteria for choosing LRF projects included a requirement that 40% of LRF funding go to "regional priority projects" across the FS regions.[79](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn79) A related concern for some was the distribution of LRF funds among large versus small parks and urban versus rural parks.[80](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn80) H.R. 9250, as ordered reported, and S. 1547, as reported, would require NPS and other agencies to achieve a "reasonable balance" between projects at higher- and lower-visitation units.[81](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn81)

Outside of debates about the LRF, some Members of Congress have advocated for prioritizing annual discretionary appropriations for NPS deferred and cyclic maintenance, especially in light of declines in this annual funding since enactment of the LRF (see section on "[Annual Appropriations for NPS Deferred Maintenance](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#_Ref168667853)").[82](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn82) Some emphasize the importance of annual appropriations for preventing further growth of the backlog and point out that the GAOA stated that no amounts in the LRF were to be used to "supplant discretionary funding made available for annually recurring facility operations, maintenance, and construction needs."[83](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn83) In contrast, some other Members of Congress, as well as NPS officials on some occasions, have expressed that the availability of LRF funding could allow annual appropriations to be used for NPS priorities other than maintenance (such as facility improvements), given limitations on overall funding for NPS and other DOI agencies.[84](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn84)

Some stakeholders have proposed additional options to increase funding for NPS infrastructure maintenance, beyond annual appropriations and the LRF. Options could include increasing the revenues that NPS collects, such as by raising park fees or introducing tolling on park highways that are used as commuter routes (e.g., in the region around Washington, DC).[85](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn85) In FY2026, pursuant to Executive Order 14314, NPS introduced a $100 surcharge for international visitors to 11 national parks, and the land management agencies also raised the price of their multiagency pass for international visitors.[86](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn86) H.R. 9250, as ordered reported, and S. 1547, as reported, would codify international visitor fee increases and would deposit revenues from the increases in the LRF.[87](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn87) Some also have proposed funding NPS DM through monies from the Land and Water Conservation Fund, income tax overpayments and contributions, taxes on outdoor recreation gear, motor fuel taxes, DOI land sales, or revenues from coin and postage stamp sales.[88](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn88) Others have opposed such proposals as diverting federal funds from other valued uses or imposing unnecessary fees on the public.

Various proposals have focused on ways NPS could reduce DM without the use of additional funds. Some have suggested that this could be accomplished by increasing the role of nonfederal partners in park management (e.g., by leasing more historic buildings to private businesses or transitioning NPS-operated campgrounds to concessioner management); by promoting volunteer assistance (e.g., through conservations service corps); by expanding the role of friends-of-the-park groups and similar nonprofits in NPS maintenance and construction projects; by disposing of unneeded structures in poor repair; or by transferring some NPS assets (e.g., commuter roads) out of federal ownership.[89](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn89) NPS and other stakeholders have identified challenges in considering some of these options, including challenges related to the costs and procedures for disposing of unneeded assets,[90](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn90) limitations to authorities for federal-nonfederal partnerships for infrastructure project management,[91](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn91) and regulatory and market obstacles to expanded use of leasing authority.[92](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html#fn92) Debate continues on the optimal strategies for reducing NPS deferred maintenance and the extent to which this should be a priority for NPS and for Congress.
