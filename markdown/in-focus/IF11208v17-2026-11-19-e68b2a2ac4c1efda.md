Pacific Primer: The Pacific Island Countries

Updated November 19, 2026
(IF11208)

Overview

The Pacific Island Countries (PICs) include 14 sovereign archipelagic nations across the Western and Central Pacific Ocean. The PICs (and Pacific territories) represent expansive environmental and cultural diversity and 20% of the world's total maritime exclusive economic zones (EEZ). At the same time, these countries share a population of less than 15 million (roughly 0.18% of the global population) and limited land area. The Pacific is often conceptualized into three distinct subregions based on culture, history, and colonial influence—Melanesia, Micronesia, and Polynesia (see [**Figure 1**](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IF/HTML/IF11208.html#_Ref157686334)).

The United States considers itself a "Pacific Nation," drawing not only on Hawaii, Alaska, and the Pacific coastline but also the 19th and 20thcentury acquisitions of several Pacific territories, including American Samoa, the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands [CNMI], and Guam. During World War II, the Pacific was a major battleground for U.S. forces against Imperial Japan. After the war, U.S. foreign policy in the region centered on U.S. economic and strategic roles in the Micronesian subregion, most notably the Republic of the Marshall Islands (RMI), Federated States of Micronesia (FSM), and Republic of Palau. These three states (commonly referred to as the Freely Associated States, or FAS) have signed voluntary Compacts of Free Association (COFA) with the United States (see CRS Report R48311, [*The Freely Associated States and Issues for Congress*](http://www.crs.gov/Reports/R48311), by Jared G. Tupuola).

The past two decades have seen a resurgent interest among successive U.S. Administrations and Congresses to reaffirm ties with PICs. This interest has been motivated in part by concern over growing influence of the People's Republic of China (PRC, or China) in the region, as well as economic and strategic issues related to fisheries, free and open waterways, natural disasters, and security ties.

U.S. Interests in the PICs

The Pacific has provided a strategic hub for U.S. military force posture and power projection in the Indo-Pacific, particularly from U.S. military installations in the FAS. Under the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2024 ([P.L. 118-42](http://www.congress.gov/law/118/pub/42), Division G, Title II, §201), Congress renewed the COFAs for 20 years (from FY2024 to FY2043). Under those agreements, the FAS grant the United States the exclusive right to operate military bases in the FAS and to make decisions related to their external security. In exchange, the FAS received continued guarantees of assistance funds from the United States.

The United States has [expanded](https://www.csis.org/programs/australia-chair/projects/mapping-security-influence-pacific) military and security relations with other PICs as well, including through a 2023 U.S.-Papua New Guinea (PNG) Defense Cooperation Agreement and negotiations, initiated in 2024, to establish a Status of Forces Agreement with Fiji.

U.S. security cooperation with the PICs includes support for maritime and law enforcement agencies, as many of these countries face challenges from illicit drug trafficking and illegal, unreported, and unregulated (IUU) fishing. As of 2026, the United States has 13 shiprider agreements with PICs, which allow local law enforcement officers to embark on U.S. naval and coast guard ships to enforce national laws and combat IUU fishing.

The vast maritime resources of the PICs may offer economic opportunities for the United States. The South Pacific Tuna Treaty (SPTT) is a multilateral agreement between the United States and 16 Pacific nations allowing U.S. tuna purse-seine vessels to fish for tuna in those countries' waters. In September 2023, the United States and parties to the SPTT signed a related Economic Assistance Agreement pledging annual payments by the United States of $60 million for 10 years to promote economic development. Congress adopted amendments to the SPTT through the National Defense Authorization Act of 2025 ([P.L. 119-60](http://www.congress.gov/law/119/pub/60)) that revise federal requirements for U.S. commercial fishing vessels operating in the Pacific parties' EEZs

Regional Geopolitics

The Pacific Islands Forum (PIF) is the region's main political and economic organization. Headquartered in Fiji, the PIF has 18 member nations—14 PICs, 2 French collectivities, Australia, and New Zealand. The PIF also has 4 associate members, 9 observers (mostly multilateral organizations), and 23 dialogue partners. The United States is a PIF Dialogue Partner, and two U.S. territories, American Samoa and Guam, gained associate Member status in 2024. The PIF has served as an important venue for regional solidarity and policy-agenda setting, including the [2018 Boe Declaration on Regional Security](https://forumsec.org/sites/default/files/2024-03/2018%20Boe%20Declaration.pdf) which identified climate change as the "single greatest threat" to the peoples of the Pacific.

The Pacific is one of few remaining regions of the world where several countries diplomatically recognize Taiwan (which calls itself the Republic of China) instead of the People's Republic of China. These countries (RMI, Palau, and Tuvalu) face pressure from China to align with Beijing by switching diplomatic recognition; PRC efforts have included economic inducements and implicit threats to their economies. Since 2019, three other PICs have shifted recognition from Taiwan to China (Solomon Islands, Kiribati, and Naoero).

The region faces non-traditional security threats including challenges from drug trafficking, natural disasters, and political corruption. Some independence movements, including in PNG's autonomous region of Bougainville, could reshape the region's diplomatic landscape.

Energy price shocks and disruptions to global commodity markets because of conflicts in the Middle East have had an [impact](https://www.csis.org/analysis/fueling-vulnerability-economic-and-strategic-costs-oil-dependence-pacific) on PICs, which are import dependent and rely on [oil for 80%](https://repository.unescap.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/52eec907-1f22-4795-bb18-2db6e6a4fd42/content) of total energy supply.

Recent U.S. Engagement

The second Trump Administration has sought to emphasize commercial diplomacy and security cooperation with the Pacific and to implement a "[trade over aid](https://islandtimes.org/setting-the-record-straight-the-u-s-commitment-to-the-pacific/)" model. In February 2026, the Administration hosted the inaugural [U.S.-PIC Business Forum](https://www.state.gov/releases/office-of-the-spokesman/2025/09/on-the-deputy-secretarys-participation-in-the-u-s-pacific-islands-business-forum-meeting/) to promote trade and investment opportunities between U.S. and PIC businesses. The Administration has expressed interest in seabed mining exploration in the EEZs of some PICs. (See CRS In Focus IF12974, [*Seabed Mining Interests Across the Pacific Islands*](http://www.crs.gov/Reports/IF12974), by Caitlin Keating-Bitonti et al.)

During the 2026 PIF Leaders Meeting in Palau, the United States [announced](https://www.state.gov/releases/office-of-the-spokesman/2026/09/promoting-pacific-prosperity-u-s-engagement-at-the-pacific-islands-forum/) it intends to provide over $150 million in U.S. support for energy security, critical infrastructure, security, trade, and scientific cooperation in the region. The United States and the FAS also [announced](https://www.peacecorps.gov/about-the-agency/media-center/news/peace-corps-signs-letter-of-intent-to-establish-north-pacific-program-with-micronesia-the-marshall-islands-and-palau/) their intent to reestablish the Peace Corps in the FAS. In October 2026, the United States hosted the [first U.S.-Pacific Islands Conference of Police Ministers](https://www.state.gov/releases/office-of-the-spokesman/2026/10/securing-the-pacific-together-u-s-commitments-at-the-inaugural-u-s-pacific-islands-conference-of-police-ministers-copmin/) and announced its intent to provide "more than $44 million in new programming to counter transnational criminal organizations operating in the Pacific and threatening the American people."

[Some](https://foreignpolicy.com/2026/07/31/pacific-islands-trump-geopolitics-china-oceania/) analysts have argued the Administration lacks a clear policy agenda for the Pacific and has failed to establish a strong diplomatic presence in the region. Cuts to foreign assistance and tariffs on some PICs have been a source of consternation for critics as well. [Others](https://www.heritage.org/global-politics/report/aloha-and-stay-tuned-the-trump-administrations-pacific-policy-inventory) argue the Administration has delivered a list of economic, security, and diplomatic accomplishments to the region in line with an "America First foreign policy."

China's Influence

Some observers' concerns over PRC influence in the Pacific have become more acute following a series of security and economic agreements between China and several PICs. These have included the details of a leaked [draft](https://x.com/AnnaPowles/status/1506845794728837120) of a 2022 China-Solomon Islands security agreement, which may allow China to deploy security and naval assets to the Solomon Islands if requested. Though China has no military bases in the region, some analysts worry that China may seek to develop dual-use facilities under the guise of economic or infrastructure development projects. Some analysts also are concerned that law enforcement agreements may lead to increased PRC influence over PIC policing, political institutions, and strategic direction.

In 2024, China was the second-largest provider of bilateral development assistance to the region, according to the Lowy Institute; the United States was third largest. At least 12 PICs have joined China's Belt and Road Initiative, which promotes PRC-backed infrastructure development. China [hosts](https://un.china-mission.gov.cn/eng/zgyw/202505/t20250528_11635595.htm) a range of regional fora to facilitate PRC-PIC collaboration. PIC governments occasionally have pushed back against PRC attempts to expand regional influence, including against PRC proposals. In 2022, for example, some PICs resisted a PRC proposal for a wide-ranging PRC-PIC security agreement that ultimately was shelved.

At times, China has exerted its military capabilities in what [some analysts](https://www.aspistrategist.org.au/chinas-pacific-ballistic-missile-test-views-from-aspi-analysts/) describe as a show of force to dissuade PICs from aligning with Western partners, namely Australia and New Zealand, on security issues. In September 2025, for example, China launched an intercontinental ballistic missile over the Pacific Ocean for the first time since 1980. In July 2026, China conducted another missile test into the Pacific, which some analysts noted was done shortly after Fiji had signed new security agreements with Australia. In both cases, several PICs raised objections to China's actions.

Congressional Considerations

The 119th Congress has seen several PIC-related pieces of legislation that Members may wish to consider, amend, or reintroduce in the 120th Congress. [S. 5131](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119/s/5131); [H.R. 9508](http://www.congress.gov/bill/119/hr/9508); [H.R. 4955](http://www.congress.gov/bill/119/hr/4955); [H.R. 6619](http://www.congress.gov/bill/119/hr/6619); [H.R. 3332](http://www.congress.gov/bill/119/hr/3332); [S. 1740](http://www.congress.gov/bill/119/s/1740); and [H.R. 562](http://www.congress.gov/bill/119/hr/562) seek to address a range of issues including U.S. diplomatic and consular presence and engagement with the region, transnational criminal activity, counterdrug interdiction, economic cooperation, and a U.S. strategic vision for the Pacific.

In its oversight capacity, Congress may monitor COFA-specific provisions in [P.L. 118-42](http://www.congress.gov/law/118/pub/42), including those [regarding healthcare programs for FAS veterans](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-109152) who served in the U.S. military, progress towards defense sites infrastructure, and FAS development needs. (See CRS In Focus IF12194, [*The Compacts of Free Association*](http://www.crs.gov/Reports/IF12194), by Jared G. Tupuola.) Likewise, ongoing implementation of the SPTT and related economic assistance to the PICs may warrant continued congressional attention to ensure executive branch implementation of its obligations.
