Programs administered by RHS support both single-family and multifamily housing, generally

properties have been implemented, several new policies were enacted in the 119th Congress as a part of the 21st Century
ROAD to Housing Act (P.L. 119-101), and additional policies have been proposed.

Background

The U.S. Department of Agriculture’s (USDA’s) Rural Housing Service (RHS), part of USDA’s
Rural Development mission area, administers a number of federal housing programs1 designed to
support homeownership or rental housing primarily for lower-income families in rural areas.2
These programs are authorized by Title V of the Housing Act of 1949 (P.L. 81-171; 42 U.S.C.
§§1471 et seq.), as amended, and are usually referred to by the section of that act under which
they are authorized.

RHS housing programs are under the jurisdiction of the Senate Committee on Banking, Housing,
and Urban Affairs and the House Committee on Financial Services. Appropriations for the RHS
programs are provided through the annual appropriations acts that provide funding for the USDA,
which are under the jurisdiction of the Agriculture, Rural Development, Food and Drug
Administration, and Related Agencies subcommittees of the Senate and the House Appropriations
Committees.

RHS housing programs are primarily grant and loan programs, including both guaranteed and
direct loans.3 For grant programs, annual appropriations acts specify the amount available for a
program each year. For loan programs, annual appropriations acts specify a total dollar amount of
loans that can be made or guaranteed (the loan authorization level), as well as provide any
appropriations necessary to cover the expected costs of those loans (credit subsidy/budget
authority).
RHS operates through a national office with staff in Washington, DC, and St. Louis, MO,4 and
within Rural Development (RD) state offices, led by presidentially appointed state directors.5
These state offices administer the federal assistance programs, including accepting and processing
applications for direct loans and grants.

While the RHS housing programs are targeted specifically toward rural areas, rural communities
and residents may also benefit from other federal housing programs, most of which are
administered by the Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD).6 For example,

Defining Rural

There are numerous definitions of rural used for different policies, programs, and purposes across
the federal government.8 The definitions of rural and rural area for the purposes of USDA rural
housing programs are established in statute. They are based on population thresholds and, in some
cases, other characteristics. Specifically

         the terms “rural” and “rural area” mean any open country, or any place, town, village, or
          city which is not [  ... ]9 part of or associated with an urban area and which (1) has a
        population not in excess of 2,500 inhabitants, or (2) has a population in excess of 2,500 but
        not in excess of 10,000 if it is rural in character, or (3) has a population in excess of 10,000

Certain characteristics of rural areas, such as remote locations and smaller populations, can pose
some challenges for housing development or residents’ access to mortgage financing. These can
include higher costs of obtaining materials and labor, lack of infrastructure, and less access to
financial services, among other things.17

These differences in the housing stock, housing conditions, and community characteristics can
have implications for rural housing needs and potential policy solutions. As noted earlier,
however, rural can be defined in many ways, and the specific definition used has implications for
the characteristics of the rural housing stock and the magnitude of various housing challenges in
such areas.18 Furthermore, while rural areas in general exhibit these features, housing conditions
are not uniform across rural areas. Specific housing conditions and challenges can vary across
rural regions (e.g., Appalachia, the Lower Mississippi Delta), different market or industry types
(e.g., resort towns, natural resource boomtowns), or areas with certain distinctive features (e.g.,
tribal areas, colonias).

RHS Rural Housing Programs

The rural housing programs administered by USDA are authorized by the Housing Act of 1949
(P.L. 81-171), as amended. That law declared a national goal of “a decent home and a suitable
living environment for every American family” and took a variety of actions related to housing.
Title V of the law authorized the Secretary of Agriculture to provide financial assistance to farm
owners to construct, improve, alter, repair, or replace dwellings or other farm buildings in order to
provide decent, safe, and sanitary living conditions and adequate farm buildings. In the 1960s,
Congress expanded eligibility for these programs to owners of other real estate in rural areas
(rather than just farm owners),19 and then to rural residents more generally.20 Several other
financial assistance programs were added via amendments to Title V throughout the 1960s, with
additional programs added in the 1980s and 1990s. In 1994, a departmental restructuring was
statutorily authorized, which resulted in USDA establishing the RHS and assigning it jurisdiction
over these programs.21 Additionally, various programmatic and policy changes have been enacted
in appropriations acts or other laws or implemented through regulations or other administrative

Single-Family Homeownership Programs25

Section 502 Single Family Housing Direct Loans

Through this program, USDA makes direct loans to very low- and low-income individuals who
do not currently own adequate housing to purchase homes to be used as primary residences. Loan
funds can be used to buy, build, rehabilitate, improve, or relocate a home.26 The program provides
mortgages with fixed interest rates to qualified borrowers who are unable to obtain credit
elsewhere. Borrowers may qualify for payment subsidies that lower their monthly mortgage
payments to the greater of (1) a principal, interest, tax, and insurance payment of no more than
24% of the borrower’s income or (2) a loan payment based on a 1% interest rate, plus taxes and
insurance.27 Payment subsidy agreements remain in effect for specified periods of time but can be
renewed if borrowers continue to qualify based on their income.28 Borrowers are required to
repay all or a portion of any payment subsidies they received when they pay off the loans, transfer
title to the properties, or no longer occupy the homes.29

Selected Issues and Trends

Multifamily Rural Housing Preservation

Over time, the multifamily housing financed through the Section 515 and Section 514 programs
may be at risk of loss to the affordable housing stock. This can happen because the RHS loan is
ending through prepayment, agency enforcement actions, or natural maturation—any of which
can terminate affordability restrictions, leading to tenant displacement and loss of affordable
housing in a community.123

Initial rural housing preservation concerns centered on owners’ ability to prepay their loans and
exit the programs. Up until the late 1970s, there were no restrictions on loan prepayment, and
there were concerns about owners exiting the programs before the end of their 40- to 50-year loan
terms, at which point affordability restrictions ended. In response, several laws were enacted to
restrict borrowers’ ability to prepay and to change the terms of the programs so that later loans
(those made after 1989) were ineligible for prepayment. For properties at risk of prepayment,
these laws directed USDA to offer incentives to owners to remain in the programs or sell to
preservation-focused purchasers; enacted various notification and first purchase right
requirements; and authorized and then funded rental housing vouchers, under the Section 542
program, for tenants facing displacement upon owner prepayment (or mortgage foreclosure).124

Over time, concerns began to grow about the physical and financial condition of aging rural
multifamily assisted housing properties. A report issued by USDA in 2004 found that no Section
515 properties had adequate reserves or sufficient cash flow to make needed repairs or meet
maintenance needs over time.125 These properties risk falling into physical disrepair, which can
both threaten the health and safety of residents, and put the property at risk of foreclosure. In
response to these concerns, the FY2006 appropriations law (P.L. 109-97) created a new
demonstration program for the preservation and revitalization of USDA multifamily rental
housing properties (see the “Multifamily Housing Preservation and Revitalization (MPR)
Demonstration” section earlier in this report). The MPR program is intended to preserve Section
515 and Section 514 projects by funding their physical revitalization and extending their
affordable use agreements. Under MPR, RHS can restructure existing loans, using debt deferral,
soft second loans, 0% loans, and grants to preserve existing properties. The program was
permanently established by the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act (P.L. 119-101).

More recently, policymakers have focused on the preservation risks posed by maturing
mortgages. When Section 514 and Section 515 loans mature, their affordability restrictions end.
RHS has adopted some policies to encourage owners to refinance or otherwise reamortize their
mortgages in order to prevent loan maturation and extend use agreements and to notify tenants

Program Coordination

As noted earlier, RHS programs are sometimes used in conjunction with other federal affordable
housing programs, and some RHS housing programs provide similar types of assistance to that
provided by non-RHS housing programs. There have been various proposals over the years to
coordinate and/or streamline RHS programs and these other housing assistance resources. These
have ranged from proposals encouraging or requiring USDA and HUD to coordinate in various
ways to proposals to amend the law to move RHS programs to HUD and potentially integrate
them with existing HUD programs.138 Proposals to move USDA rural housing programs to HUD,
in particular, have been met with opposition from rural housing stakeholders, many of whom
argue that USDA’s expertise on rural issues and its network of local offices provide advantages in
administering rural housing programs and ensuring that they reach rural residents.139
