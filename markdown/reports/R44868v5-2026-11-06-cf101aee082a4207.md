Short-term, small-dollar loans (SDLs) are consumer loans with relatively low initial principal          Specialist in Financial
amounts (often less than $1,000) and relatively short repayment periods (typically ranging from     Economics
two weeks to six months). Consumers frequently use SDL products to cover cash flow shortages
that may occur because of unexpected expenses or periods of insufficient income. SDLs can be
offered in various forms and by various types of lenders. Depositories (i.e., banks and credit
unions) can make SDLs through financial products such as credit cards, credit card cash
advances, and checking account overdraft protection programs. SDLs can also be provided by nonbank lenders, also referred
to as alternative financial service (AFS) providers, which include payday or automobile-title lenders.

Whether borrower financial situations would be made worse from the use of expensive credit or from limited credit access is
widely debated. Consumer groups often raise concerns regarding the affordability of SDLs. The rates and fees borrowers pay
for SDLs are considered expensive. Borrowers may also fall into debt traps, situations where borrowers repeatedly roll over
existing loans into new loans and subsequently incur more charges rather than completely pay off the loans. Although debt
traps are more frequently discussed in the context of nonbank products such as payday loans, borrowers may still find it
difficult to repay outstanding balances and face additional charges on loans, such as credit cards, that are provided by
depositories. Conversely, the lending industry raises concerns about the reduced availability of SDLs. Regulations designed
to reduce borrowers’ costs may result in higher costs for lenders, possibly limiting or reducing the availability of SDLs.

SDLs are considered an expensive form of borrowing because they often carry triple-digit annual percentage rates (APRs).
However, the standard APR calculation arguably overstates the actual cost of loans if their repayment terms are less than a
full year. For borrowers seeking less expensive lending alternatives, performing reliable cost comparisons is difficult because
SDLs lack standardized features. Regulatory barriers also limit the ability of banks and credit unions to compete directly with
AFS providers in the SDL market, which could drive down costs. For these reasons, evaluating the relative costs of SDLs is
problematic for borrowers.

Similarly, the non-standardization of SDLs and related market pricing issues are also problematic for policymakers. For
example, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) implemented federal regulations in 2017 for payday, vehicle
title, and certain high-cost loans—an affordability framework designed to protect borrowers by establishing baseline
requirements to serve as a floor for state regulations. However, the CFPB rescinded two provisions in 2020 that would have
required SDL lenders to underwrite borrowers for affordability. Had these requirements proven too costly for lenders, any
resulting market contraction could have driven SDL borrowing costs even higher. As of the publication of this report, the
CFPB has not introduced an alternative regulatory framework to replace the rescinded provisions. Meanwhile, on February 5,
2026, the Predatory Lending Elimination Act (S. 3793) was introduced in the 119th Congress to establish a 36% APR limit on
SDLs. If S. 3793 is enacted while current market pricing issues persist, the CFPB’s retrospective findings suggest that an
SDL market contraction may occur.

Introduction

Short-term, small-dollar loans (SDLs) for consumers are typically for low initial principal
amounts (often less than $1,000) and designed to be repaid within short periods typically ranging
from two weeks to six months.1 SDLs may be used to cover cash flow shortages that occur
because of unexpected expenses or periods of income shortfalls, particularly for individuals
lacking adequate emergency savings or those with impaired credit.2 SDLs can be offered in
various forms and by various types of lenders. Federally insured depository institutions (i.e.,
banks and credit unions) offer SDLs via financial products such as credit cards, credit card cash
advances, and checking account overdraft protection programs. Nonbank lenders—including
alternative financial service (AFS) providers and financial technology (fintech) firms—offer
SDLs such as payday loans, personal debt consolidation loans, and automobile-title loans.3

The borrowing costs associated with SDLs appear substantially higher when compared to longer-
term, larger-dollar loans. However, some borrowers may not fully understand the annual
percentage rate (APR)—the price of SDLs and most longer-term consumer loans—especially
when the loan maturities are under 12 months. Furthermore, the lack of uniform loan features
makes APR price comparisons difficult across different SDL products, which is a key problem for
both borrowers and regulators.

If borrowers do not fully understand or cannot do direct SDL cost comparisons, then they risk
falling into debt traps. Debt traps occur when borrowers reborrow (i.e., roll over existing loans
into new loans), incurring additional charges but making little progress toward paying off their
initial loans.4 Repeated borrowing of similar loan amounts while steadily accumulating fees
causes rising indebtedness, entrapping individuals into worse financial situations. Although debt
traps are frequently linked to nonbank products such as payday loans, they may arise for
borrowers who make only minimum payments (rather than pay off the entire balances at the end
of statement periods) on credit card loans issued by depositories.

If regulators cannot do direct SDL cost comparisons, then inferring lenders’ profit margins is
problematic. In other words, regulators face challenges estimating the average size of loan
markups when industry SDLs lack standardized terms, features, and consistent lender reporting
requirements. Consequently, designing consumer protection policies in the absence of uniform

This report analyzes SDL market pricing issues.7 Following an overview of various loan product
descriptions, the SDL pricing mechanics and related challenges are presented. Specifically, the
general calculation and interpretation of the APR is illustrated. Next, the report highlights how the
lack of SDL product uniformity complicates cost comparisons, thereby compromising the
effectiveness of the APR as a disclosure tool. After reviewing various regulatory approaches to
borrower protections, the report outlines the CFPB’s initial affordability framework and
subsequent modifications. The CFPB’s retrospective findings may be useful for anticipating the
implications of S. 3793 if enacted while current SDL market pricing issues persist. The report
concludes by noting regulatory constraints that limit depositories’ competitiveness with AFS
lenders in certain markets, thus lessening the downward cost pressures on SDL market pricing.

SDL Prices and Pricing Issues

SDLs are considered an expensive form of borrowing, particularly because of their triple-digit
APRs.9 Apart from credit cards, SDLs typically require full payment on maturity. Making partial
or missing payments does little to reduce the original principal, while reborrowing adds more fees
and interest charges that increase total indebtedness.10 However, the APR metric arguably
overstates a loan’s cost when a repayment term of less than a full year is used in the denominator.
Furthermore, reliable cost comparisons depend on evaluating a borrower’s next-best borrowing
alternative, but finding SDLs with similar features is difficult. For these reasons, the perception
that SDLs are overpriced (e.g., costing more than alternative burdens, possibly resulting in a
financial burden) may not be entirely accurate.11

           Loan Pricing Fundamentals: Demand and Supply Factors
 The annual percentage rate (APR) is the total loan price, encompassing both interest and fees while also reflecting
  the demand and supply for credit. Credit demand captures what borrowers are willing to pay today instead of
  saving or deferring consumption until the future. The willingness to pay a premium for credit can be driven by a
  preference for immediate consumption or by sudden, unforeseen occurrences that necessitate urgent spending.
 The supply of credit reflects the costs lenders incur to acquire the funds subsequently used to offer SDLs.
  Different lender types, however, rely on different funding sources. Depositories (i.e., banks and credit unions)
  typically finance their loan portfolios with federally insured deposits that pay rates comparable to those charged
  for overnight interbank borrowing. In contrast, nonbanks obtain funding by borrowing from depository institutions
 or private equity investors. (Some payday lending firms are wholly owned by hedge funds or publicly traded and
 owned by shareholders.)12 Consequently, nonbanks generally pay more relative to depositories to acquire the
  funds to make SDLs.

  lenders can seize to recover losses in case of default—carry lower rates relative to those secured without
  collateral. For this reason, unsecured SDLs are more expensive than longer-term loans that are typically secured
 by the purchase being financed. The cost of credit also reflects risk-based pricing, the practice of charging riskier
 borrowers higher rates to reflect their additional credit or default risk.13 While risk-based pricing strategies may

Understanding the Annual Percentage Rate (APR) Calculation

The APR represents the total annual borrowing costs of a loan expressed as a percentage. The
APR is calculated using both interest rates and origination fees.16 The APR may be calculated
using the following general formula:

           APR = [(INTFEES)/(LNAMT)] * (365/DAYSOUT) * 100, where

         INTFEES = total interest and fees paid by the borrower;

       LNAMT = loan amount or total borrowings; and

       DAYSOUT = number of days that the loan is outstanding (term length).

The formula shows that the APR is inversely related to both the LNAMT and DAYSOUT.
Assuming two loans have identical LNAMTs and INTFEES, a 30-day SDL will mathematically
generate a higher APR than a loan with a longer maturity, such as a year or more. For this reason,
how best to interpret the APR for loans with maturities under 365 days has been debated.17 An
APR based on a term length of one year or greater accurately reflects the annual cost of credit.
By contrast, an APR for a loan expected to be repaid in less than 365 days is arguably overstated.
APR comparisons are most reliable for loans with identical maturities.18 For example, comparing
two payday loans with identical two-week maturities is most meaningful, even though both carry
triple-digit APRs. In contrast, comparing APRs of loans with different maturities (e.g., a loan that
matures in two weeks versus one maturing in 365 days) can be misleading. Although the APR of
the longer-term loan will mathematically be lower, the interest and fees paid by borrowers can be
higher for larger loan amounts or longer maturity lengths. Stated differently, loans with triple-

Failure of Market Price Convergence

A market is competitive when suppliers are likely to lose market share by pricing their products
too aggressively, meaning that they lack the ability to set prices higher than their competitors.
Stated differently, an economic principle—the law of one price (LOOP)—asserts that competition
will cause the prices of identical products to converge.20 When the LOOP holds, SDL pricing is
likely to be efficient, meaning that competition has driven prices down to more closely reflect
lenders’ supply costs (rather than excessive markups) even if the products remain expensive.21

Ideally, SDL comparisons by prospective borrowers or regulators would consist of either two
identical loans (e.g., two payday loans) or an SDL product and a close alternative (e.g., a bank
overdraft product and a payday loan). However, making ideal comparisons is not always possible.
Small differences in products can still increase the difficulty of comparing costs. For example,
when setting their own overdraft pricing policies, some banks may charge a fixed fee for each
overdraft regardless of the amount borrowed, whereas others have a tiered pricing structure as the
amount of the loan increases.22 Some banks require repayment sooner than the two-week period
typically associated with a payday loan; other banks may allow slightly longer repayment periods,
such as 30 days. Thus, without uniformity among products, APR comparisons are difficult among
similar depositories and especially across depositories and nonbank lenders.23

SDL markets can converge as predicted by the LOOP to achieve efficient pricing. Not all lenders
must report data on their SDLs, and differing pricing policies cause reported metrics to lack
standardized definitions. For example, interest rates and fees can be reported separately or
combined into a single metric. Maturity lengths differ among SDL products, raising comparability
questions. Because of their unique repayment terms, buy now, pay later plans technically have

SDL Regulatory Issues: Current Approaches and
Challenges Absent Market Price Convergence

Consumer lending is largely regulated to protect consumers and ensure lenders’ compliance with
fair-lending laws. Bank and nonbank lenders are subject to federal consumer protection and fair-
lending laws when offering consumer loan products, and multiple regulators oversee small-dollar
lending. Depositories with assets below $10 billion are supervised for consumer protection
compliance by their prudential regulators. Banks are supervised by the federal prudential
regulators, namely the Federal Reserve System, the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency
(OCC), and the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC). The National Credit Union
Administration (NCUA) is the federal prudential regulator for credit unions. State-chartered
depositories may face additional state-level regulations, but they must still comply with federal
regulations if their deposits are federally insured. Depositories with national charters are
generally exempt from additional state consumer regulations, but exceptions may apply for
certain circumstances.

The CFPB is the primary federal regulator with the authority to issue regulations pursuant to most
federal consumer financial protection laws that cover an array of consumer financial products and
services.37 Depository institutions with assets greater than $10 billion are supervised for consumer
protection compliance by the CFPB. Nonbank lenders—financial firms without national or state
depository charters, which include many AFS providers (e.g., payday and auto-title lenders)—
may be supervised by the CFPB or state financial regulators.

Regulatory Challenges Absent Market Price Convergence

Market price convergence, conceptually analogous to an efficient LOOP outcome, allows
regulators to rely on benchmark comparisons rather than inflexible APR caps to implement
consumer protection regulations. For example, although achieving price convergence in the
conventional mortgage market still has challenges, various standardization initiatives in
underwriting, data collection, and credit reporting enable mortgage APRs—particularly for
creditworthy borrowers—to converge within a certain range.57 For this reason, the consumer
protection framework adopted by regulators under the Home Mortgage Disclosure Act (P.L. 94-
200) is based on APR comparisons. Specifically, the APRs offered by mortgage lenders must be
reported to the CFPB and compared to an average (weekly) benchmark APR to see whether they
stay within an established range.58

By contrast, underwriting is costly for many SDLs given their low principal amounts, and lenders
are also unlikely to review credit reports. Combined with the lack of standardized products and
price convergence, regulating the SDL markets to protect (subprime) borrowers may compromise
credit availability. This section summarizes examples of challenges.

The rule established—at the federal level—a consumer protection floor, covering loans lasting 45
days or less, such as payday loans, auto-title loans, and direct deposit advances. Loans for more
than 45 days in which the cost of credit exceeds 36% and the lender has a security interest (e.g.,
the ability to repossess a borrower’s vehicle if the loan is not repaid or access to a paycheck or a
checking account) are covered. The rule excludes various types of loans, such as consumer loans
secured by the good purchased (e.g., a durable good that can be used to secure the loan, such as a
car), mortgage loans, credit card loans, student loans, overdrafts, and PALs (issued by
prudentially regulated credit unions). The rule applies to all lenders of covered products.

             Can Small-Dollar Lending Be Profitable for Banks?
 The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) conducted a small-dollar loan pilot program between
 December 2007 and December 2009, beginning with 31 participating banks, to observe the feasibility of offering
  lower-credit-cost alternatives to payday loans and fee-based overdraft programs.88 Customers in the pilot
 program were offered loans that, unlike typical short-term, small-dollar loan (SDL) products, were for $2,500 or
  less for approximately 90 days or more with annual percentage rates (APRs) of 36% or less; the streamlined
  underwriting process included proof of identity, proof of income, and a credit report.89 The FDIC reported that
  banks found small-dollar lending to be a useful business strategy, facilitating the ability to build or retain profitable,
  long-term relationships with customers as well as the opportunity to get favorable consideration under the
 Community Reinvestment Act. However, banks found small-dollar lending programs to be more successful or
  cost-effective when targeted to existing customers who use financial products over longer time periods (as
 opposed to new customers with financial behaviors and histories that have not been previously observed).90 The
  fixed costs associated with evaluating financial risks (e.g., purchase of credit reporting data; past banking
  relationships; verification of identity, income, and employment) are similar, regardless of whether a financial
  product is offered for two weeks or a credit card loan is made for a year. Stated differently, recovering the
  underwriting costs for new SDL customers or those who infrequently make transactions is difficult.
