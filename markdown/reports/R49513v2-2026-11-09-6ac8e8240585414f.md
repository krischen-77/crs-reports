Overview and Issues for Congress

The Kyrgyz Republic (commonly known as Kyrgyzstan) is a mountainous country slightly
smaller than South Dakota. Kyrgyzstan borders three other Central Asian states in addition to the
People’s Republic of China (PRC, or China) (see Figure 1) and maintains close ties with Russia.
The population of approximately 7.4 million is predominantly Kyrgyz (74%), with minority
groups including Uzbeks (15%), Russians (5%), Dungans (1%), and Uyghurs (1%), among
others.1 Approximately 90% of the population is Muslim, primarily Sunni; a plurality of the
Christian minority, which accounts for about 7% of the population, is Russian Orthodox.2 For
most of the 30 years since independence, Kyrgyzstan was considered by scholars and
nongovernmental organizations (NGOs) as the most democratic country in Central Asia, with a
vibrant civil society and a higher degree of press freedom than is found elsewhere in the region.3
Kyrgyzstan is one of 17 countries worldwide whose legislatures participate in the House
Democracy Partnership, a bipartisan commission of the U.S. House of Representatives that aims
to advance U.S. strategic interests by promoting good governance, legislative transparency, and
institutional effectiveness.4

In light of political developments that began in 2020 (see “Domestic Political Background”
below), the U.S.-based NGO Freedom House classified Kyrgyzstan as “not free” in 2021 and has
since maintained that designation.5 Some Members of Congress, other U.S. officials, and outside
observers have voiced concern about democratic backsliding (see Table 1) in Kyrgyz politics.6
Some Members have raised alarm about Kyrgyz government pressure on independent media
outlets, including the congressionally funded Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty (RFE/RL).7
Kyrgyzstan, a lower-middle-income country that relies heavily on foreign remittances, has faced
additional economic and geopolitical pressures as a result of the 2022 Russian invasion of
Ukraine and related sanctions, but it has also benefitted from trade diversion, emerging as a major

U.S.-Kyrgyzstan Relations

The United States was among the first countries to recognize Kyrgyzstan’s independence
following the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991. From 2001 to 2014, the United States
operated a Transit Center at Manas, near the Kyrgyz capital of Bishkek, in support of both
Operation Enduring Freedom and the International Security Assistance Force (ISAF) in
Afghanistan. U.S. relations with Kyrgyzstan deteriorated under President Almazbek Atambayev
(in office 2011-2017), reportedly in part due to Russian pressure, and the Kyrgyz parliament
voted in 2013 not to extend the U.S. lease on Manas.12 Russia leases the nearby Kant airbase. In
2023, the State Department described bilateral relations with Kyrgyzstan as characterized by
“ebbs and flows” and noted “wide swings in our political and security relationship.”13 The Trump
Administration describes priority areas of engagement with Kyrgyzstan as including “expanding
economic cooperation, strengthening sanctions compliance, and enforcing national immigration
laws” and identifies infrastructure, critical minerals, and investment cooperation as areas of
opportunity for strengthening economic ties.14

U.S. bilateral relations with Kyrgyzstan are complemented by the C5+1 diplomatic platform,
which provides a forum for the governments of the United States and the five Central Asian
countries to “advance regional solutions to global challenges” by engaging on economic, energy,
and security issues.15 In November 2025, President Trump hosted a C5+1 presidential summit in
Washington, DC, to mark the 10th anniversary of the platform. According to the State Department,

Domestic Political Background

Kyrgyzstan experienced political upheavals that ousted authoritarian-leaning presidents in 2005
and 2010.25 A new constitution adopted in 2010 transitioned the country to a semi-parliamentary
system in which the president shared executive power with the prime minister. Several Members
of Congress and other U.S. policymakers voiced support for Kyrgyzstan’s parliamentary
democracy, the only such political system in Central Asia at the time.26 By 2020, however,
Kyrgyzstan’s legislature had, in the assessment of Freedom House, “de facto surrendered its
constitutionally granted power to form and control the executive branch” and had become a
rubber-stamp body “merely paying lip service to the declared goal of building a parliamentary
democracy.”27 Kyrgyzstan experienced further political upheaval following October 2020
parliamentary elections in which opposition parties alleged widespread irregularities.28 The
country’s Central Electoral Commission annulled the elections after mass protests broke out in
Bishkek, and within ten days both the prime minister and the president had resigned.29 Sadyr
Japarov, a former member of parliament who had been serving an 11-year sentence on kidnapping
charges that he maintains were politically motivated, quickly became both prime minister and
acting president after protestors freed him from prison.30

Upon taking power, Japarov—a nationalist politician with a large social media following—began
to push for a constitutional referendum that would return the country to a presidential form of
government.31 In January 2021, Japarov won 79% of the vote in a snap presidential election that
coincided with a referendum in which 84% of voters supported reverting to a presidential
system.32 A new constitution, endorsed by voters in a subsequent April 2021 referendum,
significantly expanded presidential power at the expense of the legislature, transferring several
key parliamentary competencies to the executive and reducing the number of seats in the
