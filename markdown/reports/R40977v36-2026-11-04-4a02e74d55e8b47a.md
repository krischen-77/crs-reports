Overview

Governments engage formally through international organizations and less formally through
international forums to coordinate economic and foreign policies. International organizations,
such as the United Nations (UN) and the International Monetary Fund (IMF), are governed by
official international agreements and have permanent offices with staff implementing and
managing such agreements.

The Group of 7 (G7) and the Group of 20 (G20), in contrast, are informal forums for advancing
international cooperation and coordination about economic and foreign policy issues. The G7
comprises seven economically developed democracies—Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan,
the United Kingdom (UK), and the United States—as well as the European Union (EU). G20
membership includes a mix of economically developed and emerging-market countries: the G7
members as well as Argentina, Australia, Brazil, China, India, Indonesia, Mexico, Russia, Saudi
Arabia, South Africa, South Korea, Turkey (Türkiye), and the African Union (AU) (Figure 1).1

Leaders of the G7 and G20 countries generally meet annually at summits, and lower-level
officials meet throughout the year. The forums initially focused on international economic and
financial issues and over time have expanded to include a range of foreign policy issues. The G7
and G20 do not have permanent headquarters or staff and have at times worked in conjunction
with formal international organizations. For example, G7 and G20 leaders have invited the heads
of international organizations to participate in meetings, requested international organizations to
examine specific policy issues, negotiated reforms of international organizations, and reiterated
support for the policy goals of international organizations.

Historical Context

The G7 has roots back to the 1970s, and the G20 rose to prominence during the global financial
crisis of 2008-2009.4 Both forums run in parallel today.

G7

In the mid-1970s, leaders from a group of five developed countries—France, Germany, Japan, the
UK, and the United States (known then as the Group of Five, or G5)—began meeting annually to
discuss international economic issues, including the oil price shocks in the 1970s and changes in
exchange rate policies. In the 1980s, G5 leaders broadened their discussions to include Canada
and Italy, forming the G7. The EU began participating in the G5 meetings in the late 1970s and is
a permanent member of the G7, although it does not host or chair G7 meetings.

G7 discussions originally focused on macroeconomic policies, including exchange rates, balance
of payments, globalization, trade, and economic relations with developing countries. Over time,
the G7’s focus on macroeconomic policies expanded to include a variety of other global and
transnational issues, including the environment, crime, drugs, AIDS, and terrorism.

In 1998, Russia joined the G7, creating the Group of Eight (G8). Russia participated in
discussions about foreign policy issues; it did not usually participate in discussions on
international economic policy, which continued to occur mainly at the G7 level. The G7 leaders
of the G8 disinvited Russia in 2014, following Russia’s illegal annexation of the Crimean region
of Ukraine, and the group reverted to the G7.

G20

Starting in the early 1990s, emerging markets became more active in the international markets,
and the Asian financial crisis of 1997-1998 demonstrated that problems in emerging-market
countries can have spillover effects on developed economies. In 1999, finance ministers and
central bank governors of the G7 partnered with their counterparts from 12 major emerging
economies and the European Union to establish the G20, aimed at encouraging coordination
between advanced and emerging economies.5 Over the next decade, the highest-level G20
meetings were held among finance ministers and central bank governors.

At the behest of the G7 leaders, the G20 leaders met for the first time in November 2008 in
Washington, DC, to discuss the global financial crisis. Emerging markets’ economies had grown
rapidly over the previous decade (Figure 2), and the decision to convene G20 leaders reflected
recognition that emerging markets needed to be included in the discussions about the global

At the third G20 summit, held in September 2009 in Pittsburgh, PA, the G20 leaders decided that
going forward, the G20 would be the premier forum for international economic cooperation,

the G20.7 Over time, G20 discussions have broadened from economic issues to include foreign
policy issues such as health, refugees, terrorism, and climate change. In 2010, the G20 leaders
made Spain a “permanent guest invitee” in the G20, which enables Spain to participate in the
meetings,8 and the AU joined in the forum in 2023 at the invitation of the G20 leaders.

                             BRICS
  In 2001, an economist at Goldman Sachs coined the term BRICs (Brazil, Russia, India, and China) to refer to a
 group of non-Western, rapidly growing emerging-market economies.9 The term was taken seriously, particularly
 by Russia, and led to the creation of a new international forum specifically for emerging markets.10 The BRIC
  foreign affairs ministers met for the first time in 2006, and the leaders began holding summits in 2009. South Africa
  joined in 2011, and the group updated its name to the BRICS.
  In 2024 and 2025, six new countries joined the BRICS forum as members—Egypt, Ethiopia, Indonesia, Iran, Saudi
  Arabia, and the United Arab Emirates. According to the BRICS website, BRICS leaders represent countries that
  account for almost half of the world’s population and an estimated 40% of global GDP.11 Ten “partner countries”
  also joined in 2025 (Belarus, Bolivia, Cuba, Kazakhstan, Malaysia, Nigeria, Thailand, Uganda, Uzbekistan, and
  Vietnam). Partner countries formally collaborate and participate in BRICS meetings without holding full
 membership status or say in the bloc’s decisionmaking. The expanded BRICS grouping is sometimes referred to as
 BRICS+.
 BRICS leaders discuss a range of issues at their meetings, and common themes include advocating for greater
  representation of emerging markets in global organizations, coordinating economic policy, reducing reliance on the
  U.S. dollar, and creating alternative finance systems.12 The BRICS countries created the New Development Bank
  (colloquially called the BRICS Bank) in 2013 and the Contingent Reserve Arrangement (colloquially called the
 BRICS Monetary Fund) in 2014, institutions that are intended to function similarly to the World Bank and IMF.
 The original BRICS countries are all members of the G20 but focus on matters that are prioritized by developing
  countries at BRICS summits. Some observers assert the BRICS summits serve as counterweights to Western
  influence in global financial institutions.13 U.S. policymakers have taken differing views of the BRICS forum. For
  example, the U.S. National Security Advisor in 2023 reportedly rejected the notion that the BRICS was evolving
  into a geopolitical rival to the United States.14 In 2025, at the start of the 17th BRICS summit, President Trump
  cautioned that any country aligning itself with the “anti-American” policies of the BRICS could be subject to an
  additional 10% tariff rate on imports into the United States.15
 The 18th BRICS summit was held in India on September 12-13, 2026. China is scheduled to host the 19th BRICS
 summit in 2027.


Meetings and Outcomes

The president of the G7 and the G20 rotates annually among members (Table 1). The rotating
president sets the agenda, date, and location of the annual summit, and manages the group’s
secretarial, clerical, and administrative affairs. The rotating president also organizes meetings
among lower-level officials before and after the summit. The leaders’ personal representatives for
the G7 and the G20, known as “sherpas,” meet regularly, as do the G7 and G20 finance ministers,
central bank governors, and foreign affairs ministers.16

G20 presidents have also convened cabinet-level officials focused on nonfinance issues, such as
energy, labor, and trade. The rotating president also has discretion to invite additional countries
and international organizations to summits and meetings. In addition, the G7 and G20
parliamentary speakers usually meet annually, and the G20 president at times convenes meetings
among businesses, think tanks, and civil society groups from member countries to inform G20
discussions.

The forums have led to the creation of a complex set of interactions among officials at many
different levels of government. The interactions open communication channels, which can be
particularly useful during crises. At the same time, it takes time and resources for the national
agencies tasked with managing their countries’ preparations for and participation in these
meetings to execute their responsibilities.

After meeting, the groups usually release a statement summarizing the discussions and any
commitments pledged by members. All agreements, comments, recommendations, and policy
reforms reached by the G7 and G20 ministers, central bankers, and leaders are done so by
consensus. There is no formal voting system and commitments are nonbinding; the G7 and G20
do not have a way to enforce implementation of the agreements at the national level beyond
moral suasion.

Notable G7 outcomes have included, for example, calling for the Heavily Indebted Poor
Countries (HIPC) Initiative, a debt-relief program for low-income countries in the late 1990s;
proposing the creation of the Global Fund to Fight AIDS, Tuberculosis, and Malaria in 2000;
supporting the COVID-19 vaccine rollout to low-income countries in 2021; and coordinating
sanctions in response to Russia’s war against Ukraine in 2022. For the G20, examples of
outcomes have included organizing the international response to the global financial crisis of
2008-2009, coordinating debt relief initiatives during the COVID-19 pandemic, and strengthening
the lending capacity of the multilateral development banks in the early 2020s, among other
initiatives. At the same time, not all G7 and G20 meetings result in significant policy
commitments or outcomes. The joint statements are sometimes vague, emphasizing platitudes and

U.S. Participation and the Role of Congress

The executive branch largely manages U.S. participation in the G7 and G20. The G7 and G20
work streams have generally evolved to each have a finance track and a foreign affairs track.
Within the executive branch, the Department of the Treasury (the Treasury) manages the finance
track, and the Department of State (State) manages the foreign affairs track. Depending on the
topics on the forums’ agendas, Treasury and State might coordinate with other federal agencies,
such as the Departments of Energy and Labor and the U.S. Trade Representative. The White
House, particularly through the National Security Council, is also involved in the G7 and G20
process.

Congress has tools for shaping U.S. participation in the G7 and the G20. The implementation of
some U.S. commitments made by the executive branch in the G7 and G20 require legislation to
implement, such as increasing funding to an international organization or reforming financial
regulations. Members can support such legislation, oppose it, or condition their support on
specific policy objectives. Congress also controls the funding of agencies involved in the G7 and
G20 process. Members can also use hearings and reporting requirements to exercise oversight of
the executive branch’s engagement in the G7 and G20. Additionally, Members can use public
statements to support or oppose an Administration’s policy positions in the G7 and G20.

Congress has enacted a variety of legislation relevant to the G7 and the G20, including funding
summits hosted by the United States, specifying which officials in the State Department are
responsible for coordinating with G7 members, and directing the Administration to advance
specific policy priorities in the G7 and G20. Table 2 provides specific examples enacted since

Policy Options for Congress

If Members of Congress are interested in shaping U.S. participation in the G7 and/or the G20,
they have several options, depending on their policy goals. Some examples follow.
    •  Depending on Member assessments of the utility of the G7 and G20 as foreign
        policy tools, they could consider legislation establishing a minimum and/or
      maximum of appropriated funds that Treasury and State can spend on U.S.
        participation in the forums.
    •    If Members want more visibility into the Administration’s policy priorities and
        negotiations at the G7 and the G20, they could consider legislation requiring the
        Administration to brief key congressional committees prior to and/or following
       summits. Such briefings could include discussion of commitments made by the
        Administration that would require congressional action to implement. Members
       could also consider legislation establishing regular reports from Treasury and
        State on U.S. participation in the forums.
    •    If Members are concerned about which leaders are invited to G7 and G20
       meetings hosted by the United States, Members could, for example, consider
         legislation requiring the Administration to notify Congress before disinviting any
      member governments or inviting foreign officials that the Congress might find
        objectionable, such as those who are subject to U.S. sanctions.
    •    If Members are concerned about the location of G7 and G20 meetings hosted by
        the United States, they could require the Administration to provide a report to key
        congressional committees on the different locations considered and the factors
       used to make the final determination. Members could also limit the use of
        appropriated funds for G7 and G20 meetings to venues that meet specific criteria.
    •    If Members want more information about the cost of U.S. participation in the G7
       and the G20, they could request such an evaluation from the Government
        Accountability Office (GAO). Members could also request the report identify any
        potential cost saving measures.
    •    If Members are concerned about the number of federal agencies and number of
        offices within each agency involved in the G7 and G20 process, they could
        consider establishing a G7 and G20 coordinator within Treasury and/or State,
        potentially to be appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the
        Senate, to manage and streamline U.S. participation. Members could also
        consider assigning such duties to an existing position at Treasury or State.
    •    If Members want to influence U.S. policy priorities at the G7 and G20, they
       could consider legislation directing the United States to use its voice and
        influence at the G7 and G20 to advance congressional priorities, similar to
         legislation aimed at combatting foreign opioid trafficking and addressing marine
         debris, among other issues.35
    •    If Members are concerned about the expansion of the BRICS forum and other
      BRICS activity, Members could hold hearings to evaluate the implications of
       broadened membership on U.S. economic and foreign policy interests; require
        regular reports from the Administration on efforts by the BRICS countries to de-
