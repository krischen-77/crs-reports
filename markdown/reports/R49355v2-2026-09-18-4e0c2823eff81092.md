## Summary

Oceanic conditions, climate, human activities, and weather events can affect fishery resources and commercial infrastructure, such as boats, shoreside processing, and ports. Congress authorized the Secretary of Commerce (the Secretary) to provide disaster assistance to the fishing industry when fish populations decline or other disruptions cause economic losses. The governor of a state, the Secretary, or a representative of a fishing community (including through an official resolution of an Indian Tribe) may initiate a request for assistance. The National Marine Fisheries Service (NMFS), state agencies, and fishing communities compile the information needed to make a determination. When all necessary information has been obtained and reviewed, the Secretary determines whether a fishery failure or *fishery resource* *disaster* (also referred to as fishery disaster) has occurred. In most cases, Congress has appropriated funds to support the fishing industry and/or tribal fisheries following the Secretary's determination. Since 1994, the Secretary has made 144 fishery disaster determinations, and Congress has appropriated nearly $2.1 billion for fishery disaster relief. As of August 2026, examples of fishery disaster determinations have included those made for salmon fisheries in the Pacific Northwest and Alaska, Alaskan crab fisheries, West Coast Dungeness crab fisheries, the New England Atlantic herring fishery, and fisheries affected by several hurricanes and other causes. NMFS, states, interstate marine fisheries commissions, and industry or fishing community representatives often work together to develop a spend plan for how assistance will be distributed to the fishing industry and allocated among potential projects.

The criteria for the Secretary to determine whether a commercial fishery failure or fishery resource disaster has occurred is provided in Section 312(a) of the Magnuson-Stevens Fishery Conservation and Management Act (MSA; 16 U.S.C. §1861a(a)). In 2022, through the Fishery Resource Disasters Improvement Act (FRDIA; P.L. 117-328, Division S, Title II), Congress amended Section 312(a) of the MSA and repealed fishery disaster assistance provisions in Section 315 of the MSA (16 U.S.C. §1864, repealed). It also amended Sections 308(b) and 308(d) of the Interjurisdictional Fisheries Act (IFA; 16 U.S.C. §4107, repealed). The FRDIA amendments added prescriptive definitions, timelines, requirements, and revenue thresholds regarding the determination and administration of fishery disaster assistance to MSA Section 312(a). The legislation consolidated and clarified many of the provisions originally in the MSA and the IFA, and it incorporated parts of the NMFS agency directive on fishery disasters. Subsequently, in January 2025, the Fishery Improvement to Streamline untimely regulatory Hurdles post Emergency Situation Act (FISHES Act; P.L. 118-229) amended the MSA to revise timelines for the Secretary to review requesters' spend plans and timelines related to the disbursal of relief funds.

Direct federal financial assistance may be provided to fishers and fishing communities in the form of grants, direct payments, cooperative agreements, loans, or contracts. In addition to providing direct assistance following an economic loss, assistance may support efforts to prevent or lessen the effects of future disruptions to fisheries. These efforts may include fishery data collection, resource restoration, research, stock enhancement (e.g., through hatcheries), and fishing capacity reduction programs.

Although many observers posit that disaster assistance has provided much-needed aid to the fishing industry, others contend that disaster assistance programs sometimes fall short of expectations when funds are not disbursed in a timely manner, relief is not integrated with long-term fishery management objectives, and funds do not reach the people who may be in the greatest need of assistance. Stakeholders and experts also have raised concerns about the effectiveness of current fishery disaster assistance approaches in the context of climate change and in consideration of future disasters and other potential programs.

Many in Congress have shown consistent interest in fishery disaster assistance. In addition to the Coronavirus Aid, Relief, and Economic Security (CARES) Act (P.L. 116-136), which provided support to fishers affected by the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, several bills were introduced during the 117th through 119th Congresses related to fishery disaster assistance. In the 119th Congress, the Fisheries Modernization Act of 2025 (H.R. 4800) would make certain freshwater crawfish fisheries eligible for fishery disaster assistance, and the Protect American Fisheries Act (H.R. 6150) would include an "economic cause" as an allowable cause for a fishery disaster determination (similar legislation was introduced in the 118th Congress [H.R. 10010]). Previous versions of the FISHES Act (H.R. 5103/S. 4262) also were introduced in the 118th Congress. In the 117th Congress, previous versions of the FRDIA were introduced (H.R. 5453/S. 2923), and proposed refinements to the fishery disaster assistance process also were included in two separate bills (H.R. 59 and H.R. 4690), each of which would have comprehensively amended the MSA.

## Introduction

Marine ecosystems and their living marine resources may be subject to multiple environmental stressors, which can affect the coastal communities that depend on them. Natural stressors, including hurricanes, harmful algal blooms, marine heatwaves, and other phenomena, are anticipated to continue and possibly increase in their frequency and magnitude during forthcoming years, with potential effects to marine fisheries.[1](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn1) Additionally, human effects on marine environments—such as pollution, introduced species, and contributions to regional warming through greenhouse gas emissions—may compound natural stressors, with additional consequences to marine fisheries.[2](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn2) Furthermore, natural environmental variability in oceanic, climatic, and weather conditions may adversely impact marine fisheries.[3](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn3) Thus, fishing communities continue to be vulnerable to these phenomena, with potential economic impacts to fishers, processors, retailers, and other stakeholders.

Fishery resource disasters (also referred to as fishery disasters) occur when fishers endure economic hardships resulting from fish population declines or other disruptions to the fishery.[4](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn4) Marine fisheries' productivity and profitability may vary due to multiple stressors and causes.[5](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn5) These stressors can result in unexpected fishery resource declines; lead to fishery closures; and damage commercial infrastructure such as boats, shoreside processing facilities, and ports and marinas.[6](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn6) The federal government may provide disaster relief to assist fishers, fishing communities, and the fishing industry when they have been harmed by a fishery disaster.[7](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn7)

The Magnuson-Stevens Fishery Conservation and Management Act (MSA; 16 U.S.C. §1861a(a)) allows for a governor of a state, an official resolution of an Indian tribe,[8](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn8) or any other comparable elected or politically appointed representative of a fishing community, as determined by the Secretary of Commerce (the Secretary), to request a review to determine whether a fishery resource disaster occurred.[9](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn9) The MSA also authorizes the Secretary to initiate a review independently.[10](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn10) The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration's (NOAA's) National Marine Fisheries Service (NMFS), state agencies or tribes, and fishing communities compile the information (e.g., biological, economic) needed to make a determination.[11](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn11) When all necessary information has been obtained,[12](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn12) the Secretary determines whether a fishery disaster or failure has occurred using information provided by the requester or collected and analyzed by the Secretary.

In most cases, Congress has appropriated funds to support the fishing industry following a secretarial disaster determination.[13](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn13) Congress generally has appropriated funding in supplemental or annual appropriations as needs arise rather than in anticipation of future needs.[14](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn14) However, since FY2023, Congress has intermittently provided funding to NMFS for anticipated fishery resource disasters.[15](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn15) NMFS, states, regional commissions, and industry representatives often work together to distribute assistance to the fishing industry and to allocate funding among potential projects.[16](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn16)

Under its fishery resource disaster authorities, the federal government may provide direct financial assistance to fishers and fishing communities in the form of grants, direct payments, cooperative agreements, loans, or contracts.[17](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn17) The federal government also may provide indirect assistance that includes fishery data collection, resource restoration, research, stock enhancement (e.g., through hatcheries), and fishing capacity reduction programs to prevent or lessen the effects of future disruptions to fisheries.[18](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn18) NMFS and its partners have allocated fishing disaster funds to fisheries of the North Pacific (i.e., Alaska), Western Pacific (i.e., Hawaii and U.S. Pacific Island territories), West Coast, Gulf of America, U.S. Caribbean, South Atlantic, and Northeast regions.[19](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn19) Examples of fisheries with multiple commercial fishery failure or fishery resource disaster determinations include the Fraser River sockeye salmon fishery, Northeast multispecies groundfish fishery, California red sea urchin fishery, miscellaneous Gulf of America fisheries following hurricanes, New England shellfish fisheries, South Atlantic shrimp fisheries, Alaska salmon fisheries, and the Bering Sea snow crab fishery.[20](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn20)

Although many observers posit that disaster assistance has provided much-needed aid to the fishing industry, others—including some Members of Congress—contend that disaster assistance programs sometimes fall short of expectations. They note that funds may not be appropriated or disbursed in a timely manner, relief may not be integrated with long-term fishery management objectives, economic estimates of fishery disasters are inconsistent, and funds may not reach the people in the greatest need of assistance.[21](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn21) Stakeholders and experts also have raised concerns regarding anticipated future fisheries disasters that may be associated with climate change or other environmental stressors and the ability to provide continued relief to affected fishing communities.[22](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn22)

## Disaster Requirements and Procedures

The Department of Commerce provides fishery resource disaster assistance pursuant to the MSA, including amendments to the process in MSA through the 2022 Fishery Resource Disasters Improvement Act (FRDIA; P.L. 117-328, Division S, Title II), and the 2025 Fishery Improvement to Streamline untimely regulatory Hurdles post Emergency Situation Act (FISHES Act; P.L. 118-229). Assistance may be provided to state-managed fisheries, such as blue crab, and to fisheries under federal management, such as the Northeast multispecies fishery.[23](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn23) Differences exist under the law with regard to the allowable causes of a fishery resource disaster and the use of funds (see "[Amendments to the Magnuson-Stevens Fishery Conservation and Management Act](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#_Ref164256793)," below, for more information). Until the 2022 amendments of the FRDIA, fishery disasters had been declared under the MSA and sections of the Interjurisdictional Fisheries Act (IFA; 16 U.S.C. §4107, repealed) to provide managers with greater latitude when matching relief with different needs of the fishery and its participants.[24](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn24) As stipulated in the MSA, NMFS continues to review any outstanding fishery disaster requests received prior to these amendments under the two statutes (i.e., the MSA and IFA) and in accordance with its fishery disaster policy.[25](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn25) Any fishery disaster requests received post-FRDIA (i.e., after December 29, 2022) are reviewed under the amended MSA provisions.

### Amendments to the Magnuson-Stevens Fishery Conservation and Management Act

In 1996, Congress amended the MSA and added Section 312(a) to provide fishery disaster relief when a commercial fishery failure occurs as the result of a fishery resource disaster ([**Table 1**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#_Ref164776330)).[26](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn26) A *fishery resource disaster* is a disaster that is determined by the Secretary and is "an unexpected, large decrease in fish stock biomass or other change that results in a significant loss of access to the fishery resource, which may include loss of fishing vessels and gear for a substantial period of time and results in significant revenue loss or negative subsistence impact due to an allowable cause."[27](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn27) NMFS has administered fishery disaster assistance since the 1996 amendments and developed a policy directive in 2007 to provide guidance for the disaster relief process.[28](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn28) NMFS updated the policy in 2021.[29](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn29) In 2022, Congress amended the MSA to include additional provisions and specific timelines regarding the fishery disaster assistance process.[30](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn30) Subsequently, Congress further amended the MSA in January 2025 to include directives for the Secretary of Commerce and the Director of the White House Office of Management and Budget (OMB) with respect to timelines and the review of disaster assistance spend plans.[31](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn31)

**Notes:** IFA = Interjurisdictional Fisheries Act (16 U.S.C. §4107, repealed); MSA = Magnuson-Stevens Fishery Conservation and Management Act (16 U.S.C. §1861a(a) and 16 U.S.C. §1864, repealed). NMFS still applies the repealed MSA and IFA statutes to all requests for determination received prior to the enactment of the Fishery Resource Disasters Improvement Act (FRDIA; P.L. 117-328, Division S, Title II). As of September 11, 2026, one request received prior to the enactment of the FRDIA still is pending. The types of assistance and use under the MSA also apply to displaced fishery employees (i.e., individuals who are, or were, employed in a commercial, charter, or Indian tribe fishery for which the Secretary of Commerce has determined that a fishery resource disaster has occurred).

The fishery disaster determination process begins at the Secretary of Commerce's discretion, following the request of the governor of an affected state, an official resolution of an Indian tribe, or the request of a comparable elected or politically appointed representative.[32](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn32) The Secretary determines whether a fishery resource disaster has occurred, depending on three factors. First, there must be a fishery resource disaster, as per the definition under statute.[33](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn33) Second, under the MSA, the cause of the fishery resource disaster must be a natural cause,[34](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn34) an *anthropogenic* (i.e.,man-made) cause,[35](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn35) a combination of a natural cause and an anthropogenic cause,[36](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn36) or an undetermined cause([**Table 1**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#_Ref164776330)).[37](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn37) Finally, there must be significant revenue loss or negative subsistence impact from the commercial fishery disaster.[38](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn38) Any allowable cause for a fishery disaster determination is to have occurred not more than five years prior to the date of a request for determination.[39](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn39) Furthermore, any fishery subject to *overfishing* in any of the three years prior to the date of determination is not eligible for fishery disaster assistance unless the Secretary determines overfishing was not a contributing factor to the fishery resource disaster.[40](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn40)

The Secretary may initiate—or be asked to initiate by request—a review to determine whether a fishery resource disaster has occurred in response to presumed effects of a particular event or allowable cause on a given fishery.[41](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn41) The MSA requires that a complete request for a fishery resource disaster include information describing the fishery and how it and its users were harmed.[42](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn42) A request for determination may occur no later than (1) one year after the date of the conclusion of the fishing season; (2) two years after the date of the conclusion of the fishing season, in the case of a distinct cause that occurs during more than one consecutive fishing season; or (3) in the case of a complete fishing closure, one year after the date of determination for that closure by the Secretary.[43](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn43) The Secretary typically directs the appropriate Regional Administrator for NMFS to collect and analyze information such as fishery characteristics; stock assessments; fishery surveys; estimates of mortality and economic information such as landings data, revenue, number of participants, and employments; and additional information relevant to the fishery, as included in the MSA.[44](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn44) These data are used to determine the magnitude of the disaster and the relationship between underlying causes and the alleged fishery disaster.[45](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn45) The magnitude of the disaster may be measured by the percentage decline in landings and revenues, the number of fishers affected, loss of habitat, and lost or restricted fishing time.[46](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn46) Depending on the circumstances, NMFS scientists typically conduct the analysis in consultation with the state(s) using information and data provided by the state(s).[47](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn47) According to the MSA and NMFS, a reasonably predictable, foreseeable, and recurrent fishery resource cycle of variations in species distribution or stock abundance does not constitute a fishery resource disaster.[48](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn48)

The MSA, as amended by the FRDIA and the FISHES Act, establishes specific timelines for the Secretary to complete certain actions when making a determination regarding a fishery resource disaster and when disbursing relief funds.[49](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn49) The Secretary is to provide an interim response to a request for determination within 20 days after receipt of the fishery resource disaster request.[50](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn50) The Secretary is to complete the review within 120 days after receipt of a complete request and to notify the requester of the determination within 14 days after conclusion of the review.[51](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn51) If funds are available, the Secretary is to notify the public and affected fishing communities of their availability within 14 days after the date of appropriation or determination of a disaster.[52](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn52) The Secretary also may extend the notification deadline by 90 days to evaluate and make determinations on eligible requests.[53](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn53)

The MSA states that the Secretary is to apply 12-month revenue loss thresholds for determining whether a fishery resource disaster has occurred.[54](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn54) As covered under the MSA and NMFS policy, the Secretary is to review economic data to determine whether a commercial fishery failure exists.[55](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn55) The final decision depends on whether a significant number of people engaged in the fishery have suffered economic hardship as a result of the fishery resource disaster.[56](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn56) The MSA and the guidance specify the following thresholds based on the loss of annual fishing revenue (revenue loss for the fishery is based on fishing revenue "for the 12 months during which the fishery resource disaster occurred," which typically is compared with average annual fishing revenue over the most recent five-year period):[57](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn57)

* Revenue losses greater than 80% will result in the determination of a commercial fishery failure.
* Revenue losses from 35% to 80% will be further evaluated to determine the severity of losses.
* Revenue losses less than 35% will not be eligible for determination of a commercial fishery failure, except where the Secretary determines there are special and unique circumstances that may justify considering and using a lower threshold in making the determination.[58](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn58)

Congress may use the authorization in the MSA to appropriate funds for financial assistance to harvesters and other affected parties either after the Secretary determines that a fishery resource disaster occurred or for future anticipated disasters.[59](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn59) After funds are appropriated, the affected state, community, or group must develop a spending plan that is evaluated by NMFS regional offices and that may be reviewed concurrently by OMB upon request of the Secretary.[60](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn60) The Secretary is to determine whether a submitted spend plan is complete and provide notice of the determination to the requester within 10 days.[61](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn61) At the same time, if the Secretary determines that the spend plan is incomplete, the Secretary is to provide a detailed description of the information required for completion.[62](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn62) The MSA also states that funds are to be made available to affected parties within 90 days after the date that the Secretary receives a complete spend plan.[63](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn63)

Funding for a fishery resource disaster under the MSA may be used to address a broad variety of needs, including an assessment of the social and economic effects of the failure, assistance to the community, and projects to restore the fishery or prevent reoccurrence. Fishers and fishing communities may receive direct assistance in the form of grants, direct payments, cooperative agreements, loans, or contracts.[64](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn64) The federal government also may provide indirect assistance where funds may be used for fishery data collection, habitat and resource restoration, research, and fishing capacity reduction programs (e.g., vessel, permit, and gear buybacks),[65](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn65) among other uses.[66](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn66) Displaced fishery employees affected by the fishery resource disaster may carry out these types of efforts where appropriate.[67](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn67) Other forms of past indirect fishery-related assistance have included the formation of a fisheries research trust, economic planning grants, and research grants.[68](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn68)

In most circumstances, the federal share of assistance carried out under MSA Section 312(a) cannot be greater than 75% of the cost of relief activities; the other 25% is usually provided by the state or other local entity.[69](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn69) The MSA allows for certain exceptions of these nonfederal share requirements (i.e., assistance to subsistence or tribal fisheries or direct assistance to affected parties to alleviate economic losses).[70](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn70) The Secretary also may waive the nonfederal share requirements if he or she determines that no reasonable means are available for the recipient to meet the nonfederal share requirement or that 100% federal financing is in the public's best interest.[71](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn71) In some cases, interstate marine fishery commissions administer claims and disburse funds to fishing communities.[72](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn72) The MSA also includes limitations on federal and state administrative expenses related to these efforts.[73](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn73)

### Other Potential Sources of Assistance

When businesses suffer economic injuries from a disaster, the Small Business Administration (SBA) also may determine whether a disaster declaration is warranted.[74](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn74) For example, when a red tide algae bloom required closure of the Maine shellfish fishery in 2005, SBA evaluated the impact on small businesses and determined a disaster declaration was justified. The declaration makes affected businesses eligible for Economic Injury Disaster Loans.[75](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn75) The loan program's purpose is to provide working capital at low interest rates to assist businesses harmed by a disaster in their recovery.[76](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn76)

The Economic Development Administration (EDA) provides community grants and revolving loan funds to help distressed communities.[77](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn77) EDA has assisted fishing communities through its Public Works Program by funding port and harbor improvements. EDA's Economic Adjustment Assistance Program helps communities adjust to economic disruptions through support of business development, planning, and market research. Industries that have been adversely affected by increased imports of similar or competitive goods (e.g., foreign shrimp) have been able to seek technical assistance under EDA's Trade Adjustment Assistance for Firms (TAAF) program.[78](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn78) According to EDA, as of July 1, 2022, the agency stopped accepting new petitions for the TAAF program following the expiration of "key portions" of the program's statutory authorization.[79](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn79)

Additionally, small fishing-related businesses may qualify for certain federal grants available through the U.S. Department of Labor or the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA). These grants, together with those from the EDA and SBA loans, may help address economic injury and physical damage.[80](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn80)

Fishers and seafood producers are not currently eligible for certain U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA)-administered loans and grants. Some Members of Congress have proposed legislation in the 119th Congress that would extend USDA loans and grants to include fishers and seafood producers as eligible recipients (e.g., S. 4236). In April 2026, USDA created its Office of Seafood, which among its listed roles is "coordinating across USDA agencies to ensure fishermen are integrated into USDA programs."[81](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn81) Reportedly, the office also is developing a seafood strategy in collaboration with NOAA and other federal agencies and is conducting outreach about funding opportunities.[82](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn82) Additional developments regarding this office's activities, including its codification as proposed in H.R. 7567 (119th Congress; as passed the House), remain to be seen.

Further, some Members of Congress have begun exploring other forms of financial assistance for fishing communities, such as directing NOAA and the National Academy of Public Administration to investigate the feasibility of a fishery disaster insurance program for "seafood harvesters."[83](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn83) Commercial insurance products for fishery disasters are not currently available. Some scientists and stakeholders also have been exploring the applicability of such a program for certain fisheries,[84](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn84) and note multiple considerations regarding the topic (e.g., variability in demand and applicability among fisheries; scale of implementation; uncertainty in forecasting future yields and whether wild-caught fishery populations can be effectively insured).[85](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn85)

## Secretarial Disaster Determinations

Since 1994, the Secretary of Commerce has made 144 different fishery disaster determinations, of which 130 were original determinations and 14 were continuations to existing determinations.[86](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn86) Since fishery disaster assistance began, the Secretary has issued determinations valuing approximately $1.94 billion (approximately $2.61 billion in 2026 USD),[87](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn87) and Congress has appropriated nearly $2.1 billion (approximately $2.93 billion in 2026 USD) for fishery disaster relief.[88](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn88) Approximately 33% of the 144 different fishery disaster determinations (47 cases) were made under both the MSA and the IFA. The Secretary has "denied" 20 requests for fishery disaster assistance since 1994, in addition to 1 listed determination that a request was "ineligible."[89](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn89) As of August 2026, 24 disaster requests were pending determination; 23 of these 24 requests were submitted following enactment of the FRDIA.

Cumulatively, most relief funds have gone to the West Coast and Alaska regions, particularly for salmon fisheries ([**Table 2**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#_Ref175906473), [**Figure 1**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#_Ref175648288)). The then-named "Gulf of Mexico" region received substantive amounts of fishery disaster relief during the 2000s and 2010s,[90](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn90) primarily in response to fishery disasters associated with hurricanes. Relief funds also have been provided for New England and Alaskan groundfish fisheries and for non-species-specific regional fisheries collectively (e.g., for Florida, Puerto Rico, and U.S. Virgin Islands fisheries in response to Hurricanes Irma and Maria in 2017). For determinations since 2020, approximately 84% of all fisheries disaster funds have gone to Alaska to address salmon, Bering Sea crab, and groundfish (e.g., Pacific cod) fisheries.

Fishery resource disasters are diverse with respect to their causes and scope. Most declarations have resulted from natural events such as hurricanes, floods, changes in ocean conditions, or algal blooms such as red tide.[91](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn91) Additionally, 1 declaration resulted from human-associated causes (i.e., the 2010 Deepwater Horizon oil spill) and 24 declarations resulted from unknown causes.[92](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn92) In coastal areas, hurricanes may damage fishing industry infrastructure, which may include vessels, docks, fish houses, and related businesses. Even if the fishery resource remains abundant, harvesting, processing, and transport to markets may not be possible until repairs are undertaken and basic services are restored. In addition to the costs of repairs and the replacement of equipment and gear, lost fishing time can be costly.

Hurricanes also may damage natural resources such as oyster beds by depositing silt and debris or introducing large amounts of freshwater, affecting oyster survival and recruitment.[93](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn93) Algal blooms, such as red tide, can render seafood toxic and may result in fishery closures.[94](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn94) Under these conditions, fisheries may be completely shut down for months until toxin levels in shellfish decline to acceptable levels.[95](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn95)

Declines in fishery resource abundance often are caused by multiple factors, such as natural environmental variations, human effects on the environment (e.g., pollution), and overfishing.[96](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn96) For example, salmon fisheries are sensitive to natural changes in oceanic conditions.[97](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn97) However, salmon abundance in the Pacific Northwest also has been affected where dams, irrigation, grazing, mining, and forestry practices have degraded salmon habitat.[98](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn98) Overfishing by itself may not be used to qualify for a fishery failure determination, because it is usually within the control of fishery managers (e.g., through setting annual catch limits and accountability measures).[99](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn99) However, a fishery resource disaster caused by natural or undetermined causes—criteria that may be considered by the Secretary—may be exacerbated by overfishing.[100](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn100) In these cases, assistance may include efforts to rationalize (decrease) fishing capacity.[101](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn101) For example, overfishing contributed to fish population declines in several resource disaster cases, such as the New England multispecies fishery and the Pacific groundfish fishery.[102](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn102) In these two cases, fish abundance decreased significantly and stock rebuilding has required substantial decreases in harvest.[103](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn103) However, the Secretary determined that other factors beyond the control of fishery managers played a role in these fishery resource disaster cases.[104](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn104)

### State Role

States are frequently active partners throughout the fishery disaster process, often requesting the Secretary to declare a fishery failure, providing related data, and disbursing relief to fishers and related businesses.[105](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn105) Under MSA, a disaster request is to include a spend plan that would address the causes of the disaster and may include additional details (e.g., statement of work, budget information).[106](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn106) Relief funding is often provided directly to states or through regional commissions, such as the Pacific States Marine Fisheries Commission (PSMFC) or the Gulf States Marine Fisheries Commission, which may work to develop a spend plan and grant submission for NOAA.[107](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn107) For example, the PSMFC has collected information about West Coast or Alaskan licensed fishers for inclusion in a spend plan or grant application following specific disaster determinations and may coordinate the mailing of relief checks to affected parties.[108](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn108) In another example, in 2008, the Oregon Department of Agriculture—in cooperation with related agencies and nonprofit organizations, such as the Oregon Salmon Commission—planned and coordinated the distribution of Oregon salmon troll fishery relief.[109](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn109) In addition to matching funds, state governments may provide disaster relief funding, although historically such funding has been limited.[110](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn110)

### Fishing Capacity Reduction Programs

Historically, many U.S. fisheries have been overcapitalized—investments in fishing capacity became greater than that needed to harvest the fishery resource on a sustainable basis. Fishing capacity reduction, often referred to as *buyback programs*, has been a prominent feature of several disaster relief programs and is included in the MSA as an eligible use of fishery disaster assistance for a commercial fishery.[111](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn111) If fishery disaster assistance funds are used as part of a capacity reduction program, the Secretary is to have determined that "adequate conservation and management measures are in place" for the fishery.[112](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn112) Capacity reduction is usually accomplished through the direct purchase and permanent retirement of fishing vessels, gear, and/or fishing permits.[113](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn113) Programs may be funded by the federal government, by fishers who remain in the fishery, or by a combination of both.[114](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn114) The general objectives of buyback programs are to provide immediate relief to fishers, decrease the level of fishing effort to improve the profitability of the remaining fishing fleet, and conserve the resource.[115](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn115)

The MSA requires certain conditions with respect to a vessel under a fishing capacity reduction program when providing fishery disaster assistance. These conditions include that the Secretary shall prohibit use of the vessel for fishing in any waters and that the vessel is to be either scrapped; donated for purposes of research, education, or training; or used for another nonfishing purpose provided that the vessel cannot reenter any fishery anywhere in the world.[116](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn116)

Although capacity reduction programs attempt to provide long-term benefits to those who decide to remain in the fishery, experts note that poorly crafted programs may result in little or no benefit at the expense of taxpayers.[117](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn117) Buyback programs' effectiveness in reducing fishing capacity can depend on whether the remaining fishers have an incentive to continue investing in boats and gear. Often there is also *latent* fishing effort—boats and gear with permits to fish that are inactive or only marginally utilized in the fishery. The exit of some vessels via a buyback program may encourage inactive or marginally utilized vessels to reenter the fishery more actively, offsetting any reduction in fishing capacity from the buyback program. Furthermore, the first to accept buybacks may be the least efficient vessels in the fleet. These factors can result in fleet reductions that are relatively modest yet expensive, because only the oldest and least efficient units are taken out of production. Although capacity reduction can be a means to ease financial hardship caused by a fishing disaster, lasting benefits may depend on better recognition of the motivations of vessel owners and fishers.[118](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn118)

### Selected Case Studies of Fishery Disasters

Circumstances regarding particular fishery disaster determinations may differ according to the cause of a given disaster, the specific fishery or fisheries affected, and management actions preceding or following the disaster, among other factors. The below examples include information regarding singular or recurrent fishery disaster determinations by the Secretary for selected regions and fisheries.

#### Alaskan Crab Fisheries

The Secretary has issued recurrent multiyear fishery disaster declarations for Alaskan red king crab, snow crab, and tanner crab fisheries since 2000.[119](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn119) Initially, fishery disaster declarations for Alaskan snow crab were issued for the 2000-2003 and 2005-2008 fishing seasons, which were attributed to natural disasters of unknown causes. Since the 2019 fishing season, annual fishery disaster determinations for Alaskan crab fisheries have followed, including specific determinations for Norton Sound red king crab (2019-2021) and Bering Sea tanner crab (2019-2020), and for Bering Sea crab fisheries (e.g., snow crab) in general (2021-2024). Cumulatively, all Alaskan crab disaster declarations have totaled approximately $338.0 million (in 2026 USD). The Secretary has attributed all of these disaster events to natural causes. On April 29, 2024, the Secretary issued a disaster declaration for the 2023-2024 Alaskan Bering Sea snow crab fishery following a December 2023 request from the governor, for which $39.5 million was allocated in September 2024 ($41.9 million in 2026 USD) and an additional $75.2 million was allocated in June 2026.[120](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn120) As of August 2026, a determination by the Secretary is pending for the 2024-2025 Alaskan Bering Sea snow crab fishery following a fall 2025 request from the governor.[121](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn121)

Related to the 2021-2024 determinations for Bering Sea crab fisheries, Alaskan snow crab harvest declined by 88% between the 2020-2021 and 2021-2022 fishing seasons.[122](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn122) This decline in landings coincided with a decline in the Bering Sea snow crab population of 10 billion individuals from 2018 to 2021, which experts attributed to a marine heatwave that led to mass crab starvation and mortality.[123](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn123) Given this decline and the overfished status of Alaskan snow crab, the Alaska Department of Fish and Game, in consultation with the NPFMC and NOAA, closed the snow crab fishery for the fishing years 2022-2024.[124](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn124) Additionally, continued low abundance of Alaskan tanner crab and red king crab was observed during this period.[125](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn125) On December 16, 2022, in response to a request from the governor of Alaska, the Secretary declared a fishery resource disaster for Alaska Bering Sea crab fisheries (2021-2022 season) of approximately $94.6 million (approximately $107.6 million in 2026 USD).[126](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn126) On the same date, the Secretary additionally declared a disaster for Alaska Bering Sea crab fisheries (2022-2023 season) of approximately $96.7 million (approximately $110.1 million in 2026 USD).[127](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn127) According to the Alaska Department of Fish and Game and the PSMFC, funds for this disaster were distributed to parties during 2025-2026.[128](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn128)

#### Pacific Tribal and Nontribal Salmon Fisheries

Since 1994, the Secretary of Commerce has issued multiple fishery disaster declarations for West Coast and Alaskan tribal and nontribal salmon fisheries totaling approximately $1.09 billion (in 2026 USD).[129](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn129) All Pacific salmon disaster declarations have been due to natural causes (e.g., drought, poor ocean conditions) or a natural disaster of unknown causes. Based on 2026 USD amounts, approximately 43% of all Pacific salmon disaster relief funds have gone to Alaskan salmon fisheries since 1994; during the same period, approximately 10% of funds have gone to Washington salmon fisheries and approximately 6% have gone to California salmon fisheries. Approximately 27% of all Pacific salmon disaster relief funds have been appropriated for fishing disasters that affected all three West Coast states jointly. The remainder have been appropriated for Oregon and California or Oregon and Washington salmon fisheries jointly (approximately 13%) or for Oregon salmon fisheries separately (approximately 1%).

The majority of Pacific salmon disaster relief funds generally have been appropriated for nontribal salmon fisheries. The greatest amounts for tribal fisheries—that is, amounts not combined with relief funds to the state—have gone solely to Washington tribal salmon fisheries since 2009 (approximately $55.6 million from 2009 to 2024 in 2026 USD) and solely to California tribal salmon fisheries since 2016 (approximately $18.4 million from 2016 to 2023 in 2026 USD). In some cases, the Secretary has issued joint disaster declarations for both tribal (or Alaska native) and nontribal (or non-Alaska native) fisheries, totaling approximately $30.2 million (in 2026 USD) for Alaskan salmon fisheries and nearly $298 million (in 2026 USD) for West Coast fisheries since 1994.

Among Pacific coast subregions, the Secretary most frequently has issued fishery disaster declarations for tribal and nontribal salmon fisheries occurring in the Klamath and Fraser Rivers.[130](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn130) The Secretary issued the first of the Klamath River declarations, at $60.4 million, on July 6, 2006, for the 2005-2006 California and Oregon salmon fishing seasons. Subsequently, the Secretary declared a fishing disaster on January 18, 2017, for the Yurok Tribe Klamath River Chinook salmon fishery during the 2016 fishing season; the Secretary also issued annual disaster declarations for this tribal fishery during the Yurok Tribe 2018-2022 fishing seasons. These declarations for the Yurok Tribe specifically have totaled approximately $18.4 million (in 2026 USD). Determinations for the 2023 and 2024 fishing seasons still are pending following respective April 11, 2024, and January 15, 2025, requests by the Yurok Tribe. Additionally, the Secretary issued a disaster declaration on September 24, 2018, for the Oregon and California Klamath River fall-run Chinook salmon fishery during the 2016-2017 fishing seasons to the Yurok and Hoopa Tribes, and the States of Oregon and California, at approximately $8.9 million (approximately $11.8 million in 2026 USD). A determination also is pending for the Klamath River salmon fishery during the 2024 fishing season following an April 3, 2025, request from the Pulikla Tribe of Yurok People. On October 30, 2023, the Secretary issued a disaster declaration of $20.6 million (approximately $22.5 million in 2026 USD) for California Sacramento River Fall Chinook and Klamath River Fall Chinook ocean, and inland salmon fisheries for the 2023 fishing season. A determination for the California Sacramento River and Klamath River fall-run Chinook salmon fisheries during the 2024 season is still pending following a December 12, 2024, request.

For tribal and nontribal fisheries in the Fraser River, the Secretary first issued disaster declarations for Fraser River sockeye salmon on November 13, 2002, corresponding to the 1999-2001 fishing seasons for the Lummi Nation and the State of Washington.[131](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn131) These declarations were followed by a disaster declaration continuation applied to the 2007 sockeye salmon fishing season for the Lummi Nation and the State of Washington and extending to the Makah Tribe, Lower Elwha Klallam Tribe, Tulalip Tribes, and Swinomish Tribal Community. On May 27, 2011, the Secretary issued a further continuation of the Fraser River sockeye salmon disaster for the 2009 Lummi Nation fishing season. The Secretary also issued a separate disaster declaration for that fishery on January 14, 2014, as applied to the 2013 fishing season for eight separate tribes and the State of Washington. Since then, the Secretary has issued additional disaster declarations for Fraser River tribal salmon fisheries (i.e., for the Lummi Nation; Elwha, Makah, and Swinomish Tribes; Swinomish and Tulalip Tribes; and the Port Gamble S'Klallam Tribe) during the 2014-2020 fishing seasons totaling approximately $22.0 million (in 2026 USD). Three additional determinations for Fraser River tribal salmon fisheries are still pending. Collectively, declarations including the Fraser River have totaled approximately $28.4 million (in 2026 USD) for tribal and nontribal salmon fisheries.

#### Red Tide Events in New England, Texas, and Florida

Red tide has been a recurring problem for shellfish fisheries in northern New England.[132](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn132) Blooms of the algae *Alexandrium fundyense*, commonly referred to as *red tide*, produce a toxin that is ingested by and concentrated in the tissues of shellfish such as clams, mussels, and oysters.[133](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn133) When the concentration of the algae is high, shellfish beds must be closed because shellfish may cause paralytic shellfish poisoning, which can be toxic to humans when ingested.[134](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn134) In 2005, shellfish bed closures spanned from Canada to Martha's Vineyard, MA, as a result of a red tide bloom, partially attributed to the aftermath of May 2005 northeaster storms.[135](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn135) On June 23, 2005, NOAA announced a commercial fishery failure determination for the New England region's shellfish fishery.[136](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn136) In 2006, the Emergency Supplemental Appropriations Act for Defense, the Global War on Terror, and Hurricane Recovery, 2006 (P.L. 109-234), appropriated funds related to the consequences from hurricanes of the 2005 season, from which NOAA allocated $4 million to assist fishers affected by the red tide bloom.

During 2008, a red tide event impacted a widespread area of ocean waters off New England.[137](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn137) On November 14, 2008, the Secretary determined a commercial fishery failure had occurred because the bloom triggered closures of shellfish fisheries.[138](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn138) The Consolidated Security, Disaster Assistance, and Continuing Appropriations Act, 2009 (P.L. 110-329), provided up to $5 million to assist the fishing industry and for research and monitoring related to red tide events. On December 22, 2010, the Secretary determined that red tide caused another fishery failure in the Maine shellfish fishery during the 2009 season, but Congress did not appropriate funding for this event.[139](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn139)

More recently, large-scale blooms of the toxic algae *Karenia brevis*, also commonly referred to as red tide, have affected Texas oyster fisheries and multiple Florida fisheries (i.e., mullet, red grouper, and stone crab).[140](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn140) During the 2011 season, Texas commercial oyster fisheries were closed due to red tide.[141](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn141) According to a March 26, 2012, request letter, estimated ex-vessel losses to commercial oyster fishers directly were in excess of $8 million,[142](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn142) with greater than 1,800 families along the Texas coast estimated to have been affected by the closure. The Secretary denied the request for a fishery disaster declaration on October 5, 2012, due to revenue losses not exceeding the 35% threshold (refer to "[Amendments to the Magnuson-Stevens Fishery Conservation and Management Act](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#_Ref164851148)," above).[143](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn143) Similarly, following the 2018-2019 Florida red tide events, the Secretary denied a request for a fishery disaster declaration on August 10, 2021, because the revenue losses did not meet the required minimum threshold.[144](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn144)

#### California Dungeness and Rock Crab Fishery (Harmful Algal Blooms)

In early November 2015, the California Dungeness crab and rock crab fisheries were closed due to a harmful algal bloom along the California coast.[145](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn145) The California Office of Environmental Health Hazard Assessment and the California Department of Health determined there were unsafe levels of domoic acid in crab tissue.[146](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn146) Domoic acid is a neurotoxin, and when ingested by humans it can cause nausea, diarrhea, vomiting, memory loss, seizures, and sometimes death.[147](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn147) In response, the California Department of Fish and Game closed commercial and recreational crab fisheries in the affected areas.[148](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn148) The closure occurred during the peak months of the fishery from December through January and was persistent, as many areas remained closed through June 2016.[149](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn149)

The initial estimate of the economic impact of the harmful algal bloom based on average commercial landings over the previous five years was $48.3 million for Dungeness crab and $376,000 for rock crab.[150](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn150) On February 9, 2016, the California governor requested a commercial fishery failure determination.[151](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn151) On January 18, 2017, the Secretary of Commerce found that the Dungeness crab and rock crab fisheries met requirements for a commercial fishery failure under both the MSA and the IFA. That same date, the Secretary also declared a fishery resource disaster for the Washington Dungeness crab fishery during the Quileute Tribe's 2015 fishing season.[152](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn152)

On February 9, 2018, Congress included $200 million in the Bipartisan Budget Act of 2018 (P.L. 115-123) for fishery resource disasters declared by the Secretary in 2017. Of the total, $25.8 million was allocated to provide assistance to the California Dungeness crab and rock crab fisheries affected by harmful algal blooms. In August 2018, the draft crab disaster relief spending plan was finalized, dividing total funding among mitigation of future disasters (10%), direct payments (89%), and administration (1%).[153](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn153) On May 22, 2019, the Pacific States Marine Fisheries Commission announced it had received funds to be disbursed to crab fishers.[154](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn154)

## Issues for Congress

Commercial fisheries are strongly influenced by environmental conditions that affect the abundance and distribution of fishery resources and fishing infrastructure. Changes in these conditions often take place suddenly with little or no warning, such as in the cases of severe storms, oil spills, and harmful algal blooms. Disaster relief programs may help businesses that have been harmed by these events and can address disruptions to fisheries by providing assistance until conditions return to normal. As Congress continues to debate and respond to fishery disasters, several issues have emerged related to the nature of commercial fisheries and disaster relief programs, including (1) timing relief to meet crucial needs, (2) relating disaster relief to long-term fisheries management, (3) considering future fishery resource disasters as related to climate change, (4) determining who benefits from relief, and (5) considering other related sectors.

### Timing of Relief

The delivery of disaster relief depends on the Secretary's determination that a fishery failure has occurred and on Congress's appropriation of relief funding. Additionally, the Secretary and OMB, upon request, must approve a complete spend plan before funds can be disbursed to affected parties. Given these required procedures, stakeholders have expressed concerns regarding the amount of time it can take for affected parties to receive relief funds following a request for a determination.

Historically, approximately one-third of fishery resource disaster determinations have been made within six months of the initial request and approximately 57% have been made within one year.[155](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn155) The remaining 43% of determinations have taken anywhere from one year to approximately four years post-request.[156](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn156) For the 58 determinations made since 2021, average determination time was approximately 1.4 years (range: approximately 2 months to 3.8 years).[157](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn157) In a 2025 evaluation of federal fishery resource disaster assistance, the U.S. Government Accountability Office (GAO) found that NMFS generally took three years to disburse funds for "some of the 111 requests it received since 2014."[158](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn158) GAO also noted that NMFS is altering the program in an effort to reduce the process to one year and has taken certain actions to address challenges related to processing times (e.g., redirecting staff from other programs to work on the fishery resource disaster program).[159](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn159) In September 2026, NMFS announced its priorities in support of Executive Order 14276, "Restoring American Seafood Competitiveness," including conducting a review of the fishery disaster determination and funding process and exploring pathways to expand access to other "government market and disaster support mechanisms."[160](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn160)

Information related to the scope of the disaster usually needs to be compiled by the fishing industry, state and local governments, and NMFS (refer to [**Table 1**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#_Ref164776330) and the section on the "[Amendments to the Magnuson-Stevens Fishery Conservation and Management Act](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#_Ref164862174)," above). Difficulties in concluding this task can be compounded by the lack of data and readily available economic studies. In cases such as Hurricane Katrina (2005) or Hurricane Michael (2018), it was immediately clear that a disaster had occurred and the Secretary made a determination within one to two weeks of each hurricane's landfall.[161](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn161) In other fishery resource disaster cases—such as those at the Long Island, NY, hard shell clam fishery (2008); Northern Mariana Islands fisheriesfollowing a super typhoon (2003); and the Florida shark fishery (2008)—it took two to three years before the Secretary made a determination (in these cases, a determination to deny the requests).[162](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn162)

Provisions allowing for faster determinations or funding in the case of a catastrophic regional fishery disaster were repealed in the FRDIA. As part of the amendments in the FRDIA, Congress removed the requirement for the Secretary to conduct rapid assessments to inform a determination for the case of a catastrophic regional fishery disaster.[163](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn163) Further, the NOAA Assistant Administrator for Fisheries testified in a November 2021 hearing that some circumstances, such as those proposed in H.R. 4690 (117th Congress), could limit the Secretary's discretion to move swiftly, particularly following significant events such as hurricanes, and that additional requirements, among other elements, "could further complicate things" regarding the pace of providing relief funds.[164](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn164)

In January 2009, NOAA proposed regulations to govern requests for determinations of fishery resource disasters in accordance with the 2006 amendments to the MSA.[165](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn165) Sections 600.1503 and 600.1505 of the proposed rule included a proposed evaluation process for a "fast track" determination in cases "where an 80% decline in revenues is substantiated"; in these cases, the Secretary would "send the requester a positive determination within 30 days of receiving evidence substantiating a decrease of 80%." The proposed rule continued, "In the instance of a 'standard track' determination, the Secretary will send the requester a letter of positive or negative determination as soon as practicable." NOAA elected to issue an internal policy for fishery disaster assistance instead and withdrew the proposed rule in May 2013.[166](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn166) Thus, no "fast track" option was implemented and determinations and their associated appropriations could be subject to delay. Amendments through the FRDIA include specific timelines for reviews, but the availability of appropriated funds and time to final approval of a completed spend plan also may create delays in finalizing relief payments.

After the Secretary declares a fishery resource disaster, funding is dependent on appropriations by Congress. In most cases, Congress has provided funding for declared disasters, but the timing of appropriations has varied considerably. For some approved determinations, such as the Dungeness crab and rock crab fisheries, pink salmon in Alaska, Fraser River Sockeye salmon, and Washington coastal salmon, Congress appropriated funds more than two years after the Secretary's determination.[167](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn167) Hurricanes Katrina and Rita fishery disaster funding was appropriated more than nine months after the Gulf fishery disaster was declared in September 2005, although many in the industry believed the greatest need occurred immediately after the hurricanes, when fishers lost fishing opportunities because of disrupted markets and damaged infrastructure, vessels, and gear.[168](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn168) Although the full dimensions of the disaster and the level and scope of resource needs remained uncertain immediately after the disaster, some fishers thought some basic aid should have been provided to members of the fishing industry at that time.

Fishers have expressed frustrations about ongoing expenses or economic hardships before relief payments are made and a reluctance to consider certain other funding sources during the interim, such as SBA loans.[169](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn169) Other fishers have communicated an unawareness of other relief opportunities beyond NMFS's fishery disaster assistance.[170](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn170) Still others have noted that the amounts ultimately received through a disaster declaration may be less than what was initially requested or anticipated.[171](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn171) Additionally, tribal and subsistence fishers have raised concerns about the adequacy and timeliness of disaster funds for tribal fisheries and of the vulnerability of their fisheries to future disasters.[172](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn172) In more recent examples, particularly for the higher volume of West Coast and Alaskan fishery disasters, some of that assistance was covered through $300 million in supplemental appropriations in the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2023 (P.L. 117-328). However, according to the Pacific States Marine Fisheries Commission and the Alaska Department of Fish and Game, funding for 10 declarations over the period of 2018-2024 was still awaiting full disbursement (i.e., mailing of relief payments to eligible parties) as of August 2026.[173](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn173)

#### Potential Options and Considerations Regarding Timing of Relief

For immediate needs following a fishery failure, some have advocated establishing a disaster fund with annual appropriations that could provide more immediate assistance.[174](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn174) For example, the Robert T. Stafford Disaster Relief and Emergency Assistance Act (P.L. 93-288) provides disaster assistance to state and local governments. FEMA provides the funds in various forms through its Disaster Relief Fund (DRF). The DRF is funded through regular appropriations acts using a formula that includes several factors, including historical disaster costs. As another example, some USDA agricultural disaster assistance programs receive mandatory appropriations of "such sums as necessary" with formulae defined in statute to define when benefits are triggered, their magnitude, and eligibility requirements, which may provide payments to farmers relatively quickly after a triggering event.[175](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn175) Fishery disaster assistance is sometimes included in NOAA's annual appropriations, such as the FY2018, FY2019, and FY2023 Consolidated Appropriations Acts, but in many years disaster assistance is not included in the agency's appropriations requests or annual appropriations acts passed by Congress.[176](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn176) Congress may consider whether creating a stepwise approach for issuing fishery disaster assistance to affected parties may be feasible using available information for a given affected fishery (e.g., revenue amounts from previous years, number of permitted individuals, annual catch limits for a given fishing season) rather than awaiting all information from the sector before the determination process begins. Furthermore, some stakeholders have expressed concerns about potential delays in determinations or assistance resulting from a lapse in appropriations. The Department of Commerce does not include the fishery disaster assistance program in its Lapse in Appropriations Contingency Plan.[177](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn177)

Others have considered the use of existing agriculture programs to supplement existing fishery disaster assistance. For example, S. 4236 in the 119th Congress would include fishers and seafood producers as eligible recipients for USDA ownership and operating loans and grants. The House-passed and Senate committee draft versions of a 2026 farm bill also include provisions to authorize USDA ownership and operating loans for commercial fishing.[178](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn178) In another example, during the 112th Congress, the Senate approved an amendment to S. 3240, the Agriculture Reform, Food, and Jobs Act of 2012 (the 2012 farm bill), which would have made commercial fishers eligible for emergency loans that are currently available to farmers. Emergency loans assist farmers who have suffered physical or production losses in disaster areas that are declared by the President.[179](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn179) The amendment was not included in the Agricultural Act of 2014 (P.L. 113-79). In April 2026, USDA established an Office of Seafood; its responsibilities include "coordinating across USDA agencies to ensure fishermen are integrated into USDA programs."[180](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn180) Some experts and fishers also have communicated that farm programs could serve more broadly as models for disaster assistance to fishers.[181](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn181)

In April 2025, GAO published an evaluation of federal fishery resource disaster assistance, which included three recommendations with respect to NMFS's tracking, timing, and capacity for responding to fishery resource disasters.[182](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn182) Specifically, GAO recommended that NMFS expand access to tracking information for staff working on fishery resource disasters; provide more detailed information on the NMFS website about the fishery resource disaster process; and assess the sufficiency of NMFS's staffing capacity for administering the fishery resource disaster program. According to GAO, as of August 2026, NMFS's implementation of these recommendations remains ongoing.[183](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn183) As reported by GAO, other stakeholders have suggested that fishery disaster assistance could be informed by other federal disaster assistance programs, including USDA's crop insurance program.[184](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn184) In GAO's review, it found that these other programs face various challenges and also have varying eligibility requirements, authorized funding uses, and design features compared to fishery disaster assistance.[185](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn185) GAO had previously made recommendations for executive action and identified matters for congressional consideration to improve the operation of each of these programs. These recommendations may be relevant in considering these programs as models for future fishery assistance.[186](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn186)

Some Members of Congress have proposed enhancing the transparency and traceability of fishery disaster assistance. In a December 21, 2023, letter to the NOAA Assistant Administrator for Fisheries, four Senators asked the agency to create an online tracking system to allow policymakers and stakeholders to follow all steps of disaster assistance requests through NOAA's and OMB's decisionmaking processes.[187](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn187) Some Senators also offered that substantive replies to any applicants or stakeholders requesting updates on the status of their request could be provided instead of an online tracker.[188](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn188) Bills in the 118th Congress (H.R. 5103/S. 4262) as introduced would have required the Director of OMB to approve a spend plan for fishery disaster assistance within 30 days (or deny a spend plan within 15 days) after the date that the Secretary submits the plan for approval.[189](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn189) Some, such as the Atlantic States Marine Fisheries Commission, expressed their support of H.R. 5103.[190](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn190) An amended version with directives for the Secretary and the Director of OMB was signed into law on January 4, 2025, as the FISHES Act (see "[Disaster Requirements and Procedures](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#_Ref188365632)").[191](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn191)

### Long-Term Management Approaches

Often, direct or indirect assistance to the fishing industry is part of a relief program. Some have criticized federal assistance because it can delay the readjustment (i.e., decrease in fishing) that may be needed for fisheries with excess harvesting capacity.[192](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn192) These critics argue that climatic and/or environmental conditions are often blamed for fish population declines that may have been caused by overfishing.[193](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn193)

Features of several programs, such as buybacks and training for fishers in other vocations, focus on concerns related to a need identified by managers to decrease fishing fleet size. When relief is provided—even when it includes a buyback program—greater numbers of fishers and more effort may remain in the fishery than might be sustainable in the long run.[194](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn194) Many fisheries managers assert that relief, such as vessel buybacks, needs to be more closely integrated with ongoing fisheries management objectives.[195](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn195) Other types of assistance that may provide long-term fishery benefits include habitat restoration and enhancement, marketing and promotion programs, and cooperative research.

Some have proposed that long-term measures and disaster planning should take place before disasters occur. In this way, more deliberate approaches to build resiliency may be considered and potentially enacted instead of emergency measures that fill short-run needs. Furthermore, stakeholders have requested that regulatory agencies, such as the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (USACE), consider fisheries' concerns in decisionmaking or water management processes.[196](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn196) For example, on October 30, 2019, the Secretary declared a fishery disaster following the 2019 opening of the Mississippi Bonnet-Carre spillway to divert floodwater from New Orleans.[197](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn197) The influx of freshwater from the opening to the Gulf affected Louisiana, Mississippi, and Alabama fisheries and led to a U.S. District Court finding that USACE is to consult with NOAA on subsequent openings of the spillway.[198](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn198) USACE has disputed this requirement and appealed the ruling in March 2023.[199](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn199) A further decision is pending.

### Fishery Resource Disasters and Climate Change

Stakeholders, some Members of Congress, and other parties have raised concerns about the impacts of climate change to fisheries resources. Climate change is anticipated to intensify multiple stressors that may affect fishery populations, such as warming events, hurricanes, harmful algal blooms, invasive species, and other phenomena.[200](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn200) Recurrent poor harvests and fisheries closures have been observed in recent years (e.g., Alaskan snow crab, Pacific cod), with associated fishery disaster determinations for those periods identified as being due to natural causes.[201](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn201) Some experts and stakeholders have characterized the current fishery disaster approach as being only a reactive one and have raised concerns about whether NMFS is considering proactive approaches to address climate change impacts to fisheries and future fishery disaster relief (e.g., prioritizing particular regions or fisheries, considering future vulnerabilities).[202](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn202) Furthermore, stakeholders express concerns about future climate-related catastrophic events and that the FRDIA repealed the ability for the Secretary to issue quick-turnaround determinations in response to catastrophic events.[203](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn203) Some also note that wealthier fishers typically have greater financial resources to adapt their fishing practices in response to climate-related effects on fisheries compared with small vessel owners (e.g., abilities to cover fuel expenses associated with fishing at greater distances, to afford multiple fishing permits, or to modify their vessels to diversify fishing gears and targeted species in response to change).[204](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn204) Some organizations, such as the California Ocean Science Trust, have organized symposia and workshops to consider the future of fishery disaster assistance in light of climate change.[205](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn205) NMFS also has carried out climate vulnerability assessments in several of its regions for fisheries species and fishing communities.[206](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn206) That information may be useful for prioritizing particular species, locations, and coastal communities with respect to current and future disaster assistance needs, such as in approaches for agricultural disaster assistance. Congress may consider future directives to NMFS for fishery disaster assistance, such as prioritization exercises, engagements with stakeholders, or scenario planning accounting for regional vulnerabilities to future fishery resource disasters. Congress also may consider whether permitting flexibilities for vulnerable species or in particular regions may be warranted, including considerations for different vessel sizes.

### Who Benefits?

Who benefits from disaster funding is a recurring point of contention.[207](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn207) Participants such as fishers and fish processors may be difficult to identify and directly associate with a fishery disaster. Although it is often possible to contact vessel and processing plant owners or licensed fishers, industry-related labor such as crew members and fish-processing employees may be difficult to track. Because of the transient nature of employment in the fishing industry and seasonal movement of fishing vessels among regions, labor statistics regarding the employment of fishers are either difficult to obtain or may not exist. For example, in some fisheries, crew members may be temporary laborers who follow fishing opportunities. Similar problems may occur in related fishery processing and distribution sectors.

Economic effects of fishery disasters on the local community and region also are difficult to quantify. Services directly related to fishing, such as boat repairs, dock services, and fishing equipment suppliers, as well as other businesses indirectly related to fishing are likely to be harmed by losses in the fish harvesting and processing sectors. Although general regional impacts can be estimated using economic models, it may be difficult to identify the level of impacts on these businesses because of their dispersed nature and their indirect relationship to fishing. A broader understanding of these community impacts depends on more deliberate, long-term data collection and planning to link community concerns with marine fisheries management.

In addition, stakeholders have raised concerns about the effectiveness and administration of disaster funding for serving beneficiaries. For example, the New Jersey Office of the State Comptroller found that approximately $2.4 million of federal COVID-19 funds paid to New Jersey fisheries may have been improper because their administration did not meet program guidelines (e.g., awardees may have failed to disclose other sources of COVID-19 assistance) or because fisheries received more funds than their actual losses in 2020.[208](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn208) Given these administrative concerns, an open question is whether NOAA's efforts to integrate management with social dimensions might be applied to increasing fishing community resilience to fishery failures and to improving assistance programs when disasters occur.[209](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn209) Congress may consider whether NOAA's increased engagement with the fishing community may be warranted through workshops or other fora to evaluate stakeholders' perspectives on the effectiveness of the disaster assistance process. In past explanatory statements for appropriations (e.g., as far back as FY2014), Congress has directed the Department of Commerce to "continue to work with states and tribes in the future with respect to fishery disaster determinations."[210](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn210)

### Aquaculture, Subsistence, and Recreational Fisheries

Fishery disasters affect other resource users not mentioned above, such as recreational fishers, subsistence users, and aquaculture facilities, but some ambiguity exists regarding these groups' eligibility for disaster relief. Aquaculture producers are not considered explicitly in disaster relief sections of the MSA,[211](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn211) but the legislation does make reference to recreational fishers and subsistence users. Certain disaster losses for aquaculture producers may be covered under USDA agricultural disaster assistance programs or federal crop insurance.[212](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn212)

*Aquaculture* is broadly defined as the propagation and rearing of aquatic species in controlled or selected environments.[213](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn213) Aquaculture operations range from extensive farming where there is only minimal control over the organism's environment to intensive systems where complete control is taken at each stage of the organism's life history.[214](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn214) Aquaculture is not addressed or defined in the MSA, but, according to NMFS, the act's management authority over all fish within the exclusive economic zone (EEZ) and statutory definitions of *fishery* and *fishing* provide a sound basis for regulating aquaculture in the EEZ.[215](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn215) NMFS has considered marine aquaculture operations in disaster assistance determinations. For example, in red tide fishery failures, references to shellfish farms were included in the request for assistance with wild shellfish fisheries.[216](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn216) However, questions remain regarding the eligibility of losses that are specific to aquaculture, such as salmon cage culture (i.e., salmon farmed and submerged at sea),[217](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn217) or events that affect only aquaculture and not wild fisheries. Further, a 2018 court decision found that NMFS does not have authority to regulate aquaculture under the MSA.[218](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn218) The U.S. Court of Appeals for the 5th Circuit affirmed the lower court in 2020, confirming the invalidity of any regulation of aquaculture by NMFS under the MSA.[219](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn219) Previous Congresses have considered including aquaculture producers as eligible recipients for fishery disaster assistance. For example, the Commercial Fishing and Aquaculture Protection Act of 2019 (S. 2209), introduced in the 116th Congress, would have amended the MSA to provide supplemental revenue assistance to eligible commercial fishers and aquaculture producers.[220](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn220) The bill specified that assistance could be provided when an eligible loss specifically occurred due to an algal bloom, freshwater intrusion, adverse weather, bird depredation, disease, or other condition determined by the Secretary. Presently, aquaculture producers are not listed as a specific entity for disaster assistance in the FRDIA amendments. However, as noted above, the Secretary may consider aquaculture operations revenue loss when determining the allocation of appropriations for a fishery resource disaster.

Challenges also exist when accounting for recreational fisheries in fishery disaster determinations. Charter boat operators that take paying customers for fishing trips have been included in previous determinations and have benefited from assistance. However, it is unclear whether and how assistance would be provided to other businesses that support recreational fishing, such as bait and tackle shops. Some observers could contend that these businesses should benefit from disaster assistance because they depend on fisheries and are part of the coastal community. Congress also might consider questions related to whether a disaster could be determined for the decline of a species sought only by recreational fisherman, such as red drum, and how losses to these businesses would be quantified. Following the FRDIA amendments, the MSA specifies that 12-month revenue loss for charter and headboat fisheries may be included as part of a complete request for a fishery resource determination.[221](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn221) The MSA, post-FRDIA amendments, also states that economic information regarding the charter sector shall be considered by the Secretary to ensure financial coverage for charter businesses.[222](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn222)

Subsistence users are affected by resource declines and associated losses to household benefits. These impacts may be difficult to assess in economic terms; consequently, it may be challenging to determine the form that relief might take. Furthermore, the term *subsistence*, as it relates to fisheries, is not defined in the MSA or IFA.[223](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn223) Some observers might contend that different approaches may be needed for cases of subsistence disaster relief than for commercial or recreational fisheries. Following the FRDIA amendments, the MSA includes eligibility for subsistence users and their fisheries for disaster assistance and stipulates that a fishery resource disaster includes negative subsistence impact due to an allowable cause.[224](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn224) However, *negative subsistence impact* is not defined in the MSA.[225](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn225)

### Congressional Actions in the 117th-119th Congresses

Previous Congresses (i.e., 117th-119th) have taken several actions with respect to fishery disaster assistance. These actions include passing amendments to the fishery disaster assistance process through the FRDIA and the FISHES Act, in addition to appropriating funds in support of fishery resource disasters through legislation such as the Disaster Relief Supplemental Appropriations Act, 2025 (P.L. 118-158, Division B, Title II) and in other appropriations acts.[226](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn226) Examples of recent legislation and legislative proposals are included below.

#### Fishery Resource Disasters Improvement Act

The 117th Congress amended fishery disaster assistance provisions in the MSA and repealed them from the IFA by enacting the FRDIA. The act amended Section 312 of the MSA, repealed Section 315 of the MSA, and repealed Section 308 of the IFA. Additionally, the amendments to Section 312 of the MSA added prescriptive definitions, timelines, requirements, and revenue thresholds regarding the determination and administration of fishery disaster assistance (see "[Amendments to the Magnuson-Stevens Fishery Conservation and Management Act](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#_Ref164425121)"). Generally, the legislation consolidated and clarified many of the fishery disaster provisions originally in the MSA and IFA and incorporated parts of the NMFS agency directive on fishery disasters.

#### Fishery Improvement to Streamline untimely regulatory Hurdles post Emergency Situation Act (FISHES Act)

The 118th Congress further amended fishery disaster assistance provisions in the MSA by enacting the FISHES Act. The act further amended Section 312 of the MSA to include timeline requirements for the Secretary and the Director of OMB (upon request by the Secretary for co-review) to review requester spend plans and disburse funds to grantees.

#### Other Selected Bills Introduced in the 117th-119th Congresses

In addition to the FRDIA and the FISHES Act, several other bills related to fishery disaster assistance were introduced during the 117th-119th Congresses. In the 119th Congress, the Fisheries Modernization Act of 2025 (H.R. 4800) would amend the MSA to make certain freshwater crawfish fisheries eligible for fishery disaster assistance. Additionally, the Protect American Fisheries Act (H.R. 6150) would amend the MSA to include an "economic cause" as an allowable cause for a fishery disaster determination, among other amendments to the process.[227](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn227) Similar legislation was introduced in the 118th Congress (H.R. 10010).[228](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn228)

Prior to passage of the FRDIA, bills seeking amendments to the fishery disaster assistance process were introduced in the 117th Congress (H.R. 5453/S. 2923), and these bills proposed similar prescriptive language to the FRDIA and repeals of sections from the MSA and the IFA. Refinements to the process also were proposed during the 117th Congress in two separate bills (H.R. 59, H.R. 4690) that each would have comprehensively amended the MSA.

#### Disaster Determinations and Appropriations

Funding of fishery disaster assistance depends on congressional action because there is no permanent fund to provide relief. In many cases, funding was appropriated for a number of disasters after the Secretary had made determinations and allocated among specific fishery disasters by NOAA.[229](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn229) Alternatively, a certain amount may be appropriated for unspecified disasters to remain available until expended. For example, in 2021, Congress appropriated $200 million for fishery disasters, including those associated with 2020 and 2021 hurricanes, to remain available until expended through the Extending Government Funding and Delivering Emergency Assistance Act (P.L. 117-43). The Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2023 (P.L. 117-328), included $300 million in supplemental funding for necessary expenses associated with the mitigation of fishery disasters, to remain available until expended. Further, the Disaster Relief Supplemental Appropriations Act, 2025 (P.L. 118-158, Division B, Title II) also included $300 million for necessary expenses associated with fishery resource disaster relief, to remain available until expended.[230](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-18_R49355_f95ea3c98f08518dd95aecf246490e2729dda4c4.html#fn230)
