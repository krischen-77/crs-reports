The U.S. Census Bureau produces statistics on demographic and economic characteristics across
the United States based on more than 130 different surveys, including the agency's largest
products: the decennial census and the American Community Survey (ACS).

Data from the decennial census are used for apportioning seats in the House of Representatives as well as for redistricting in
the states. Other uses for census data include informing various policy decisions, guiding federal funding, offering the public
insights on population trends, and helping the private sector make informed business decisions. To produce the statistics used
in its data products, the Census Bureau surveys and samples the population of the United States and its territories.

For these reasons, constituents across states and congressional districts may have questions regarding the Census Bureau and
the surveys it conducts. This FAQ is intended to provide information on these potential questions to support district and state
staff and other staff that interact with constituents in fielding questions on these topics.

This report addresses several Census Bureau-related topics that may be of particular interest to constituents, including, but
not limited to, how constituents may be contacted by the Census Bureau, how collected data are used, how data are kept
private, and how Census data and other products can be accessed.

What is the U.S. Census Bureau?

The U.S. Census Bureau is a federal statistical agency housed within the Department of
Commerce. It is tasked with producing statistics on demographic and economic characteristics
across the United States through more than 130 different surveys, including the agency's largest
surveys, the decennial census and the American Community Survey (ACS). Among other
purposes like apportionment of congressional seats, data gathered by the Census Bureau inform
federal funding for programs across all levels of government and provide data used by
governmental and nongovernmental entities for a variety of purposes. More information on the
Census Bureau can be found in CRS Report R47847, The U.S. Census Bureau: An Overview.

Why and how might the Census Bureau contact my
constituents?

In addition to contacts related to the decennial census, the Census Bureau may contact
households, individuals, businesses, or governmental units for one of its numerous ongoing
surveys, including but not limited to the ACS.1

The Census Bureau may contact constituents by official mailing, in-person contact, phone calls,
and text messages.2 The bureau is to provide the name of the survey to which it requests a
constituent’s response at first contact.3

Typically, in the first part of years ending in 0, households may be contacted by the Census
Bureau to complete the decennial census of housing and population. During previous censuses,
the bureau has mailed the questionnaire itself or a mailer requesting households to respond online
to most addresses.4 The bureau sends field staff to enumerate households that do not respond after
the initial contact.5

How can my constituents tell if someone contacting
them from the Census Bureau is legitimate?

The Census Bureau contacts respondents by mail, in-person contacts, email, telephone, and text
message. The Census Bureau has established a web page (https://www.census.gov/programs-
surveys/surveyhelp/verify-a-survey.html) that provides guidance to potential survey respondents

Verify a Mailing

Individuals who receive a mailing can expect to see “U.S. Census Bureau” or “U.S. Department
of Commerce” in the return address. Additionally, the Census Bureau has a mail processing center
located in Jeffersonville, IN, which could appear on the return address. The Census Bureau also
sends out reminder letters from its regional offices or its Washington DC-area headquarters.7

Verify an Email or Text Message

The Census Bureau may send text messages and emails to potential respondents with information
or instructions to complete a survey. The bureau’s guidance says these messages will not ask the
respondent to provide any personal information directly by email or text. The bureau also
explains that emails from the Census Bureau are sent from the bureau’s official @census.gov
domain.8 Constituents may verify a text or email sender’s legitimacy by searching the Census
Bureau online staff database. Constituents may also reach out to their local Census Bureau
Regional Office to verify a contact.9

Verify a Census Bureau Field Representative

The Census Bureau sometimes uses field representatives to collect responses door-to-door. These
individuals are to carry a Census Bureau-branded bag and electronic device (laptop, tablet,
smartphone) and have an identification badge that includes their name and photograph, an
expiration date, and a Department of Commerce watermark.10

Are my constituents required to respond to Census
Bureau surveys? Are there consequences for not
responding?
Some of the Census Bureau’s data collection is mandatory under Title 13 of the U.S. Code.12
Respondents are required by law to respond to the following:
    •   Decennial Census of Housing and Population,
    •  American Community Survey (ACS),
    •  Annual Business Survey (ABS),
    •  Annual Integrated Economic Survey (AEIS),
    •   Business and Professional Classification Report, and
    •  Economic Census.

Other surveys the Census Bureau conducts are voluntary.

Can the Census Bureau provide monetary incentives
to survey respondents?

The Census Bureau has provided monetary incentives to survey respondents on occasion. For
example, the bureau did this in the 1990s for the Survey of Income and Program Participation
(SIPP) and the Survey of Program Dynamics.13 Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) research found
that introducing monetary incentives to survey respondents increased response rates.14 Monetary
incentives may be provided for other surveys as well.

What happens with my constituents’ information
after the Census Bureau collects it?

The Census Bureau says it keeps the information it collects on a secure computer server.
Information that is released is to undergo disclosure avoidance techniques in which any
personally identifiable information is removed and only aggregate statistics about people and
businesses are published.15 This “deidentified” and aggregated information is made publicly
available in data tables at https://data.census.gov.

Policymakers, governments, researchers, businesses, and others utilize data from Census Bureau
surveys for many purposes, including to inform various policy decisions, guide federal funding,
offer public insights on population trends, and help the private sector to make informed business
decisions.16 Census data are used to determine allocation of funding for numerous federal
programs, which amounted to more than $2.8 trillion in FY2021 according to Census Bureau
estimates.17

The Census Bureau publishes data on a variety of population and economic characteristics, and it
produces statistical products, including data tables, interactive visualizations, and reports. For
more information, see CRS Report R47847, The U.S. Census Bureau: An Overview and “How
can my constituents find Census Bureau data?”

How does the Census Bureau protect a
respondent’s privacy?

The Census Bureau is legally prohibited from disclosing identifiable information that it collects
from respondents. By law, information collected during Census Bureau surveys can only be used
for aggregated statistical purposes.18 This means that Census Bureau employees who wrongfully
disclose a respondent’s information are subject to penalties.19

Specifically, Title 13, Section 9(a) of the U.S. Code states that the Secretary of Commerce, any
officer or employee of the Department of Commerce or Census Bureau, or a local government
census liaison may not

         (1) use the information furnished under the provisions of this title for any purpose other
        than the statistical purposes for which it is supplied; or

         (2) make any publication whereby the data furnished by any particular establishment or
         individual under this title can be identified; or

Under federal law, individuals with access to sensitive information maintained by the Census
Bureau are subject to penalties for wrongful disclosure of such information. Penalties established
in Section 214 of Title 13 provide a maximum fine of $5,000, imprisonment of not more than five
years, or both.21 Section 3571(b) of Title 18 provides an alternative schedule of fines that may be
applicable, raising the potential maximum penalty for a violation of Title 13 to $250,000.22

The Census Bureau explains that it takes steps to ensure that individual respondents cannot be
identified through the statistics it publishes by using a disclosure avoidance system.23

However, the Census Bureau transfers information gathered during the decennial census to the
National Archives and Records Administration (NARA) for permanent storage. NARA eventually
releases information gathered during the decennial census under the “72-Year Rule.” Established
in P.L. 95-416, this rule allows decennial census data to be released to the general public 72 years
after they are collected.24

What is the American Community Survey?

The American Community Survey (ACS) is the largest household survey administered by the
Census Bureau. Each year, it samples 3.5 million residential addresses across the 50 states, the
District of Columbia, and Puerto Rico.25 The ACS is a long-form questionnaire that covers more
than 40 topics, including education, employment, income, housing, and transportation.26

Typically, the Census Bureau initially contacts households by mail and requests that they
complete a survey online, by mail or phone, or during an in-person interview.27 If a household
does not use one of these methods to respond, it may be contacted by an in-person interviewer.28

What is the Household Trends and Outlooks
Pulse Survey?

The Household Trends and Outlook Pulse Survey (HTOPS), formerly the Household Pulse
Survey, is an experimental survey originally developed as an effort to quickly gather demographic
information in response to the COVID-19 pandemic. The HTOPS aims to provide data with a
relatively short turnaround time. Due to the survey’s experimental nature and the faster
turnaround time, HTOPS data products do not necessarily meet some of the Census Bureau’s
statistical quality standards.29

During May 2026, HTOPS sampled about 136,000 households, based on an assumed 10%
response rate.30 Survey topics include food and nutrition, transportation, and education. The
bureau intends to use HTOPS data to improve and inform future surveys. Participation in HTOPS
is voluntary.31

There has been a considerable change in population
in my state or district since the last decennial
census. Can the Census Bureau conduct a new count
to address this change?

Some constituents may have concerns about the timeliness of certain Census data. For example,
constituents may have observed significant changes in local or regional areas between decennial
censuses. In this scenario, constituents may consider discussing the Special Census Program with
local officials. A special census is a basic enumeration of population, housing units, group
quarters, and transitory locations that the Census Bureau can conduct at the request of a
governmental unit. Local officials may consider requesting a special census or a smaller partial
special census if they believe there has been considerable population size or demographic change
in their community.32

For the bureau to conduct a special census, the given state’s legislation must allow it. Also, only
the following functioning governmental unit types are eligible for a special census:
    •   tribal areas, including federally recognized tribes with a reservation and/or off-
        reservation trust lands, Alaska Native Regional Corporations, and Alaska Native
         villages;
    •   states or equivalent entities (e.g., District of Columbia, Puerto Rico);
    •   counties or equivalent entities (e.g., boroughs, parishes, municipios);
    •  minor civil divisions (e.g., townships);
    •   consolidated cities; and
    •   incorporated places (e.g., villages, towns, cities).33

The bureau conducts special censuses on a cost-reimbursement basis, which means that the
governmental unit requesting the special census is responsible for the full cost associated with
conducting the count and is to reimburse the Census Bureau for all costs it incurs. The Census
Bureau is to conduct a special census cost estimate before agreeing to conduct a special census
and commencing any associated work.34 Interested jurisdictions may complete the SC-900
Special Census Cost Estimate Request form to begin this process.35

While a special census can provide updated population estimates for a community, it cannot
review or recount results from a previous census.36

The urban or rural classification of a community in my state or
district has changed under Census Bureau delineations, potentially
changing its eligibility for certain federal funding. Can this be
changed?

The Census Bureau has an established urban and rural classification that is re-delineated after
each decennial census. Re-delineation attempts to ensure that the classification of places across
the nation reflects population changes.37

During the re-delineation process, the Census Bureau seeks feedback from stakeholders through
the Federal Register.38 There is no other formal process by which the bureau changes urban and
rural delineations outside of the existing decennial census cycle.

In establishing these delineations, the Census Bureau considers only their use for statistical
purposes. The Census Bureau does not attempt to consider or meet the requirements of
nonstatistical uses of urban and rural delineations or any associated data.39
Some legislation may define rural and urban differently than the Census Bureau.40 Additionally,
other geographic delineations exist across the federal government, including core based statistical
areas developed by the Office of Management and Budget (OMB).41

How can my constituents find Census Bureau data?

There are several ways constituents can access Census Bureau data. Most publicly available data
can be found at https://data.census.gov/. Additionally, the Census Bureau maintains a library of
publications using Census data online at https://www.census.gov/library.html.42 Decennial census
data are also released 72 years after they were collected, available online through NARA at
https://www.archives.gov/research/census/online-resources.43 Restricted-use, confidential data
may be accessed at a federal statistical research data center. Constituents must complete an
application process to access such data.44

Data about congressional districts is made available online through the My Congressional District
tool.45

Are there any employment opportunities available
at the Census Bureau that may be relevant for my
constituents?
Employment opportunities are available on the Census Bureau’s website.46 Permanent
employment may be available at the Census Bureau headquarters located in Suitland, MD;
locations in one of the six regions across the United States; or the National Processing Center
facilities located in Arizona and Indiana.

The Census Bureau also often hires temporary positions for enumerators during the decennial
census, as well as any census tests leading up to the decennial census.
