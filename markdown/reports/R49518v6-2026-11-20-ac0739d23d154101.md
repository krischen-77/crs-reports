On August 24, 2026, President Donald Trump submitted to Congress a U.S.-Saudi agreement for
cooperation on peaceful uses of nuclear energy (hereinafter “123 agreement”), pursuant to Section 123 of the Atomic Energy
Act (AEA) of 1954, as amended, together with a Bilateral Safeguards Agreement (BSA). The Administration also submitted
two side letters to the agreement to Congress; the letters have not been publicly released. The BSA, which is a feature unique
to the U.S.-Saudi Arabia 123 agreement, would involve IAEA monitoring and would need to be approved by the IAEA
Board of Governors before the 123 agreement is implemented. The BSA has provisions similar to, but less extensive than,
what would be included in an Additional Protocol to a comprehensive safeguards agreement with the IAEA. In the 123
agreement, Saudi Arabia commits to using “qualified suppliers” for its nuclear energy program, which are companies
licensed or approved by the United States.

Under the AEA, Congress reviews Section 123 agreements for two periods totaling 90 days of continuous session. At the
beginning of the second of these review periods, which lasts for 60 days of continuous session, joint resolutions of
disapproval are to be automatically introduced in each house. After the second review period, the President can take the steps
necessary for the agreement’s entry into force unless Congress adopts a joint resolution of disapproval that becomes law.
Under the AEA, the review periods commence from the date of the agreement’s transmittal to Congress. Based on the U.S.-
Saudi agreement’s receipt in Congress, the review periods for the U.S.-Saudi 123 nuclear agreement may conclude in
December 2026.

Congress closely considers instances of nuclear cooperation that may enable uranium enrichment for fuel production, which
is allowed under the NPT but can pose nuclear weapons proliferation risks. The proposed 123 agreement says the United
States and Saudi Arabia “may explore options to support the long-term commercial feasibility of civil uranium enrichment
capacity” in their respective countries and will initiate a two-year Joint Enrichment and Conversion Study. The agreement
does not authorize transfer of enrichment technology. The agreement requires that the BSA must enter into force prior to the
United States’ “providing consent for enrichment or support for any enrichment facility” in Saudi Arabia. President Trump
stated in a July 2026 social media post that, “There will be no enrichment of material.”

Through 2024, successive U.S. Administrations, including the first Trump Administration, had linked a potential 123
agreement with Saudi Arabia to the kingdom’s acceptance of enhanced international safeguards on its nuclear program and
its forgoing acquisition of the most proliferation-sensitive nuclear facilities—those for enriching uranium or reprocessing
spent nuclear fuel to obtain plutonium. These terms—which U.S. officials and international observers at times have referred
to as “the gold standard”—were incorporated into the 2009 U.S. 123 agreement with Saudi Arabia’s neighbor, the United
Arab Emirates (UAE). An Agreed Minute (2009) to the U.S.-UAE 123 agreement states that its terms “shall be no less
favorable in scope and effect” than U.S. agreements with other countries in the Middle East and that the United States will, at
the UAE’s request, consult “regarding the possibility of amending” the agreement with equally favorable terms. The U.S.-
UAE agreement does not include qualified supplier provisions or a bilateral safeguards agreement.

Members of Congress have adopted varying positions on U.S.-Saudi nuclear cooperation. Some Members have argued that
no agreement should go into effect until Saudi Arabia commits to forgo uranium enrichment and spent nuclear fuel
reprocessing and to conclude an Additional Protocol to its comprehensive safeguards agreement with the IAEA. In 2025, the
Trump Administration waived a provision of law that limited consideration of a 123 agreement because Saudi Arabia has not
concluded an Additional Protocol. Other Members have welcomed the 123 agreement as a commercial opportunity for U.S.
firms and as a means of displacing potential Chinese or Russian vendors from participation in Saudi Arabia’s nuclear energy
program. In the 119th Congress, some Members have introduced joint resolutions of disapproval for the proposed U.S.-Saudi
123 agreement (H.J.Res. 217 and S.J.Res. 221).

If the 123 agreement is not disapproved during the congressional review period, it may enter into force through an exchange
of diplomatic notes between the two countries. President Trump stated on July 21, 2026, that implementation of the
agreement would be contingent on Saudi Arabia signing the Abraham Accords, which involve the recognition of Israel. Saudi
Arabia’s Crown Prince said in 2025 that the kingdom wants to be a part of the Accords, but official Saudi statements
condition recognition of Israel on the establishment of an independent Palestinian state with East Jerusalem as its capital. The
government of Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu has opposed the establishment of a Palestinian state, and leading
Israeli opposition figure Gadi Eisenkot has pledged that no Palestinian state will arise under a government he forms. The
U.S.-Saudi 123 agreement does not mention Israel or the Abraham Accords, and in a Senate Foreign Relations Committee
hearing in November 2026, the U.S. Ambassador-designate for Saudi Arabia said the matter is subject to continuing
negotiations.

Saudi Arabia: Background and U.S. Relations

Saudi Arabia is a monarchy governed by the Al Saud family. King Salman bin Abd al Aziz Al
Saud (born 1935) has ruled since 2015. His son, Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman (born
1985, aka MBS), serves as Prime Minister and acts as the kingdom’s principal decisionmaker.
The king’s age and health challenges suggest that a leadership transition could occur at any time.
Since the 1940s, the United States and Saudi Arabia have maintained close relations anchored in
security and energy partnerships and tested at times by disagreements over oil production, the
Israeli-Palestinian conflict, terrorism, human rights, and regional security. U.S. forces are
deployed in Saudi Arabia to aid in Saudi missile and air defenses and to provide training to Saudi
security forces under long running cooperative programs.

Under the Crown Prince’s leadership since 2017, Saudi Arabia has pursued an economic and
social transformation agenda at home and a multifaceted foreign policy abroad involving complex
relationships with the United States, U.S. rivals, other G-20 economies, and the developing
world. The kingdom’s Vision 2030 initiatives outline ambitious goals for reducing the kingdom’s
fiscal dependence on oil revenue, developing a diverse and globally connected domestic
economy, and creating opportunity for Saudi citizens and foreign investors in sectors such as
tourism, global logistics, mining, manufacturing, and artificial intelligence.

In 2025, President Trump and Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman exchanged visits, and
announced progress on bilateral agreements on mining, defense, artificial intelligence, and civil
nuclear energy cooperation. The leaders also discussed plans for what the White House has
estimated may be “nearly $1 trillion”1 in trade and mutual investment, including purchases of
U.S. goods and services. President Trump designated Saudi Arabia as a Major Non-NATO Ally in
January 2026 and has notified Congress of more than $35 billion in proposed foreign military
sales to the kingdom since December 2025, including the proposed sale of F-35 fighter aircraft.

As of 2026, the kingdom has made measurable progress on several of its Vision 2030 objectives,
but conflict in the Persian Gulf and Red Sea regions is challenging Saudi plans and preferences.
The U.S. and Israeli conflict with Iran since February 2026 and the subsequent resurgence of war
between Saudi Arabia and the Yemen-based Ansarallah/Houthi movement have disrupted Saudi
oil exports and economic activity and threaten Saudi infrastructure. Attacks attributed to Iran and
Iran-backed groups in Iraq and Yemen have damaged energy sites and other government and
civilian targets in Saudi Arabia and other Arab Gulf states.

Iranian attacks resulted in the death and injury of U.S. military forces deployed in Saudi Arabia
and damaged U.S. military aircraft there. U.S. and Saudi forces have jointly struck targets
associated with Iran-backed groups in Iraq. In September 2026, Secretary of State Marco Rubio
said that “we have a very strong defense agreement with Saudi Arabia and we will live up to our
commitments in that agreement.”2

Saudi Arabia’s Nuclear Energy Program

Rationale

Saudi Arabia holds nearly 17% of the world’s proven reserves of crude oil, has the world’s fourth-
largest reserves of natural gas, and is the second-largest consumer of energy on a per capita basis
in the Middle East.3 In 2025, oil and natural gas generated about 32% and 64% of the kingdom’s
electricity, respectively.4

In 2010, a Saudi royal decree called for the development of nuclear and renewable energy for use
in electricity production and water desalination, as well as a means to diversify the country’s
energy supply. As part of this effort, the 2017 Saudi National Project for Atomic Energy (NPAE)
includes plans to build large and small nuclear reactors. The NPAE is part of the Saudi
government’s broader Vision 2030 initiative to diversify the kingdom’s economy, which includes
efforts to expand the use of non-fossil-fuel-based energy to preserve oil and natural gas resources
for revenue-generating exports. Vision 2030 includes a National Renewable Energy Program
(NREP) to expand the generation of electricity from renewable sources such as wind and solar to
supply 50% of the kingdom’s electricity by 2030.5 In addition to the development of large and
small reactors, Saudi Arabia is building a low power research reactor facility, and Saudi officials
have stated their intent to mine domestic uranium resources and develop a capacity to enrich
uranium and fabricate fuel for nuclear power plants.

Saudi Arabia’s 2022 National Policy for the Atomic Energy Program states that the program is
intended to help the country “move from an oil-dependent economy towards a more diversified
and productive economy,” meet “water desalination and other needs,” and contribute to an
alternative to the kingdom’s “steady increase in the consumption of fossil fuel based energy.”6 An
additional priority of the nuclear power program, according to the Saudi energy minister, is

Program Plans and Progress

Power plants, research, and uranium exploration and extraction constitute the three components
of the planned Saudi national nuclear program. The kingdom has worked to advance each
component since 2017.

Power Generation

Saudi Arabia plans to develop and operate both large and small nuclear power plants to generate
electricity. The government’s longer term nuclear power capacity targets have evolved over time.
In 2011, the King Abdullah City for Atomic and Renewable Energy (KA CARE) set a goal of
developing more than 17 gigawatt electric (GWe) of nuclear power capacity by 2032, to include a
proposal for 16 eventual power plants.8 However, the kingdom’s nuclear and overall renewable
energy targets were later amended formally through the development of Vision 2030, the NREP,
and the approval of the 2017 National Project for Atomic Energy.

Preparations since 2017 have focused on plans to construct two 1-to-1.6 GWe nuclear reactors at
Khor Duwayhin (alt. Duweihin, see Figure 1), a coastal area on the Persian Gulf between the
kingdom’s borders with Qatar and the United Arab Emirates (UAE).9 The kingdom initiated a
bidding tender process for the construction of the two planned reactors, with Russia’s Rosatom,
France’s Électricité de France (EDF), China National Nuclear Corporation (CNNC), South
Korea’s Korea Electric Power Corporation (KEPCO), and Westinghouse having all provided
information for consideration. The Trump Administration states that the Saudi government has
repeatedly extended the timeline for completing the tender process to allow for the United States
and Saudi Arabia to enter into a nuclear cooperation agreement and “has encouraged
Westinghouse participation, while remaining committed to its completion and to selecting a
vendor to build the Kingdom’s first nuclear power plant.”10

Research

Saudi Arabia has completed construction of a low power research reactor at the King Abd al Aziz
City for Science and Technology (KACST) in Riyadh.12 The reactor is being built in partnership
with Argentina’s Investigacion Applicada (INVAP), and “will be used for nuclear science-related
research and training.”13 Additionally, KACST hosts an Atomic Energy Research Institute (AERI)
that conducts research on “industrial applications of radiation and radioactive isotopes, nuclear
power and reactors, nuclear materials, and radiation protection.”14 KACST also oversees the
kingdom’s Nuclear Research Institute, which consists of a Nuclear Regulatory Unit and “national
centers for accelerators, applied-radiation, irradiation, and nuclear technologies.”15 The Saudi
government has identified the development of indigenous research and applications expertise as a
priority.16

Uranium Resources and Nuclear Fuel Development17

Mining is one of the main strategic pillars of the Vision 2030 project, and Saudi leaders are
working to establish the kingdom as a hub for mineral extraction, processing, and supply chain
integration. Saudi Arabia has engaged in extensive mineral exploration since about 2017,
including with assistance from the government of the People’s Republic of China. Over the
medium term, Saudi Arabia seeks to develop its domestic uranium resources for use as nuclear
reactor fuel. According to the NPAS, the kingdom “will depend on the international market for
nuclear fuel services for the near-term.”18

The Saudi Uranium Exploration Project has included joint uranium resource mapping efforts
between the Saudi Geological Survey and China’s CNNC, as well as a separate initiative with
Jordan. To date, three main uranium deposit sites have been identified in northern and western
Saudi Arabia—Qurriyah/Ghurayyah, Jabal Sayid, and the Al Jalamid phosphates zone in the
Thaniyat Turayf basin adjacent to Jordan and Iraq.

According to a Nuclear Energy Agency and IAEA worldwide assessment of uranium resources,
Saudi Arabia had about 64,100 tonnes of identified recoverable uranium resources (tU)19 in the
polymetallic Th-U-Nb-Ta and rare earth element (REE)-bearing Qurriyah/Ghurayyah deposit and
the Jabal Sayid U-Th prospect20 as of January 1, 2025.21 As of January 1, 2025, Saudi Arabia’s
total identified unconventional resources were 99,984 tU, including other deposits, such as the
Thaniyat Turayf phosphorite prospect.22 Saudi Arabia plans to continue mineral exploration.
To date, the identified recoverable uranium resources are of low to very low grade,23 and may be
included within deposits that also are enriched in other critical minerals.24 Saudi Arabia’s
identified recoverable uranium resources are only recoverable as a by-product or co-product (i.e.,
derived from a mine that produces other mineral commodities and may produce uranium as an
additional product).25 The recoverable uranium resources identified in Saudi Arabia are
considered among the more difficult and more expensive to extract with current methods and
technologies compared with other types of deposits, based on what is known about their geologic
characteristics.26

In May 2025, U.S.-based entities entered into a bilateral agreement with Saudi Arabia-based
entities to support exploration and processing of potential REE deposits in the country, including
the Jabal Sayid U-Th prospect.27 Such work may also advance uranium resource exploration and
lead to more information about uranium resources in Saudi Arabia.

Saudi Arabia and IAEA Safeguards

In 1988, Saudi Arabia acceded to the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons (NPT),
Article II of which prohibits nonnuclear-weapon states-parties (NNWS) from acquiring nuclear
weapons.30 Article III of the treaty requires NNWS to conclude Comprehensive Safeguards
Agreements (CSAs) with the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA). (See Appendix B for
more information.) Such agreements require governments to declare their entire inventory of
certain nuclear materials, as well as related facilities; the agency subsequently applies safeguards
“on all nuclear material in all nuclear activities” in the state.31 CSAs enable the IAEA to detect
the diversion of nuclear material from peaceful purposes to nuclear weapons uses, as well as to
detect undeclared nuclear activities and material.32 Safeguards include agency onsite inspections
and continuous monitoring of declared nuclear facilities. Although all nuclear material in an
NNWS is subject to IAEA safeguards, CSAs, as a practical matter, limit the agency’s monitoring
and inspections authority to declared facilities.
Saudi Arabia’s CSA entered into force in 2009.33 Until 2024, the kingdom’s agreement had a
small quantities protocol (SQP) that held “in abeyance” most CSA procedures given the limited
nuclear activities that were taking place in Saudi Arabia.34 Effective in 2025, the kingdom
rescinded that SQP and the CSA is now fully operable.35

Additional Protocol (AP) to its CSA over successive Administrations.36 (See Appendix B.) To

Congressional Review

This section describes AEA congressional review procedures for a nuclear cooperation
agreement, such as the agreement with Saudi Arabia, which the President has not exempted from
any requirements of Section 123(a).65 Congress has the opportunity to review such an agreement
during two consecutive time periods totaling 90 days of continuous session. After the review
period, the President is permitted take the steps necessary for the agreement’s entry into force.

The AEA requires the President to submit the text of the proposed nuclear cooperation agreement,
along with the unclassified NPAS, to the House Foreign Affairs Committee and the Senate
Foreign Relations Committee. The President must also authorize “the execution of the proposed
agreement for cooperation” and make “a determination in writing that the performance of the
proposed agreement will promote, and will not constitute an unreasonable risk to, the common
defense and security.” The President is then to consult with the committees “for a period of not
less than 30 days of continuous session.”

After this period of consultation, the President is to submit the agreement to Congress, along with
the above-described presidential approval and determination, as well as the classified NPAS.66
This action begins the second review period, which lasts for 60 days of continuous session. At the
beginning of this 60-day period, joint resolutions of disapproval are to be automatically
introduced in each house. During this period, the committees are to hold hearings on the proposed
agreement and “submit a report to their respective bodies recommending whether it should be
approved or disapproved.” If either committee has not reported the requisite joint resolution of
disapproval by the end of 45 days, the committee is automatically discharged from further
consideration of the measure. After the joint resolution is reported or the committee is discharged
from considering the resolution further, Congress is to consider it under expedited procedures, as
established by Section 130i of the AEA.

The agreement can become effective after the 60-day period unless, during that time, Congress
adopts a joint resolution disapproving the agreement and the resolution becomes law (after being
signed by the President or a veto is overridden). In practice generally, the President has submitted
the agreement to Congress, along with the unclassified NPAS, its classified annex, and his
approval and determination, at the beginning of the full 90-day period. Congress has considered
the 60-day period as following immediately upon the expiration of the 30-day period.

Security and Governance

Ongoing conflict in the Middle East region has demonstrated the vulnerability of Saudi critical
infrastructure to attack by missiles and drones. The U.S. travel advisory for Saudi Arabia notes
that “Iran and Iran-backed Houthi rebels have launched missile and drone attacks into Saudi
Arabia” and states that terrorist attacks “can occur with little or no warning” against targets that
may include government facilities and areas frequented by U.S. citizens.93 In May 2026, an attack

nuclear power plant; U.S. and South Korean-origin UAE missile defense systems contributed to
the UAE’s defenses.94 Barakah sits on the Persian Gulf coast of the UAE, roughly 62 miles from
the planned Saudi plant site at Khor Duwayhin.

Saudi Arabia is a monarchy without a legislative body or other constitutionally established
entities with budgetary or oversight authorities. National security and economic decisionmaking
in Saudi Arabia are functions of the kingdom’s executive leadership, as manifested in the cabinet
and the Crown Prince in his roles as Prime Minister and Chairman of the Council of Political and
Security Affairs, the Council of Economic and Development Affairs, and the Public Investment
Fund, the kingdom’s sovereign wealth fund. Policy decisions and the issuance of legal and
regulatory mechanisms to implement them are functions of the Saudi executive. As noted above,
succession in Saudi Arabia could occur at any time given the king’s advanced age and health
challenges. Members of Congress have taken divergent approaches to bilateral cooperation with
Saudi Arabia, with some prioritizing sustaining cooperation and others taking a more conditional
approach reflecting concerns about national security, human rights, or regional developments.

On a bilateral basis, the implementation of a civil nuclear cooperation agreement and the
involvement of U.S. personnel in bilateral safeguards activities could extend the duration and
expand the extent of U.S. official presence in Saudi Arabia, with attendant security and cost
implications. As of October 2026, the U.S. State Department advised U.S. citizens to “Reconsider
Travel” to Saudi Arabia due to “risk of Iranian drone and missile targeting of American interests,
armed conflict, terrorism, exit bans, and local laws regarding social media activity.”

Appendix A. Atomic Energy Act and Nuclear
Cooperation Agreements105

Under the Atomic Energy Act (AEA) of 1954, as amended, all significant U.S. nondefense
nuclear cooperation with other countries requires a nuclear cooperation agreement.106 These
agreements authorize cooperation but do not guarantee that cooperation will take place. Currently,
the United States has 26 Section 123 agreements in force.107 Significant nuclear cooperation
includes the transfer of U.S.-origin special nuclear material subject to licensing for commercial,
medical, and industrial purposes, and the export of reactors and critical parts of reactors.108 The
NRC issues licenses for the export of nuclear items pursuant to Section 103 of the AEA.

Requirements109

Section 123 of the AEA requires that nuclear cooperation agreements include the terms,
conditions, duration, nature, and scope of cooperation, as well as meet nine nonproliferation

The NPAS is meant to explain how a proposed agreement would meet the aforementioned

performance of the proposed agreement will promote and will not constitute an unreasonable risk

The AEA and the IAEA Additional Protocol

The AEA does not require a country to adopt an Additional Protocol to its IAEA comprehensive
safeguards agreement in order to conclude a 123 agreement with the United States. However,
except for Argentina and Brazil, all states with which the United States has a 123 agreement have
concluded such a protocol.112

Enrichment and Reprocessing Provisions

As noted, enrichment and reprocessing facilities are a source of proliferation concern. The AEA
mandates that 123 agreements require U.S. consent for any “alteration in form or content” (to
include enrichment or reprocessing) of U.S.-origin material or any material processed in a facility
containing transferred U.S. nuclear technology, including reactors. Section 123 agreements also
require U.S. consent for any retransfer of material or technology.113 The AEA, however, does not
require governments to forgo possession of enrichment and reprocessing facilities as a condition
for concluding a 123 agreement.

U.S. policy has aimed to persuade certain countries with which it is negotiating nuclear
cooperation agreements to forgo obtaining enrichment and reprocessing facilities.114 As noted, the
2008 MOU stated Saudi Arabia’s intent to refrain from enrichment and reprocessing. The United
States concluded similar MOUs that year with Bahrain, Jordan, and the UAE. However,
subsequent 123 agreements’ provisions concerning enrichment and reprocessing have varied.

Appendix B. Comprehensive Safeguards
Agreements and Additional Protocols

Comprehensive Safeguards Agreements

The Nonproliferation Treaty (NPT) requires non-nuclear-weapon states-parties (NNWS) to
conclude Comprehensive Safeguards Agreements (CSAs) with the IAEA.121 Such agreements,
which apply safeguards “on all nuclear material in all nuclear activities in a State,” are intended
to enable the agency to detect the diversion of nuclear material from peaceful purposes to nuclear
weapons uses, as well as to detect undeclared nuclear activities and material. Safeguards include
agency inspections and monitoring of declared nuclear facilities. Although all nuclear material in
an NNWS is subject to IAEA safeguards, CSAs, as a practical matter, limit the agency’s
monitoring and inspections authority in a particular country to facilities that have been declared
by the government. CSAs authorize the IAEA to conduct special inspections.122

Although CSAs give the IAEA the authority “to verify the absence of undeclared nuclear material
and activities, the tools available to it to do so, under such agreements, are limited,” according to
the agency.123 As a practical matter, the IAEA’s ability to inspect and monitor nuclear facilities, as
well as obtain related information pursuant to a particular country’s comprehensive safeguards
agreement, is limited to facilities and activities that have been declared by the government.

Additional Protocols124

In 1991, IAEA inspectors revealed that Iraq, despite having a CSA in force, had concealed a
nuclear weapons program by conducting activities in undeclared locations. As part of a larger
effort to improve the agency’s ability to detect of undeclared nuclear activities, the IAEA Board
of Governors in 1997 adopted the model Additional Protocol (AP) to CSAs.125 APs, which are
voluntary, augment the IAEA’s ability to detect clandestine nuclear facilities and activities by
providing the IAEA with additional authority to inspect certain nuclear-related facilities and
demand information from member states. The Additional Protocol also allows for
“complementary access” by inspectors to sites on short notice.126

Information

The AP requires NNWS to provide certain types of information concerning nuclear-related
activities that do not involve the use of nuclear material. CSAs already cover such activities that
use nuclear material. The information most relevant to producing nuclear weapons falls into
several categories:
    •  A “general description of and information specifying the location of nuclear fuel
        cycle-related research and development activities not involving nuclear
        material.” These activities include those “specifically related to any process or

    •  A “description of the scale of operations for each location engaged in the
         activities specified” in the AP’s Annex I, which covers a variety of activities

    •   Information “specifying the location, operational status and the estimated annual
        production capacity of uranium mines and [uranium] concentration

relating to the correctness and completeness” of the information that a state has provided to the
IAEA. The methods and activities (e.g., environmental sampling, radiation detection) that IAEA
