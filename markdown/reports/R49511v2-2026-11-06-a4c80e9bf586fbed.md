The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026 (BAAJA; S. 5653) was introduced
on September 30, 2026. Negotiated by Members of the Senate Committees on Environment and
Public Works and Energy and Natural Resources, the bill as introduced contains seven titles
within two divisions, which aim to reform certain federal environmental review and permitting processes to expedite a wide
range of project types, with an emphasis on energy-related projects.

Members have shown sustained interest over several Congresses in amending the nation’s permitting laws to increase energy
production and expedite a variety of projects (e.g., transportation infrastructure, energy, data center development, ecological
restoration, stormwater infrastructure). Some previously proposed permitting reform bills have focused on specific
environmental statutes with review and/or permitting provisions. Others have focused on specific project types, and some
have included broader reforms to permitting and reviews across project types. Areas of debate on previous legislative
proposals have included whether reforms should target conventional or renewable forms of energy, whether amending
bedrock environmental laws to shorten reviews may weaken environmental protections, and whether certain proposals may
narrow the role of states in the review process and/or reduce opportunities for public input and judicial review.

This report discusses the three sections in BAAJA Title IV, titled “Project Certainty.” In brief, BAJAA Section 1401 would
generally bar federal agency actions that modify, suspend, or revoke existing permits or authorizations, or that interfere with
construction or “operation at full capacity” of fully permitted projects. Such actions would be permissible only when
necessary to comply with a court order or address certain emergent threats, or when a permittee violates the terms of its
permit or other applicable law, obtains its permit by misrepresentation, or requests the action in question. Section 1401 would
authorize suit to enforce its terms and would entitle prevailing plaintiffs to injunctive relief and damages, including 25% to
50% of total project costs, to be paid from the Judgment Fund, a permanent, indefinite appropriation codified at 31 U.S.C.
§ 1304. Section 1402 would authorize developers of certain types of energy projects to challenge permit delays or denials that
were allegedly “due to a pattern of disparate treatment,” defined, in part, to mean a “substantial” increase in rates of permit
delay or denial for projects of the same type, as compared to a five-year baseline. Like Section 1401, Section 1402 would
entitle prevailing plaintiffs to injunctive relief and damages, including 50%-100% of anticipated construction costs, again
payable from the Judgment Fund. Section 1403 would authorize suits to enforce a one-year deadline for agencies to approve
or disapprove “complete applications” for permits or authorizations that do not require an environmental assessment or
environmental impact statement under the National Environmental Policy Act (42 U.S.C. §§ 4321 et seq.). Section 1403
would entitle prevailing plaintiffs to injunctive relief but not damages.

By authorizing damage awards of between 25% and 50% of total project costs, or 50% to 100% of reasonably anticipated
construction costs, Sections 1401 and 1402 could substantially increase payments from the Judgment Fund relative to
historical baseline averages. Such payments would be available to satisfy both court-ordered judgments and out-of-court
settlements. Thus, claimants seeking relief under Section 1401 could obtain payment without prevailing in court by resolving
their claims through agreements with the Department of Justice.

Section 1401’s impact on other statutes is uncertain but could be significant if courts were to construe that provision to bar
agencies from taking actions authorized by other laws. Under such a reading, Section 1401 could prevent agencies from
fulfilling statutory duties to revisit and revise prior permitting and regulatory decisions upon certain triggering conditions.

The scope of potential liability under Section 1402 is uncertain, in part because it is not clear if any empirically demonstrated
“substantial” increase in relevant rates of permit delay or denial could support a claim, or if a plaintiff would instead need to
establish a statistical patten of disparate treatment and show that the government intended to create such a pattern. Both
readings find support in Section 1402’s text. If Section 1402 contains no specific intent requirement, then a series of legal,
good-faith permit denials could give rise to liability. Conversely, if plaintiffs must establish the government’s subjective
motivation to secure relief under Section 1402, then agencies might more easily evade liability by using facially neutral
policies that have disproportionate impacts on disfavored project types.

Introduction

The chairs and ranking members of the Senate Environment and Public Works and Energy and
Natural Resources Committees introduced S. 5653, the Bipartisan American Affordability and
Jobs Act of 2026 (BAAJA), on September 30, 2026. BAAJA is organized into seven titles across
two divisions. This report provides an overview of the as-introduced version of BAAJA Division
A, Title IV, which is titled “Project Certainty.”

Title IV contains three sections. Section 1401 would restrict agencies’ authority to alter or repeal
existing permits or interfere with operation or construction of fully permitted projects. Section
1401 would also authorize suits to enforce those restrictions and would entitle prevailing
plaintiffs to injunctive relief and damages, including 25% to 50% of total project costs. Section
1402 would allow developers of certain projects to challenge permitting delays or denials that
were allegedly “due to a pattern of disparate treatment” against projects of the same type. As with
Section 1401, Section 1402 would entitle prevailing plaintiffs to injunctive relief and damages,
including 50% to 100% of total anticipated construction costs. Finally, Section 1403 would allow
developers to sue to enforce agencies’ compliance with a one-year deadline to approve or deny
complete applications for authorizations or permits that do not require an environmental
assessment (EA) or environmental impact statement (EIS) under the National Environmental
Policy Act (NEPA).1 Under Section 1403, prevailing plaintiffs would be entitled to certain
injunctive relief, but not to damages.

This report summarizes Sections 1401, 1402, and 1403. It then identifies considerations for
Congress, including the potential implications of damage awards under Sections 1401 and 1402,
and uncertainties about the scope and potential impact of those sections.

BAAJA’s “Project Certainty” Provisions

BAAJA Sections 1401, 1402, and 1403 concern “Federal authorization[s] or permit[s],” a term
defined to include “an authorization, permit, record of decision, right-of-way,” other specified
agency actions, and “any other approval or order that is necessary” to construct a project or
operate a project at full capacity.2 Each of those sections is discussed in turn below.

Section 1401

BAAJA Section 1401 would prohibit agencies from taking “any new action” that would (1)
“revoke, rescind, withdraw, terminate, suspend, amend, or alter” a federal authorization or permit
effective on or after September 16, 2026, or (2) “interfere with or prevent the construction or
operation at full capacity of a project that has secured all necessary Federal authorizations and
permits.”3

These prohibitions would be subject to five exceptions. Under Section 1401, an agency would be
able to take an otherwise prohibited action only if

Agencies invoking the fourth of those exceptions would be authorized to take only “reasonably

Before acting under any of Section 1401’s five exceptions, agencies would need to give written
notification to the “project sponsor or holder of” the authorization or permit.6 That notice would
need to provide “a detailed explanation of the action,” identify “the statutory authority relied on
for the action,” and include “the evidence supporting the action.”7

Section 1401 would authorize a project sponsor or holder of a permit or authorization to sue to
enforce its terms. This cause of action would alter the status quo in several respects. First, under
existing law, the party challenging agency action bears the burden of persuasion. By contrast,
Section 1401 would “place the burden” on the government. Second, in suits challenging
permitting actions, judicial review is currently governed by the statutes underlying the challenged
actions or by the Administrative Procedure Act (APA), and reviewing courts typically defer to
agencies’ factual findings and policy judgments.8 Section 1401 would supersede these existing
statutes and replace their deferential standards of review with a requirement that agencies
establish “by clear and convincing evidence” that permit modifications, rescissions, or
suspensions fell within one of Section 1401’s five enumerated exceptions.9 Third, at present there
exists no cross-statute authorization to challenge “any” agency “action” that interferes with
construction or operation at full capacity of full permitted facilities.10 By creating such a cause of
action,11 Section 1401 would potentially expand the scope of challengeable agency conduct.

Suits challenging agency action under Section 1401 could be brought in the federal courts of
appeals, or, if no existing statute provides for direct circuit-court review of the challenged action,
then in the district court where the affected project is located.12 Review in the U.S. Court of

appropriation that provides unlimited funds to pay monetary settlements and court-ordered
judgments, subject to certain requirements.20

projects related to energy production22—to sue in the federal district court in which their project
is located and challenge an agency’s allegedly unreasonable delay or improper denial of federal

Section 1402 defines “delay” as “more than 60 days beyond” specified deadlines, which for
completed environmental documents range from roughly one year for agency actions that do not
require an EIS under NEPA, to two years for those that do, plus an additional 90–120 days to
approve “all outstanding authorizations” after completing the review.24

To establish a claim of “unreasonabl[e]” delay or “improper[]” denial under Section 1402, a
plaintiff would need to show that

    1.   its application for the delayed or denied government action “satisfies all
        applicable Federal law”;
    2.   if granted, the requested permit or authorization would not be canceled for
        violating federal law, or for having been obtained through fraud, intentional
       concealment, or material misrepresentation; and
    3.  the government’s delay or inaction reflected a “pattern of disparate treatment”
        against projects of the same type that “occurred after” BAAJA’s enactment.25

Section 1402 would define a “pattern of disparate treatment” as a “substantial” increase in the
rate of “unreasonably delayed” or “improperly denied” government action for energy projects of
the same type, as compared with the rates of delayed or denied actions for projects of that type
over the previous five calendar years.26 This “empirically demonstrated statistical pattern” would
also need to be “the result of an intentional course” of government action “to create such a
pattern.”27

Section 1402 does not specify what would constitute a “substantial” increase in the relevant rates
of permit denial or delay, but to support a claim, such an increase would need to persist for at
least 180 days after BAAJA’s enactment for actions that do not require an EIS and for at least 280
days after BAAJA’s enactment for those that do.28

If a plaintiff produced prima facie evidence of disparate treatment, Section 1402 would shift the

The reviewing court would then need to base its decision only on the plaintiff’s evidence, the

Section 1402 would entitle prevailing plaintiffs to four forms of relief:

    1.  injunctive relief, “as provided by applicable Federal law”;
    2.  “reasonable” attorney fees and costs of litigation;
    3.  “economic damages, including any costs associated with the applicable denial or

    4.  “damages in an amount equal to not less than 50 percent and not more than 100
        percent of the total reasonably expected cost of construction of the project.”34

As in Section 1401, Section 1402 would direct courts to determine the recoverable amount of
reasonably expected construction costs by considering “the degree to which” the defendant
agency acted in bad faith, the degree to which “there exists a pattern of similar Federal
interference” in other projects, and the “actual degree” of economic harm to the project sponsor
or operator.35

To be eligible for a remedy under Section 1402, a plaintiff would need to “demonstrate” that it
possesses the “technical and financial ability” to construct the type of project at issue.36 A plaintiff
that received a remedy under Section 1402 would then need to “complete the applicable specific
type of covered project,” or, barring certain extenuating circumstances, would need to repay any
money it received. Section 1402 does not provide a deadline for project completion.37

As with Section 1401, funds awarded under Section 1402 would be payable from the Judgment
Fund.38

Section 1403

BAAJA Section 1403 would provide applicants for federal authorizations or permits that do not
require an EA or an EIS a “right to petition” for “judicial review of . . . compliance” with “the
deadline established under section 1402(c) with respect to the covered application.”41

Section 1403 would not require compliance with the deadline in 1402(c) if a federal agency could
establish by clear and convincing evidence that its delay was caused by

    1.  “a natural disaster, act of God, or act of war that reasonably impaired” an
       agency’s ability to comply;
    2.  “a national emergency” that necessitated delay;
    3.  “the covered application being based on fraud or material misrepresentations of
         fact”;
    4.  the applicant withholding necessary information after the agency has made “a
        reasonable and timely” request for that information; or
    5.  a lapse in appropriations lasting more than 14 days that precluded compliance.42

Section 1403 would authorize suits in the federal district court in which the project is located and
would direct reviewing courts to “establish an expedited schedule for briefing and disposition,”
and to issue a decision within 120 days of a suit’s commencement, barring “extraordinary
circumstances.”43

A prevailing plaintiff would be entitled to a court “order requiring a decision on” its application
within 60 days, and to other “injunctive relief, as appropriate, to give effect to this section.”44
Courts would be authorized “to retain jurisdiction to ensure compliance” with terms of their
orders.45

While possible, such outcomes are not certain. When interpreting statutes, courts apply a
presumption against implied repeals,63 and when specific statutory provisions appear to conflict
with more general provisions, courts often presume that the specific controls the general.64 Thus,
as commentators have noted,65 courts construing Section 1401 may conclude, in the absence of
explicit statutory language to the contrary, that Congress did not mean for Section 1401’s broad
prohibitions to sweep away more particularized statutory directives.

It is also possible that courts would disagree about the proper interpretation of Section 1401, with
some courts reading that provision to override provisions in existing statutes, such as the Clean
Water Act or Clean Air Act,66 and other courts reaching the opposite conclusion. Such divergent
interpretations could lead to inconsistent application of federal law.

If desired, Congress could provide express direction on whether Section 1401 would supersede or
leave intact existing statutory provisions. For instance, Congress could disclaim intent to modify
existing laws, or, conversely, provide that Section 1401 governs notwithstanding any other
provision of law. Alternatively, Congress could define “federal authorization or permit” or agency
“action” to exclude activities under certain statutes.

Section 1402’s Intent Requirement of Lack Thereof

Section 1402 would create a cause of action to challenge certain permit delays or denials that are
“due to a pattern of disparate treatment,”67 defined, in part, to mean an “empirically
demonstrated” “substantial” statistical increase in rates of “unreasonably delayed” or “improperly
denied” permits as compared to a five-year baseline.68 It is unclear, however, if the government
could be held liable under Section 1402 whenever its actions create such a pattern, or,
alternatively, only when it both creates a pattern of disparate treatment and intends to do so. Parts
of Section 1402 could be read to support either interpretation.

Section 1402 defines “pattern of disparate treatment” to mean not just a “statistical pattern,” but
one that is “the result of an intentional course of action undertaken by the Federal Government to
create such a pattern.”69 The latter definitional phrase suggests that plaintiffs suing under Section
1402 would need to establish the government’s subjective motivation to obtain relief.

Different provisions in Section 1402 suggest otherwise. For example, Section 1402(d)(1)(B)(i)–

“empirical evidence,” which plaintiffs could, but would not be required to, supplement with

record. Given these evidentiary constraints, Section 1402 appears to contemplate a finding of
liability without any direct evidence that the government intended to create a pattern of disparate
treatment.

The presence or absence of a specific intent requirement in Section 1402 has implications for the
scope of potential liability under that section. On the one hand, if a “pattern of disparate
treatment” means nothing more than a statistical increase in rates of permit delay or denial for a
given project type, then even lawful, good-faith permit denials could give rise to liability. On the
other hand, if the government can be held liable only when it acts with specific intent, then
agencies might evade liability by employing facially neutral policies that disproportionately
impact disfavored project types.
