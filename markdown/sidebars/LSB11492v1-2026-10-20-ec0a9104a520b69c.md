Legal Sidebari



The Fourth Amendment and the Speech or
Debate Clause


October 20, 2026

Various searches by federal agents of sitting Members of Congress can raise questions about the
intersection of Fourth Amendment principles and the privilege held by Members under the Speech or
Debate Clause of the Constitution. In one of these examples, reporting indicates that agents from
Homeland Security Investigations seized documents and the Member’s cellphone and laptop as part of an
immigration-related investigation. It has also been reported that the search in the previous example was
executed pursuant to a warrant and that the issuing judge imposed special procedural requirements on law
enforcement in light of the Member’s constitutional protections under the Speech or Debate Clause. The
Member has reportedly alleged that some of those protocols were not followed, including a requirement
that the Member and the House General Counsel be notified prior to execution of the warrant. Similar
questions regarding the Fourth Amendment and the Speech or Debate Clause were raised in September
2026 in an interim staff report detailing the Department of Justice’s (DOJ’s) access to Member phone
records during Special Counsel Jack Smtih’s investigation of the events of January 6, 2021.

This Legal Sidebar discusses the intersection of the Fourth Amendment’s prohibition on unreasonable
searches and seizures, the search warrant requirement under that Amendment, and the sometimes-
countervailing protections afforded by the Speech or Debate Clause.

Fourth Amendment Background

The Fourth Amendment prohibits unreasonable searches and seizures. As a result, law enforcement
generally must obtain a search warrant from a “neutral and detached magistrate” before entering a private
space to look for evidence. The Amendment states that “no Warrants shall issue, but upon probable cause,
supported by Oath or affirmation, and particularly describing the place to be searched, and the persons or
things to be seized.” The Supreme Court has characterized the probable-cause standard as “the kind of
‘fair probability’ on which ‘reasonable and prudent’” people act.

To obtain a search warrant, law enforcement must establish probable cause that the materials sought are
contraband or evidence that will “aid in a particular apprehension or conviction,” or otherwise “seizable
by virtue of being connected with criminal activity.” Law enforcement must also demonstrate “a fair
probability” that these materials “will be found in a particular place.” (This requirement may include a

Where a proposed search may implicate certain constitutional, statutory, or common-law privileges, such
as the attorney-client privilege, courts sometimes impose search protocols that law enforcement must
follow to preempt potential law enforcement access to privileged material. These processes can involve
preliminary review by a “filter team”—meaning a group of law enforcement agents and prosecutors who
are separate from the prosecution team and agree not to disclose any privileged information to the
prosecution team. In other situations, where materials might be especially sensitive or otherwise subject to
heightened concern, a judge or a special master can review the government’s privilege determinations or
conduct a filter review in the first instance.

DOJ policy requires certain procedural steps to be taken before law enforcement may execute a search
warrant in which a Member of Congress is involved. These steps include consultation with the
Department’s Public Integrity Section and the filing of an “Urgent Report” to Department leadership.

Speech or Debate Background

The Speech or Debate Clause ensures that, “for any Speech or Debate in either House,” Members of
Congress “shall not be questioned in any other Place.” The Clause is a key pillar in the separation of
powers and serves chiefly to protect the independence, integrity, and effectiveness of the legislative
branch by barring executive or judicial intrusions into the protected legislative sphere. Despite its text,
courts have found the protections afforded by the Clause extend well beyond “speeches” or “debates,”
instead providing Members with a series of privileges and immunities in both civil and criminal contexts
that are meant to shield Members from a “hostile judiciary” or “intimidation by the executive.” The
Clause does not, however, turn Members into “super-citizens” by providing them with a blanket
exemption from legal liability for all illegal acts. Rather, the Clause shelters only a certain class of
actions, known as “legislative acts,” which the Court has defined as conduct that is an “integral part of the
deliberative and communicative processes” through which Congress implements “matters which the
Constitution places within the jurisdiction of either House.”

The Clause’s protections are implemented in a variety of ways. First and foremost, the Clause provides
Members with absolute immunity from civil or criminal liability for all “legislative acts” taken in the
course of their official responsibilities. Second, even where absolute immunity is not applicable, the
Clause provides Members with complementary evidentiary and testimonial privileges. The evidentiary
component prohibits evidence of legislative acts from being introduced in court for use against a Member,
while the testimonial component of the Clause generally may be invoked when a Member is questioned
about his legislative acts, either in a trial, before a grand jury, or in a deposition. As a result of the breadth
of these protections, criminal prosecution of a Member can be complicated, but this impact on executive
enforcement of the law was fully understood at the time the Clause was adopted and was considered a
necessary consequence of protecting legislators from undue influence or intimidation.

Most relevant for this Sidebar, courts have divided over whether to extend the Clause further to prohibit
not only the use of legislative act material at trial, but also the mere disclosure of that material to the
executive branch—for example, during an administrative or criminal investigation. The U.S. Court of
Appeals for the District of Columbia (D.C. Circuit), the federal circuit court with jurisdiction over the
District of Columbia, has held that the Clause includes a “non-disclosure privilege,” which can prohibit
the disclosure of protected legislative act documents pursuant to a subpoena or during a search executed
as part of a criminal investigation. The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit and U.S. Court of
Appeals for the Third Circuit have rejected this “non-disclosure privilege,” considering it an undue
expansion of the Clause. Instead, these courts have held, at least in criminal cases, that the Clause

Intersection of the Fourth Amendment and the Speech or
Debate Clause

Various courts have addressed how the Fourth Amendment and the Speech or Debate Clause interact.
These cases suggest that whether a search violates the Speech or Debate clause may depend on
(1) whether the circuit at issue recognizes a non-disclosure privilege; (2) a fact-specific inquiry as to
whether the location of the search is realistically likely to contain legislative materials; and (3) whether a
reviewing court views filter team procedures as adequately protective of the privilege.

In United States v. Rayburn House Office Building, the D.C. Circuit held that a Federal Bureau of
Investigation (FBI) search of Rep. William Jefferson’s office pursuant to a warrant obtained from a
federal judge violated the Clause because the manner in which the search was carried out “denied the
Congressman any opportunity to identify and assert the privilege with respect to legislative materials
before their compelled disclosure to Executive agents” (emphasis added). The FBI had adopted special
procedures for the search (approved by the judge who had issued the warrant), which required that the
agents who searched the office have no other role in the investigation. All hard drives in the office were
copied, and these copies along with any collected documents were then turned over to a separate filter
team, which was required to assess the material as follows:

         Materials determined to be privileged or not responsive would be returned without dissemination to
         the prosecution team. Materials determined by the filter team not to be privileged would be turned
        over to the prosecution team, with copies to the Congressman’s attorney within ten business days
         of the search. Materials determined by the filter team to be potentially privileged would, absent the
        Congressman’s consent to Executive use of a potentially privileged document, be submitted to the
          district court for review, with a log and copy of such documents provided to the Congressman’s
         attorney within 20 business days of the search.

These precautions, the court held, were inadequate under the Clause because they “called for review by
FBI agents and the several members of the Justice Department filter team before the Congressman would
be afforded an opportunity to identify potentially privileged materials.” Any physical act of searching the
office, the court reasoned, would inevitably expose the agents to protected legislative materials in
violation of the non-disclosure privilege. For that same reason, however, the court held that the copying of
hard drives did not violate the Clause, since no executive branch agents had yet viewed the electronic
material. The opinion did not articulate a permissible procedure to be applied when the executive branch
obtains a warrant to search a Member’s office, but the court did suggest that an accommodation could be
reached whereby the office is “seal[ed]” and the Member is “afforded an opportunity to identify
potentially privileged legislative materials prior to any review by Executive agents.” Regardless, the D.C.
Circuit held that the Member must have the opportunity to assert the protections of the Speech or Debate
Clause prior to executive agents conducting any search of the office.

The majority opinion in Rayburn did not directly address whether its holding would extend beyond
Members’ offices to cover searches of other areas or devices. There was, however, language in the
opinion that could suggest that the principles established in Rayburn may appropriately be applied when
searches “occur[] in a location where legislative materials [are] inevitably to be found.” Moreover, a
circuit judge who concurred in the judgment but not in majority’s reasoning was critical of the breadth of
the majority’s non-disclosure privilege, expressing concern that criminal investigations would be
hampered by the need to provide a Member with advance notice “of any search of his property, including

In 2023, the D.C. Circuit considered the application of the Speech or Debate Clause to an FBI search of
Representative Scott Perry’s cell phone pursuant to a search warrant. In that case, the FBI obtained a
warrant to seize and create a forensic digital image of the phone, and then another warrant to review the
contents of that copy. Pursuant to Rayburn, the second warrant provided that Representative Perry “would
have an opportunity to assert any claims of privilege under the Speech or Debate Clause before the
government could review the phone’s content.” In considering Representative Perry’s claims of privilege,
the court rejected the government’s threshold argument that Rayburn should be limited to Members’
physical offices and consequently that the cell phone was not privileged at all. The court wrote that
“Rayburn, however, explained that the privilege applies whenever the Executive Branch searches ‘a
location where legislative materials [a]re inevitably to be found,’” and that a Member’s cell phone “is
realistically such a location.”

By contrast, in a criminal case against Representative Christopher Collins, a judge in the United States
District Court for the Southern District of New York (which is located within the jurisdiction of the U.S.
Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit) found that certain device and account data obtained by the
government pursuant to search warrants was not subject to the Speech or Debate privilege because, as a
threshold matter, the Clause did not include a non-disclosure privilege. Further, “in stark contrast” to the
Rayburn case, the court found that “the searches did not involve congressional offices or official accounts
but were of the personal electronic devices and accounts of certain current and/or former staff members of
Defendant Collins.” Finally, the court wrote that “even if the protections provided by the Speech or
Debate Clause includes a non-disclosure privilege, the procedures undertaken by the Government to
identify and segregate any protected materials without involving the prosecution team were sufficient
under the facts and circumstances presented here.” The government had used a filter team to ensure that
“any materials that were arguably legislative materials potentially protected by the Speech or Debate
Clause were not reviewed by or made available to the prosecution team.”

A recently reported search of a Member’s home occurred in the District of Columbia. As such, under the
controlling precedent of Rayburn, a court might look to whether the search is of a place “where legislative
materials [a]re inevitably to be found.” If so, a court might find that the non-disclosure privilege applies
and might require the Member to be given the opportunity to assert the protections of the Clause prior to
the disclosure of any privileged materials to executive branch agents. Whether a Member’s home—or a
personal office within a home—would be subject to the same protections as a Member’s office appears to
be an open question. In appealing Rayburn to the Supreme Court (an appeal that was denied), the
Solicitor General warned that the Rayburn rule “threaten[ed] to impede searches of Members’ homes,
vehicles, or briefcases.” “Within the District of Columbia,” the Solicitor General stated, “the United
States will no longer search for documents in an office of a Member located in his home because of
concerns that such a search could (under the court of appeals’ decision) taint an investigation.”

One reason why the Rayburn court seemed willing to create procedural guardrails for the executive
branch was that the “historical record” was “utterly devoid” of searches of a Member’s office—the search
at issue in Rayburn was the “first time a sitting Member’s congressional office [had] been searched by the
Executive.” Executive searches of a Member’s home, though also rare, have reportedly occurred on
several occasions. For example, the FBI had searched Representative Jefferson’s home and found $90,000
in cash in his freezer before executing a search of his office. While Representative Jefferson filed a
motion to suppress evidence from the search of his home, the motion does not appear to have raised the
Speech or Debate privilege.

With respect to the seizure of electronic devices, under the D.C. Circuit’s opinion regarding
Representative Perry’s cell phone, any search or seizure of a Representative’s electronic devices in the
District of Columbia—at least if the device is used for official purposes—would likely require the

Considerations for Congress

Congress has options for protecting potentially privileged material from executive branch search and
seizure and has on multiple instances legislated with respect to executive search and seizure authority.
Among Congress’s options, should it seek to enhance protections for potentially privileged materials, is to
impose procedural requirements on the execution of search warrants. Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure
41 governs processes for federal search warrants. Congress could choose to impose requirements in that
rule to govern the execution of warrants in special circumstances, like requiring filter teams or other
safeguards in cases where the search might expose privileged legislative materials. For example,
Congress could codify the procedure endorsed in Rayburn, requiring that a Member of Congress have an
opportunity to review materials being searched prior to law enforcement access to that material.

In another example, following revelations of law enforcement access to Senate toll records during the
Jack Smith special counsel investigation, Congress amended 2 U.S.C. § 6628 in 2025 to require that the
Senate Sergeant at Arms (SAA) or any third-party provider of an “electronic communication service” or
“remote computing service” for a Senate office notify the “Senate office,” whenever the SSA or provider
receives a subpoena or other “legal process” for covered Senate data, with certain exceptions when the
Senator is a target of the criminal investigation. The law does not define “legal process,” but that phrase
generally can refer to a variety of processes, including search warrants.

Alternatively, Congress may opt to leave the matter to the courts, including waiting to see whether the
Supreme Court takes up the non-disclosure privilege in light of the current circuit split.
