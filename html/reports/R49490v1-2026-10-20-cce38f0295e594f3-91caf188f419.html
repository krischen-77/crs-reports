Summary

As automated tools for decisionmaking, including those using artificial intelligence (AI), have grown in popularity and sophistication, they raise questions about how their use intersects with federal antidiscrimination laws. Federal laws address various kinds of discrimination, including disparate treatment and disparate impact. Disparate treatment generally occurs when a person makes a decision or classifies people on the basis of a protected trait, such as race, sex, religion, or another characteristic. Int'l Bhd. of Teamsters v. United States, 431 U.S. 324, 335 n.15 (1977). Disparate impact occurs when a person makes a decision using neutral criteria, but the decision adversely and disproportionately affects members of a protected group, and either the decisionmaker cannot put forth a legally adequate justification for using the criteria, or an alternative exists that would serve the decisionmaker's purposes with less of a disparate impact. Tex. Dep't of Hous. & Cmty. Affs. v. Inclusive Cmtys. Project, Inc., 576 U.S. 519, 524–25 (2015).

How disparate treatment and disparate impact frameworks apply to decisionmaking via automated, algorithmic tools (including AI) is not always clear. Some situations involving algorithmic decisionmaking would clearly be disparate treatment under present law. For example, a person covered by antidiscrimination law who intentionally designs an algorithm to discriminate on the basis of a protected characteristic, or who selects an algorithm because of its discriminatory effect on a protected group, engages in disparate treatment. See Pers. Adm'r of Mass. v. Feeney, 442 U.S. 256, 279 (1979). However, the law is unsettled on how to evaluate cases where no human consciously chooses to discriminate, and yet an automated system classifies people on the basis of protected characteristics. There is also substantial uncertainty over when an algorithmic system could be said to engage in disparate treatment via proxy—that is, classification based on a facially neutral trait that is closely correlated with a protected characteristic. The law is not clear on whether proxy discrimination always requires human intent to use a proxy to discriminate, or whether some characteristics are so closely related to protected traits that they are inherent proxies. It is also not clear how one could prove that certain AI systems are engaging in proxy discrimination if there is no way to know how some systems arrive at their conclusions. How laws handle such situations, and how difficult it is for plaintiffs to prove their case, may also depend on the causation and intent standards specific to each law.

Many unanswered questions persist regarding when and how the use of algorithms can give rise to disparate impact claims, as well. Using algorithmic tools can result in disparate impacts in several ways. An algorithm could use decision rules or targeted outcomes that proxy for protected characteristics or that are more or less favorable to certain groups. Algorithms could rely on criteria that are less predictive for certain groups than others, or fail to consider criteria that would make them more predictive for particular groups. AI systems may be trained on data that are affected by past discrimination, or that are over- or under-representative of particular groups, potentially leading to biased or less accurate outcomes for members of those groups. See Solon Barocas & Andrew D. Selbst, Big Data's Disparate Impact, 104 Cal. L. Rev. 671, 677–94 (2016).

There are already gray areas in legally significant elements of disparate impact doctrine. These uncertainties make it hard to predict how courts will respond when algorithmic decisionmaking is added to the mix. Courts may have to decide, for example, whether algorithmic systems should be analyzed as a whole or whether plaintiffs can, or must, challenge particular elements of how a system works. They may have to determine whether a decisionmaker can rely on the fact that an algorithm's predictions are accurate, or whether the decisionmaker has to posit or prove how the algorithm works or why it is accurate. Courts may also need to decide how much of a premium decisionmakers may place on cost savings or efficiency when justifying the use of an algorithm that causes a disparate impact. To evaluate plaintiffs' proposed less discriminatory alternatives, courts may need to grapple with whether plaintiffs must put forward alternatives that are as efficient and cost-effective as a challenged algorithm and what standards govern showing that an alternative—including an alternative algorithm—is available.

Congress has many options if it chooses to regulate algorithmic decisionmaking to prevent discrimination or bias. It may consider amending current antidiscrimination laws to better take into account the unique features of algorithmic decisionmaking, or it can pass laws specifically targeted at potential algorithmic bias. It may prohibit discrimination or require other, affirmative conduct by those that develop or deploy algorithmic systems, such as greater transparency, bias audits, or guaranteed human participation in certain decisions. Some potential pathways to mitigating the risks of algorithmic discrimination may raise constitutional concerns under the Takings Clause, First Amendment, and Equal Protection Clause. Congress may also consider how to balance the risks of discrimination with the risks of regulation, which could stifle the growth of automated tools, including those that may advance less discriminatory decisionmaking.

Introduction

Automated decisionmaking and artificial intelligence (AI) tools have generated interest from lawmakers about the effect of new technology and "big data" on privacy, security, and opportunity, among other areas. One area that has seen limited federal legislative development is in guarding against algorithmic discrimination. At various points in time, the executive branch,1 lawmakers,2 and others3 have voiced concern about the use of "biased" algorithms and AI systems in important areas of public life, including employment, housing, financial transactions, and beyond.4 At the same time, these and other commentators have championed the potential of algorithmic decisionmaking to root out discrimination and make recommendations or decisions beyond the reach of human bias.5

This report explores legal frameworks for evaluating claims of algorithmic discrimination. It reviews the broad legal principles that animate much of federal antidiscrimination law and how those principles may, or may not, apply when people use algorithms to support decisionmaking.

Background

Algorithmic Decisionmaking and AI

There is a lack of consensus on much of the terminology used to describe different forms of algorithmic decisionmaking and AI. These terms are defined below as used in this report. More detail on types and applications of AI can be found in other CRS products.6

In general, an algorithm is "a set of step-by-step instructions for solving a problem or completing a task."7 In the context of computing, an algorithm tells "a computer how to process inputs such as data to produce an output, such as a decision, prediction, or sorted list."8 While people can use algorithms without computers, in this report the term algorithmic decisionmaking means the use of automated, machine-based processes to make or assist with decisions, predictions, or recommendations. Algorithmic decisionmaking can be quite basic, using rules programmed by human experts. Software programmed to pass all college applicants with a grade point average above 3.0 to an admissions officer for further consideration and reject all others is an example of simple algorithmic decisionmaking. An algorithmic system could also use a more complex statistical formula and consider multiple variables (such as the difficulty of a college applicant's high school course of study, the quality of their high school, their extracurriculars, standardized test scores, or other factors).

Algorithmic systems may rely on AI. While definitions of AI vary,9 this report uses AI to refer to "algorithms and techniques that aim to give computer systems the ability to learn new concepts or tasks and solve complex problems in a manner that mimics human intelligence."10 When experts discuss AI models, they often mean computer programs engaged on some level in machine learning, a process in which a program uses algorithms and mathematical functions to process data and discover patterns and statistical relationships between variables. AI models can then use these discovered relationships to classify new inputs, that is, to make recommendations or predictions using previously unencountered data.11 An AI system may include an underlying AI model and a user-facing application that is powered by the model. Such systems include AI virtual assistants, chatbots, and screening tools, among others.

There are many different types of AI models, varying in, among other things, how they are trained, the mathematical functions that power their operations, and the outputs they generate. For example, AI models may be trained using supervised learning, unsupervised learning, or reinforcement learning.12 A subset of AI, known as generative AI, can be used to "generate new content," such as "text, images, videos, computer code, or music."13 Other AI models may classify data.14 This report uses the term AI in a very general sense, without differentiating between the various kinds of AI models available today.

As a hypothetical example of the use of one type of AI system, consider an employer using an AI system to screen resumes. The employer might feed the system data about employees the employer considers "successful." "Successfulness" is the employer's target variable, or the outcome a person seeks to predict using AI.15 The AI system may then discover statistical relationships between data points about these employees—perhaps that "successful" employees are statistically likely to have a certain employment background, use a particular computer browser, or own a particular car.16 The AI system may assign weights to the elements it finds to be statistically related to success or lack thereof, and use the resulting algorithms to rank new applicants (i.e., the program creates internal decision rules).17 In this scenario, the employer may or may not tell the program what "success" means, and the employer may or may not know which input data the AI system considers salient and what weight it has assigned them. It may not be possible to determine the reason for some AI systems' classifications due to the scale and complexity of certain AI models, a scenario often referred to as the "black box" problem.18 Additionally, there may not be a single, discoverable reason; the decision rules may change with each set of inputs.19

Antidiscrimination Legal Frameworks

Federal antidiscrimination laws have different, though sometimes overlapping, scopes. The laws vary in whom they cover, the decisions they govern, and the characteristics they protect. The Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause and the Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause, for example, apply only to government actors and subject laws that classify on certain bases, including race, national origin, and sex, to more rigorous scrutiny by courts.20 Other federal antidiscrimination schemes are differently targeted. Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 (Title VII), for example, generally prohibits "employers," "employment agencies," and "labor organizations" from making employment-related decisions on the basis of race, color, national origin, religion, and sex.21 The Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA) forbids "creditors" from discriminating in credit transactions on the basis of race, color, religion, national origin, sex, marital status, age, or source of income.22 Other antidiscrimination laws prohibit discrimination on the basis of race,23 age,24 or disability25 by recipients of federal funds.

In addition to who and what is covered by an antidiscrimination law, the types of claims available, and how to prove them, vary from law to law. Two of the primary forms of discrimination recognized in federal antidiscrimination law are disparate treatment and disparate impact.26 The specifics of disparate treatment and disparate impact vary depending on the law; this report largely focuses on big-picture principles common to many approaches.

The next sections provide an overview of the fundamentals of disparate treatment and disparate impact claims, followed by a discussion of how these legal frameworks may apply to allegations of algorithmic discrimination.

Fundamentals of Disparate Treatment Doctrine

Intentional Classifications

Disparate treatment occurs when someone makes a decision based at least in part on another's protected trait, such as race or sex.27 Disparate treatment can involve formal classifications, such as when a school district assigns students to schools based on race.28 It can also involve the intentionally discriminatory application of facially neutral policies, that is, policies that do not overtly classify on the basis of a protected characteristic. For example, the selective enforcement of a neutral school dress code against only members of a protected group is a form of disparate treatment.29

A party also engages in disparate treatment when it adopts a facially neutral policy with discriminatory intent.30 To plead a disparate treatment claim on such facts, a plaintiff must show that a defendant adopted the policy "'[b]ecause of,' not merely 'in spite of,' its adverse effects upon an identifiable group."31 For example, an apartment complex that enacts a rule against placing decorations on doors and doorposts may be engaging in disparate treatment if the company adopted the rule to harass or drive away Jewish tenants (who may want to place mezuzot on their doorways).32

This third type of intentional discrimination can take the form of proxy discrimination. Proxy discrimination occurs when an entity classifies people based on facially neutral traits that are closely correlated with protected characteristics.33 An example is redlining, where financial institutions discriminate against consumers based on zip codes, which, due to residential segregation, can be an effective proxy for race.34 Understanding proxy discrimination is relevant to understanding algorithmic discrimination because, as discussed below, algorithms that are programmed to avoid considering protected characteristics might nevertheless rely on proxies.35

The law on proxy discrimination as disparate treatment is not entirely clear.36 The Supreme Court has at times held that a regulated entity must be "actually motivated" by the relationship between the proxy and the protected characteristic for proxy discrimination to support a disparate treatment claim.37 The Court may presume such motivation when a policy targets traits or activities that are "an irrational object of disfavor . . . if they also happen to be engaged in exclusively or predominantly by a particular class of people."38 For example, "[a] tax on wearing yarmulkes is a tax on Jews."39 At other times, the Supreme Court has suggested that some classifications might proxy for protected characteristics without considering motivation at all.40 However, one recent case adds weight to the view that a close correlation between two characteristics is not enough, standing alone, to show intentional discrimination via proxy. In United States v. Skrmetti, the Court held that laws that prohibit certain medical care to treat gender dysphoria, but allow the same treatments for other conditions, do not classify on the basis of transgender status, despite the close link between having gender dysphoria and being transgender.41

On the other hand, some circuit courts have suggested that certain characteristics may be considered proxies for protected traits because of their inherent connection to those traits, without requiring evidence of motivation.42 Some of the proxy classifications cited as examples by these courts could fall within the category of "irrational object[s] of disfavor" that "also happen to be engaged in exclusively or predominantly by a particular class of people"43—for example, discrimination based on "gray hair"44—but not all of them necessarily would be considered "irrational." The Third Circuit,45 for example, has held that discrimination in the terms of employee health benefits based on Medicare eligibility is age discrimination because "Medicare eligibility 'follow[s] ineluctably upon attaining age 65.'"46 Not all circuit courts consider characteristics with a close fit to a protected trait to be inherent proxies.47

Mixed Motives and "Rational" Discrimination

In some cases, a defendant may have multiple motives for a decision, one or more of which may be discriminatory while others are not. Federal laws take different approaches to these so-called "mixed-motive" cases.48 Under Title VII, for example, so long as the protected characteristic is a "motivating factor" for the decision, the fact that other factors may have been determinative does not matter; the decision is still illegal.49 Courts applying the Equal Protection Clause take a different approach: Once a plaintiff shows that a decision was at least in part motivated by a discriminatory purpose, the burden shifts to the defendant to prove it would have made the same decision without the illegal consideration (and thereby avoid liability).50

Not all laws recognize "motivating factor" discrimination or shift the burden to the defendant once some discriminatory intent is shown. Under the Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA),51 for example, private-sector employees must prove that age (greater than forty) was a "but-for" cause of any adverse treatment. They cannot bring disparate treatment claims if their employers improperly considered their age but would have reached the same decisions regardless, and plaintiffs always bear the burden of showing age was determinative.52

A defendant's motive is generally discriminatory even if the defendant's reliance on a protected trait is arguably "rational."53 In other words, even if a protected trait is strongly correlated with or has a causal relationship to a factor a person may legitimately consider, the decisionmaker often may not take the protected trait into account.54 For example, the Supreme Court has held that Title VII does not allow employers to require female employees to make higher contributions to their pension funds, even if one accepts that women generally live longer than men.55 Employers also cannot refuse to hire women for physically demanding jobs, even if sex is correlated with certain physical aptitudes.56 Strict limitations on "rational" discrimination are a significant consideration when examining algorithmic discrimination. AI models may discover hidden relationships, including correlations, between input data and desired outcomes. The AI model may then offer recommendations, make predictions, or take action based on those machine-identified patterns and trends. Under federal antidiscrimination law, the fact that a correlation is real does not make acting on that correlation legal.

Fundamentals of Disparate Impact Doctrine

Disparate impact discrimination occurs when a protected group experiences disproportionately adverse effects from a facially neutral practice (that is, a practice that does not take protected characteristics into account), and the regulated entity lacks an appropriate justification.57 Not all federal antidiscrimination laws provide for disparate impact liability. Under the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act of 2008 (GINA) and Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 (Title VI), for example, plaintiffs may bring only disparate treatment claims.58 Under laws that allow for disparate impact claims, such as Title VII,59 the ADEA,60 and the Fair Housing Act (FHA),61 a party may be liable without any intent to discriminate;62 disparate impact is therefore sometimes referred to as "unintentional discrimination."63 The purpose of disparate impact law, the Supreme Court has said, is to eliminate "artificial, arbitrary, and unnecessary barriers to" opportunity "when the barriers operate invidiously to discriminate on the basis of racial or other impermissible classification."64 Disparate impact doctrine also "plays a role in uncovering discriminatory intent" by "permit[ing] plaintiffs to counteract unconscious prejudices and disguised animus that escape easy classification as disparate treatment."65 At the same time, the Court has said that disparate impact law must allow regulated entities to exercise appropriate judgment without undue second-guessing from judges.66

The precise formulation of disparate impact doctrine varies somewhat depending on the applicable law and facts, but generally speaking, a basic disparate impact case proceeds through three inquiries:

  • 1. whether the plaintiff can show that a regulated party engages in a specific practice that causes a significant disparate effect on the basis of a protected trait;
  • 2. whether the regulated party can advance a legally adequate justification for the practice; and
  • 3. if there is such a justification, whether the plaintiff can prove there is an alternative practice that would serve the regulated party's legitimate interest with less of a disparate impact.67
Disparity Caused by a Specific Practice

Typically, plaintiffs in disparate impact cases rely on statistical disparities between outcomes for members of a protected group versus others.68 Plaintiffs might attempt to show, for example, that a bank approves credit applications at lower rates for members of a particular race, or that a school suspends or expels students of one sex at higher rates. On its own, however, a statistical disparity in outcomes does not make out an initial disparate impact claim; the plaintiff must also identify a specific practice or policy of the defendant's that causes the disparity.69 The Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned that plaintiffs must tie disparities to causes within defendants' control so that defendants are held liable only for their own acts, rather than general social conditions; otherwise, the Court has warned, defendants would be forced to turn to forbidden preferences and quotas to avoid disparate impact liability.70

Disparate impact plaintiffs commonly challenge the use of particular criteria or tests.71 Plaintiffs may also challenge defendants' subjective decisionmaking processes in addition to more concrete policies or criteria.72

Legally Adequate Justification

Once a plaintiff has shown that a defendant's policy or practice causes a disproportionate impact, the burden generally shifts to the defendant to give its reasons for the challenged practice. The standard for a legally adequate reason varies by statute and is often unclear. In its first decision developing the concept of disparate impact liability, the Supreme Court articulated the standard in different ways. In that case, Griggs v. Duke Power Co., the Court left unclear whether Title VII required employers to show a challenged practice was truly "necessary" or only "related" to the job in question, stating: "The touchstone is business necessity. If an employment practice which operates to exclude [a protected group] cannot be shown to be related to job performance, the practice is prohibited."73 Later in the same opinion, the Court said that any employment requirement "must have a manifest relationship to the employment in question."74 Subsequent Title VII cases also used different formulations.75

Then, in 1989, the Court in Wards Cove Packing Co. v. Atonio held that "the dispositive issue is whether a challenged practice serves, in a significant way, the legitimate employment goals of the employer."76 Congress responded with the Civil Rights Act of 1991, which codified the concepts of business necessity and job relatedness in Title VII.77 Congress declared its intent to capture those concepts as "enunciated by the Supreme Court in Griggs v. Duke Power Co., 401 U.S. 424 (1971), and in the other Supreme Court decisions prior to Wards Cove Packing Co. v. Atonio, 490 U.S. 642 (1989)," a case Congress thought had "weakened" Title VII.78 The statutory text of Title VII now requires a challenged practice to be "job related for the position in question and consistent with business necessity."79 The Supreme Court's different formulations prior to Ward's Cove, however, leave in doubt whether "job relatedness" and "business necessity" are the same or separate standards and whether a practice must be truly "necessary," or meet some lower bar, to be legally sufficient.80 The Court has injected further variability into how to evaluate the legitimate reason by highlighting the importance of deferring to defendants' expertise.81

Moreover, while Title VII often influences how courts interpret disparate impact liability under other laws,82 not all laws apply the same standards. Some statutes and regulations implementing disparate impact liability refer, like Title VII, to practices that are "necessary" to the defendant's operations or the advancement of its interests.83 The ADEA, by contrast, requires only that an employer's practice be "reasonable."84 Complicating the picture even more, while Congress rejected parts of Wards Cove in amendments to Title VII, some courts have applied all of Wards Cove to disparate impact claims under other laws, such as the FHA.85 In these cases, defendants must prove that challenged practices "serve[], in a significant way, the[ir] legitimate . . . goals."86

Less Discriminatory Alternative

If the defendant proves a legally adequate justification for a challenged practice, the third and final inquiry in many disparate impact cases is whether the plaintiff can show that there is an alternative practice that would cause less of a disparate impact. An academic review of Title VII disparate impact cases observed that "relatively few cases have explored the concept" of the less discriminatory alternative.87

Generally, courts appear to require plaintiffs to propose an alternative to the challenged practice that is "available," "valid," and "less discriminatory."88 Formulations of these factors vary from court to court and law to law. Broadly speaking, to be "available," a practice must be concrete, not "vague" or "theoretical,"89 and the defendant must have "kn[o]w[n] or should have known" about it.90 The alternative must be at least "substantially equally valid," meaning, in the employment context, that it "would lead to a workforce [that is] substantially equally qualified."91 Some courts, including a plurality of the Supreme Court, have said the alternative should be "equally as effective as the challenged practice in serving the [defendant's] legitimate business goals," taking into account "factors such as the cost or other burdens."92 Little guidance is available on how burdensome on the defendant a less discriminatory alternative can be before it is not equally effective.93 Finally, as mentioned, the alternative must cause less of a disparate impact.94

Plaintiffs must support their showing with specific evidence as to each point; they may not meet their burden with speculation that a different approach would have less of an impact or would effectively accomplish the defendant's legitimate objectives.95

Tensions Between Disparate Treatment and Disparate Impact

There are limits on how far people can go to eliminate disparate impacts without running afoul of prohibitions on disparate treatment. The Supreme Court has cautioned that disparate impact liability may not be used to require "numerical quotas," or to peg compliance to eliminating racial imbalance.96 In Ricci v. DeStefano, the Court went further and held that New Haven, CT, violated Title VII when it invalidated the results of a firefighter promotion process that had ended in no eligible Black candidates.97 While the city argued that it was lawfully attempting to avoid disparate impact liability, the Court held that it had intentionally discriminated against White and Hispanic officers when it acted "because of the statistical disparity based on race."98 Title VII allowed such race-based decisionmaking after the results of a selection process were known, the Court held, only in the face of a "strong basis in evidence of disparate-impact liability."99

How far Ricci goes to limit attempts to reduce disparate impacts is not fully known. In Ricci, an employer voided the results of a process that had concluded, resulting in identifiable people losing a benefit because of their race.100 Parties may have more flexibility to modify their policies or practices to eliminate prospective disparate impacts.101 Post-Ricci, the Supreme Court opined in dicta in an FHA case that government may "foster diversity and combat racial isolation with race-neutral tools."102

Ricci is also a statutory decision. The Court declined to issue a general rule on whether and when policies undertaken to avoid disparate impacts, or government mandates for private parties to avoid disparate impacts, constitute unconstitutional intentional discrimination.103 Some Supreme Court Justices, both then and now, have expressed concern that public actors violate the Constitution when they reformulate policy to avoid prospective disparate racial impacts or increase racial diversity.104 Concurring in Ricci, Justice Scalia wrote that the case's

resolution . . . merely postpones the evil day on which the Court will have to confront the question: Whether, or to what extent, are the disparate-impact provisions of Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 consistent with the Constitution's guarantee of equal protection? . . . Title VII's disparate-impact provisions place a racial thumb on the scales, often requiring employers to evaluate the racial outcomes of their policies, and to make decisions based on (because of) those racial outcomes. That type of racial decisionmaking is, as the Court explains, discriminatory.105

While that view has not garnered a majority on the Supreme Court, it was echoed by the Department of Justice (DOJ) in a 2026 opinion from the Office of Legal Counsel (OLC), which contended that Title VII disparate impact liability suffers from a "fundamental problem" in that it "tends to incent—and even coerce—employers to make race-based decisions."106

Applying Antidiscrimination Legal Frameworks to Algorithmic Decisionmaking

Algorithms have been used in at least some decisions covered by antidiscrimination law for many years. Lenders, for example, have long used credit scores to determine applicants' creditworthiness.107 Technological advances, particularly in data analysis, machine learning, and AI, have raised new questions under antidiscrimination law. Commentators have observed that machine-supported or AI-powered decisionmaking (including the data, algorithms, and models involved) can be too complex for humans to understand; that advances have broadened the data that can be considered in decisionmaking; and that automated decisionmaking is being used in an increasing number of domains and has the potential to cause much larger-scale impacts than human decisionmaking.108 Technological advances and their consequences raise novel questions as to how antidiscrimination law may operate when algorithms make or contribute to decisions.

Disparate Treatment

How Algorithms May Result in Disparate Treatment

Discriminatory intent could make its way into algorithmic decisionmaking in a number of ways. The most straightforward example may be when someone intentionally designs an algorithm to rely on a protected characteristic in decisionmaking. In one matter, for example, the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) alleged that an employer violated Title VII and the ADEA when it programmed software to reject applicants older than certain ages, with different thresholds for men and women.109 In another, the Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) and DOJ alleged that Meta violated the FHA by allowing housing advertisers to use Facebook to target or screen out consumers based on protected characteristics, such as sex, religion, and family status.110 Housing advertisers could, for example, elect to screen out people Meta had identified as "Christian" or "moms of grade school kids."111 Black patients have sued hospitals and the nonprofit managing the national organ transplant waiting list for disparate treatment, claiming that until recently, the algorithm that ranks candidates for kidney transplants expressly factored in race and caused them to wait longer for transplants.112

Algorithms may facilitate intentional discrimination by proxy as well. In the case against Meta, for example, HUD and DOJ alleged that Facebook's algorithm also allowed advertisers to target close proxies for, among other things, disability and religion, such as users' interest in "service animals" or "Hijab Fashion."113 Former Meta employees have separately alleged that Meta used AI to evaluate employee productivity to make decisions about layoffs, and the AI system used metrics that proxied, at least in part, for employees' protected leave and disability accommodations.114 In another reported incident, a company vetting a resume-screening tool determined that the algorithm favored applicants named "Jared," a potential proxy for male identity.115

A party could also select a facially neutral algorithm intending to harm a protected group. In one lawsuit, for example, a fair housing nonprofit alleged that a developer of tenant-screening software designed its program to consider applicants' criminal records with the intent to allow housing providers to discriminate based on race.116 Although the system did not directly consider race, the plaintiffs were able to plead a disparate treatment claim by alleging the defendant's discriminatory intent.117

Open Questions on Disparate Treatment and Algorithms

The foregoing examples represent relatively straightforward applications of the disparate treatment framework to potential algorithmic discrimination. Yet using algorithms and AI can also introduce new variables into these frameworks that courts have yet to consider in any depth. Two such examples are addressed below.

Can Defendants Who Don't Understand an Algorithm Be Liable?

The law is not clear on how the disparate treatment framework applies when a party uses an algorithmic tool into which they have little or no insight. Courts have not, until recently, had to consider situations in which a defendant does not know the rules governing a challenged decision. While federal statutes and the Constitution do not use these terms, courts often discuss disparate treatment by referencing the defendant's "purpose," "motive," or "intent," words that suggest a level of consciousness by a human decisionmaker.118 Indeed, the term "disparate treatment" is often used synonymously with "intentional discrimination."119 To be clear, courts do not require a malicious motive: people are liable if they use prohibited classifications for ostensibly benevolent reasons.120 Courts have also frequently stated that there is no need to inquire into motive when confronted with explicit classifications.121 In such cases, however, the defendant has presumptively created or known about the classification.122 As one scholar puts it, courts assume that "decisionmakers possess 'transparency of mind'—that they are aware of the reasons why they are about to make, or have made, a particular . . . decision."123 That assumption may be untenable in many situations where defendants use algorithms that they do not understand.124

Whether federal antidiscrimination law covers someone's unknowing use of a discriminatory algorithm, even an algorithm that expressly classifies using protected characteristics, is uncertain. As referenced above, at times courts have stated that using a prohibited classification alone creates disparate treatment liability.125 Courts have made these statements, however, in the context of defendants accused of intentionally making the relevant classifications.126 It is difficult to predict how courts will resolve cases absent that context. Scholars disagree as to whether current law protects against discrimination when a regulated party does not know that an algorithm relied on a prohibited trait.127 Several commentators posit that express classifications violate antidiscrimination law regardless of intent.128 Others think it likely that, doctrinally, "an AI cannot intentionally discriminate independently of any human."129

At least one court seems to favor the view that human intent must be involved. In Mobley v. Workday, Inc., one of the few cases alleging algorithmic discrimination to have generated judicial guidance to date, a job applicant alleged discrimination by the developer of resume-screening software.130 The district court acknowledged that the challenged algorithm might use proxy information to classify applicants based on race and age131 but stated that the plaintiff could not plead a disparate treatment claim against the software developer unless the developer subjectively "intended" disparate outcomes.132 Discussing a hypothetical, the district court also suggested that an employer would not be liable for intentional discrimination if it used an algorithm that, unbeknownst to the employer, a third party designed to intentionally discriminate against graduates of historically Black colleges.133

On the other hand, a different federal district court ruled that the government was liable for intentional discrimination under the Equal Protection Clause even when it outsourced decisionmaking to an AI system. In American Council of Learned Societies v. National Endowment for the Humanities (NEH), plaintiffs challenged the terminations of their NEH grants.134 As described by the court, discovery revealed that government staff had selected which grants to terminate in large part by instructing ChatGPT to identify grants involving "DEI," without elaborating further on the meaning of that term, without understanding the AI's methodology, and without reviewing the AI's results for accuracy.135 The court found that the results expressly classified on the basis of protected characteristics, including race, sex, and religion.136 For example, the court found that the government had made the "chilling" decision to terminate a grant to produce a Holocaust documentary because the project "centered on 'Jewish cultures' and 'female' voices."137 The court ruled that regardless of who, or what, generated the classifications, "[w]hen the Government adopts AI-generated classifications as part of its official actions, those classifications are the Government's own for constitutional purposes."138 In this case, ChatGPT generated explanations for its classifications that were visible to the government decisionmakers and clearly implicated protected characteristics (although it is not clear whether the decisionmakers reviewed those explanations).139 Many of those explanations, the court found, showed that ChatGPT's conclusions "rest[ed] solely on the project's focus on a particular race," such as an explanation classifying a grant as "DEI" because the project "invit[ed] reflection on the Asian American experience."140 Whether a court would reach a similar result in a case where plaintiffs could prove an AI system relied on discriminatory classifications, but indicia of discrimination were less available to the defendant, is unknown.

Beyond cases directly addressing the unknowing use of an allegedly discriminatory algorithm, courts at times have considered cases where someone who influences a decision exhibits prejudice, but the ultimate decisionmaker lacks conscious discriminatory intent. In the employment discrimination context, for example, courts have considered cases where the ultimate decisionmaker unknowingly acts as a "conduit" for the discriminatory animus of a lower-level supervisor or coworker.141 For example, an employer may fire an employee based on a supervisor's poor performance review, not knowing that the supervisor was biased. Courts refer to these types of matters as "cat's paw" cases.142

The Supreme Court has ruled that employers may be liable in cat's paw cases when they act as a conduit for the discriminatory intent of low-level supervisors.143 The employer is liable only if the supervisor with discriminatory animus acted with the intent that the employer take some adverse action against the victim.144 The Court has not decided whether employers are liable when a coworker's discriminatory animus influences the decisionmaking process.145

These facets of the doctrine may make it difficult to analogize an employer's use of a discriminatory algorithm to a cat's paw case. First, a court would have to decide whether an algorithm was more like a biased low-level supervisor or more like a coworker. Second, these cases still hinge on intentional human actions. An algorithm cannot intend an adverse action against an employee, which may make it impossible to use precedent from cat's paw cases in algorithmic discrimination matters.

How Could Plaintiffs Prove an AI Model Engaged in Disparate Treatment Via Proxy?

AI developers concerned about discrimination could further complicate the question of what constitutes disparate treatment by excluding or hiding information about protected characteristics from AI training data, or by programming AI models to not consider protected characteristics in their decision rules.146 An AI model's decision rules may still reflect protected characteristics, contrary to its developers' intent, because protected characteristics may be correlated with other data points.147 Some commentators posit that given a rich enough dataset, an AI model will invariably create decision rules that use proxies to incorporate the protected characteristics of people whose data are included, so long as the protected characteristics are correlated with the algorithm's target variable.148

This concept is called omitted variable bias. Omitted variable bias occurs when a predictive variable is omitted from the data on which a statistical model is built, leading the model to misattribute the omitted variable's determinative effects to other variables included in the data. In that case, "the included variable will partially proxy for the omitted characteristic and the estimated impact of the included variable will be biased."149 Two law professors use the example of an AI model that attempts to select police recruits and favors recruits who listen to certain pop songs. It could be that a preference for a particular style of music is proxying for some forbidden demographic characteristic, like race, sex, or age. On the other hand, perhaps a preference for this musical style is correlated with "motivation to exercise," and such motivation could be the "relevant hidden variable" that the model cannot directly measure.150 The hidden variable could even be a factor or factors not known by social scientists or understood by humans.

To the extent users or a court would be able to identify any explanation for an AI system's output, it may not be possible to determine what, if any, omitted variables are at play.151 It would matter to the law whether the hypothetical AI model described above had identified a relationship between music preferences and suitability as a police officer separate from a relationship between music preference and a forbidden demographic trait. As previously discussed, under many antidiscrimination laws, "rational" discrimination is illegal. Even if a protected trait like race, sex, or age is correlated with a legitimate target variable—such as likelihood of graduating from a local police academy—acting on that correlation may be illegal.152 In many cases, however, the protected trait is correlated both with the target variable and with other variables that are legitimate to consider. To take a real-world example, ECOA allows financial institutions to consider income when setting the terms of credit transactions, even if income is correlated with sex, race, or other protected traits.153 Just because an AI system's output is correlated with a protected trait does not mean that the protected trait is a hidden cause of the output.

Complicating matters further, antidiscrimination laws generally do not bar irrational or arbitrary classifications (if not based on protected traits), and this is especially true of laws that do not prohibit disparate impact discrimination.154 If a financial institution decides to deny credit to people born on odd days of the year, for instance, federal antidiscrimination law generally would not prohibit it.

If a human decisionmaker claimed that they denied a consumer credit because of seemingly arbitrary criteria, a court might scrutinize whether those criteria masked discriminatory intent, especially if the arbitrary criteria were correlated with a protected trait. Courts and juries may infer that seemingly irrational decisionmaking—like selecting police recruits based on music taste—is likely a pretext for prohibited discrimination.155 In cases involving human decisionmakers, other evidence of their motive may also be available.156 In cases involving AI models, without an assumption of human motivation behind the decision and potentially without any explanation for why a model incorporated a particular variable, courts may have a more difficult time disentangling true proxies for prohibited traits from proxies for other omitted, permissible variables, whether those variables appear rational or not.

Additionally, proxy discrimination is already a legal gray area. As discussed above, the law is not clear as to whether some traits are so closely related to protected characteristics as to be inherent proxies,157 and assuming they can be, there is no clear standard for how close a proxy must be to a protected characteristic to count. Potential proxy discrimination in the context of an AI system that assigns weights to a constellation of traits that together may stand in for a protected characteristic is a new development for courts to confront in an area of existing uncertainty.158

The more a plaintiff must know about how a complex algorithm works, the more difficult the case may be to prove. In cases under laws that do not allow for motivating-factor claims, like some ADEA matters, plaintiffs could have to prove both that the algorithm considered variables that served only to proxy for protected characteristics and that those variables were a but-for cause of an adverse action against them.159 That is, they would have to prove that the algorithm would have produced a different output if it had not considered the proxy data. In many cases, plaintiffs may not even be able to show what variables an AI model incorporated into its decision rules. With many state-of-the-art AI models, it may be impossible to understand any reason for the model's output.160 Plaintiffs may never be able to prove disparate treatment in such cases.

Disparate Impact

How Algorithms May Cause Disparate Impacts

Algorithmic decisionmaking could result in particular groups experiencing disproportionately negative outcomes in several ways. As a non-exhaustive list of examples, disparate impacts could result when algorithms rely on proxies for protected characteristics; when a model uses a target variable or decision rules that disfavor certain groups; when a model relies on criteria that are less predictive for particular groups; or when models are trained on biased data.

An algorithm's consideration of proxies for protected characteristics, even if not considered or proven to be disparate treatment, could give rise to disparate impact claims.161 Take a hypothetical AI system used to evaluate employees for "fit" with an organization's culture that disfavors applicants who use the email sign-off "Have a blessed day." Even if one could not prove the system was simply disfavoring religious applicants, such a system could result in an adverse impact on people of certain faiths, requiring the employer to present a legitimate reason for using it.

As with human decisionmaking, an algorithmic decisionmaker could also focus on target variables or decision rules that favor certain groups above others, even if most people would not consider those criteria closely related enough to a particular group to be proxy variables. For example, a landlord who designed an algorithm to select for predicted renter tenure could end up disproportionately rejecting military families if they tend to move more often than others.162 As another example, one lawsuit alleged that a tenant-screening tool had an unjustifiable disparate impact on racial minorities by considering arrests without convictions, because, the plaintiffs contended, minorities "are significantly more likely to be arrested, charged, and indicted."163

A model could be built or trained in a way that results in the model emphasizing attributes that are less predictive for some groups than others or that results in the model failing to consider factors that are particularly predictive for certain groups.164 For example, one study showed that an algorithm that used health care spending as a proxy for health care need understated the needs of Black patients. Black consumers, the study found, spend less on health care than comparably sick White consumers.165 Separately, one lawsuit alleged that a tenant-screening tool disproportionately screened Black and Hispanic renters out of housing in part because it failed to factor in housing vouchers when considering an applicant's likelihood of making payments.166 Medical researchers have cautioned against training AI models on datasets using measurements from "wearable technologies that perform sub-optimally or lack validation in people with darker skin."167

The data used to train an AI model could be biased in a number of ways as well.168 The training data could itself be affected by discrimination. For example, a school may train an AI model to spot the kinds of applicants it wants to admit using data from past students, and those past students may have been admitted using discriminatory criteria.169 An insurance company could flag claims for additional scrutiny based on past data reflecting the bias of human claims adjudicators. In a lawsuit against a home insurance company, Black policyholders alleged that the company's claims processing algorithms "use inputs that correlate with race or learn from 'historically biased housing and claims data.'"170 An image generator trained on pictures scraped from the internet may generate images that reflect widespread social stereotypes. One study found that one such model "exhibits a bias towards the sexualization of women."171 In circumstances involving the use of biased training data, commentators have asserted that an AI model "will necessarily infer rules that exhibit the same prejudice."172

Training data could also be biased in the sense that they are unrepresentative, and algorithms may "systematically disadvantage those who are under- or overrepresented in the dataset."173 For example, if certain demographic groups are underrepresented in the training data for a facial recognition model, that model may misidentify members of those groups at higher rates.174 An AI model trained to estimate creditworthiness based on consumer banking data may lack adequate information about groups that tend to be unbanked.175 The model may therefore predict the behavior of members of that group less reliably.176 Some scholars predict that these sorts of errors will tend to affect "historically disadvantaged groups at higher rates" because their "lives are less 'datafied' than the general population's."177 Models may also extrapolate inaccurate conclusions about groups that are overrepresented, such as a predictive policing model that concludes members of one race are more likely to commit crimes because it is trained on a dataset that overrepresents that group in the criminal population.178

Open Questions on Disparate Impact and Algorithms

Under antidiscrimination laws that allow for disparate impact claims—which, as explained above, is not all of them—using disparate impact doctrine would relieve plaintiffs in algorithmic discrimination cases of the burden of proving intent or showing what variables an algorithm based its output on. Nevertheless, these cases would likely not be straightforward. There are a number of unanswered questions about how disparate impact doctrine might apply in this context.

What Is the Specific Practice Causing the Disparity?

As discussed above, disparate impact plaintiffs must identify the specific practice that causes a disparity. In algorithmic discrimination cases, one question is whether using an algorithm or AI system is a specific enough practice, or whether plaintiffs will need to prove something more detailed—how the algorithm works, bias in the training data, or specific problematic inputs, for example.179 Title VII allows plaintiffs to analyze the entire decisionmaking process as "one employment practice" when the "elements" of the process "are not capable of separation for analysis."180 While that provision seems to point to allowing plaintiffs to challenge an algorithm or AI system as a whole, whether courts will follow that interpretation in practice, and whether they will apply that rule outside of Title VII, remains to be seen.181

Relatedly, disparate impact claims are often brought as class actions, which require the plaintiffs to show a common cause for their injuries.182 At least one court has refused to certify a class alleging discriminatory mortgage lending when the plaintiffs pled only that the lender's algorithm led to discriminatory results. The court held that the plaintiffs had not identified a specific enough common reason for their injuries.183 The data necessary to prove commonality, or other parts of a disparate impact claim, may be difficult to obtain. For example, the plaintiffs in Mobley v. Workday, the case discussed above alleging that the defendant's resume-screening software causes disparate impacts, have struggled to obtain data about applicants to third-party employers who used the defendant's software. According to the plaintiffs, several of the defendant's customers responded to subpoenas for the data by directing the plaintiffs back to the defendant.184 The court has refused to order the defendant to produce the data, agreeing with the defendant that the data belong to its individual customers and are not in the defendant's legal "possession" because the information "resides on segregated, password-protected customer tenants that Workday is contractually prohibited from freely accessing."185 Commentators observe that this restriction could inhibit the plaintiffs' ability to certify a class and potentially to prove a disparate impact at all.186

It is also unclear whether plaintiffs could successfully challenge an element of algorithmic decisionmaking when the final output does not create a disparate impact. On the one hand, under Title VII, the Supreme Court has held that plaintiffs may challenge one part of a decisionmaking process even when the process as a whole does not cause a disparate impact. In Connecticut v. Teal, the Court allowed plaintiffs to challenge their employer's use of a written examination in its promotion process.187 Although the examination disproportionately screened out Black employees, the employer nevertheless ultimately promoted Black employees at a higher rate than White ones considering other criteria.188 A court might read Connecticut v. Teal to allow plaintiffs to challenge elements of an algorithmic system even if the system overall does not cause a disparate impact. A broad reading of Teal might even permit plaintiffs to challenge a single input among thousands.

On the other hand, Teal did not address the viability of a disparate impact challenge to, for instance, a single question on the exam at issue, and the Supreme Court has never held that plaintiffs could challenge such subcomponents of a decisionmaking process. In a different context, considering an FHA challenge to a federal grant program, the D.C. Circuit observed that focusing on each element of a grant formula, instead of the formula as a whole, would make it nearly impossible for an agency to use any formula "without some element having a disproportionate impact on a protected group."189 As a practical matter, potential plaintiffs may be unlikely to know enough about an algorithmic system to bring such tailored challenges,190 but courts may have to decide which parts of an algorithmic process, if any, are separable enough for plaintiffs to contest.

What Is a Legally Adequate Justification?

After a plaintiff proves a specific practice causing a disparate impact, the defendant must show that the practice serves a legitimate, nondiscriminatory purpose. As explained above, there is general uncertainty regarding when a defendant's justification is legally adequate.191 When defendants use AI systems to make decisions, it raises the question of whether they need to show more than that the system is predictive of some legitimate consideration, such as a renter's tenure or a worker's productivity. In other words, do defendants need to show a logical connection between the inputs the system considers and the defendant's valid goal, or only that the system is predictively accurate, even if no one knows why?192 Commentators have pointed out that if a defendant needs to show only that an AI system has some predictive value for the defendant's target variable, most defendants will pass the test.193 Many AI models are designed to predict outcomes based on historical data and inputs. As long as the target variable appears legitimate, one legal scholar has opined with respect to Title VII that "to ask whether the model is 'job related' in the sense of 'statistically correlated' is tautological."194

Models trained on biased, incomplete, or erroneous data may prove less predictive of reality, and an AI model could be so badly designed or trained that a court would not consider its use to be related to a legitimate purpose.195 Courts may need to decide how far to require defendants to go to make sure a model is predictive in the environment in which it is used, and just how predictive it needs to be. As a practical matter, however, putative plaintiffs might have difficulty rebutting a defendant's argument that a model is predictive due to lack of access to the model and its training data, difficulty reproducing the model's decision rules, or expense in designing and executing a study, among other reasons.

Rather than focus on whether a model is truly predictive of the user's target variable, courts might instead focus on whether defendants can articulate some logical connection between an algorithm's decision rules and the defendant's legitimate end.196 For example, if a financial model evaluating creditworthiness downranks people who attend historically Black colleges and universities, or institutions with a religious affiliation, a court could require the lender to prove, or at least posit, a connection between those variables and creditworthiness divorced from borrowers' race or religion.197 This approach would require the users of an AI model to know how it arrived at its output, which may be impossible.198 It may also require some limits on which factors a defendant needs to explain, given the impracticality of addressing thousands, millions, or even billions of parameters governing a model's output.199

Courts may also consider how much a defendant's interests in cost savings or efficiency can count in and of themselves as legitimate justifications for using an algorithm, even when a model's predictions are flawed. Cost savings may not be "job related," but they could be "business necessities," underscoring the lack of clarity in case law as to whether these are the same standard or different standards that must be met separately.200 Accepting time and money savings as legitimate justifications could lighten defendants' burden.

How Could Plaintiffs Identify a Less Discriminatory Alternative?

Plaintiffs may struggle to suggest equally effective, less discriminatory alternatives to allegedly discriminatory algorithms "given that AIs are indeed uniquely effective at optimizing their programmed objective."201 Some scholars have suggested that plaintiffs may be able to identify less discriminatory algorithms, for example, by using "techniques" to "'repair' data sets" to "cleanse . . . data of its proxies for protected classes."202 Others are skeptical that disparate impacts can be eliminated this way.203 In any case, plaintiffs may lack the technical expertise, access to the algorithm, appropriate training data, or other necessary resources to suggest these changes or to develop independent alternatives.204

Plaintiffs may especially struggle to prove that any alternative model was available to the defendant.205 As reviewed above, to be available, courts have held, an alternative must be concrete rather than theoretical, and it must be an option the defendant should have known about.206 Defendants in lawsuits involving algorithmic discrimination may not have developed the challenged software themselves and may not have had the power to affect a model's design.207 Even if plaintiffs prove that theoretically, someone could have developed an equally valid but less discriminatory algorithm, if that program was not commercially available, there is no obvious legal avenue for claiming that an end user should have known about it or created it themselves.

It may also be difficult to obtain the necessary proof that an alternative would work just as well. Some courts have held plaintiffs to a high evidentiary bar to make this showing. In Allen v. City of Chicago, for example, the Seventh Circuit acknowledged that the city had previously successfully used the plaintiffs' proposed promotions process without generating a disparate impact but concluded nevertheless that prior successful use "merely predicts, but does not establish" future success.208 In an area of emerging technology, such a standard of proof may circumscribe the kinds of solutions available to plaintiffs. Plaintiffs seeking to force defendants to deploy a differently designed AI model, for example, may not have even past results to rely on.

If plaintiffs cannot rely on less discriminatory algorithms, they would need to come up with other solutions that serve defendants' needs. This, too, raises the question of whether or how a defendant in a disparate impact suit may rely on cost control and efficiency—that is, in the context of the less discriminatory alternative inquiry, whether and at what threshold a defendant could argue that an alternative is less effective because of its higher cost or other burdens.209

Considerations for Congress

Thus far, courts have considered few cases alleging algorithmic discrimination. How federal antidiscrimination law may apply to such claims is therefore largely undeveloped. In this fast-changing area of technological development and litigation, Congress could wait to see what fact patterns emerge and how courts handle them before considering legislative interventions.

If Congress did seek to legislate on how federal antidiscrimination law applies to algorithmic decisionmaking, it would not be writing on a blank slate. Congress could look to various approaches proposed by states and federal agencies. Some states and localities have passed laws or issued regulations to prevent and/or remedy algorithmic "bias,"210 some of which are described further below. Federal agencies over the past two decades have also issued regulations and guidance on algorithmic discrimination. Some of these rules persist, while others have been significantly revised or rescinded.211 Several federal agency documents discuss mitigating the risk of algorithmic discrimination in documents addressing AI regulation more generally. Such documents include the Government Accountability Office's (GAO's) "accountability framework" for the federal development and deployment of AI;212 the National Institute of Standards and Technology's (NIST's) Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework;213 the Office of Management and Budget's (OMB's) memorandum on "Accelerating Federal Use of AI through Innovation, Governance, and Public Trust,"214 and the National Telecommunications and Information Administration's Artificial Intelligence Accountability Policy Report,215 to name a few.216 Congress could delve more deeply into the approaches attempted by other regulators as potential models for federal legislation.

The following is a discussion of some of the questions and options Congress might consider were it to seek to address potential algorithmic discrimination through legislation.217

Regulating Algorithmic Decisionmaking Under General Antidiscrimination Laws or Using Standalone Antidiscrimination Rules

Congress may consider whether to regulate algorithmic decisionmaking under general antidiscrimination authorities or whether to pass new antidiscrimination laws specifically targeting algorithms or AI.218 Congress could amend existing antidiscrimination laws to clarify areas that may generate confusion in the context of algorithmic decisionmaking. Congress could, for example, clarify the intent standard for disparate treatment; the standard to show an adequate interest justifying a policy or practice that causes disparate impacts; or the standards for less discriminatory alternatives. Such changes could, depending on how they were framed, impact both algorithmic discrimination claims and other kinds of claims.219

Alternatively, rather than relying on general antidiscrimination principles to reach algorithmic decisionmaking, Congress could decide to specify what uses of algorithms are or are not discriminatory. Some of the ways algorithms may be alleged to be "unfair" are difficult to classify as disparate impact or disparate treatment, or as discrimination under current law at all, leaving unclear what standards apply. For example, it is not clear if using an algorithm trained on unrepresentative data violates antidiscrimination law, and assuming it does, whether the relevant antidiscrimination framework would be disparate treatment or disparate impact. Congress could legislate specific rules governing when certain uses of algorithmic models constitute discrimination. In so doing, it could try to adhere to current legal frameworks or write new ones specific to this context.

Adopting Broad Versus Tailored Requirements

Congress could seek to apply antidiscrimination standards to algorithmic decisionmaking across a variety of domains or choose to focus on particular contexts. Some state laws apply the same or similar standards across many contexts in which algorithms may be used. Colorado, for example, passed a bill with transparency requirements for when "automated decision-making technology" is used to "materially influence" a "consequential decision," including access to education, employment, housing, financial services, insurance, health care, or "essential government services and public benefits."220 Other laws are more narrowly targeted. Montana and New Hampshire, for example, have passed laws regulating the states' own use of potentially discriminatory AI.221

In addition to determining whether to target algorithmic decisionmaking broadly or in specific areas, Congress may consider how narrow or broad a range of technology it wishes to regulate. Some laws regulate a wide range of automated decisionmaking tools,222 while others apply only to AI223 or to a particular subset of automated tools, like facial recognition technology.224

Broad and tailored approaches to regulation each involve trade-offs. For example, broad regulations can tackle many instantiations of similar problems and apply consistent standards across domains. They also risk treating conduct, entities, or problems that are different similarly, potentially creating confusion or resulting in under- or over-regulation. Tailored regulations can take into account the specificities of different sectors or technologies and adjust accordingly, but they also risk leaving manifestations of a problem unaddressed.

Deciding Who Should Be Regulated

Congress may wish to consider whether federal antidiscrimination laws target the right defendants when assigning liability for discriminatory algorithms. Antidiscrimination laws often, but not always, cover specific actors. The federal employment discrimination laws, for example, forbid discrimination by an "employer," "employment agency," "labor organization," or "joint labor-management committee."225 ECOA forbids discrimination by a "creditor."226 Several provisions of the FHA, in contrast, provide only that "it shall be unlawful" to engage in certain discriminatory conduct, without specifying a particular kind of person or entity to whom the law applies.227 The parties responsible for developing allegedly discriminatory algorithms may not be the same parties who deploy them, and the law governing a claim may or may not cover an algorithm's developer.228 Courts have held that some laws can render software developers liable for discriminatory algorithms, depending on the facts.229

Given this, Congress could determine that current antidiscrimination law does not cover all of the actors potentially responsible for discriminatory algorithms. Conversely, it could determine that the law sweeps in parties too remote from discriminatory decisionmaking. Congress could amend antidiscrimination laws to specify which entities are to be held liable for algorithmic discrimination and under what facts.

Considering Requirements Beyond Nondiscrimination

Beyond establishing new antidiscrimination requirements in federal law, or amending existing ones, Congress could consider other frameworks for addressing algorithmic decisionmaking exercising different levels of oversight, such as transparency, audit, or design requirements. Other requirements could be added to the current requirements of federal antidiscrimination law or substituted for them. Congress might determine, for example, that requiring litigating parties to explain how AI systems work is prohibitively difficult. It could allow AI developers or users to defend against discrimination claims by showing that their systems meet federal requirements for transparency, testing, design, or other features. Similarly, it could allow plaintiffs to prove claims by showing that an AI system did not comply with such requirements. Congress could also impose requirements on AI systems without providing for a private right of action to enforce them, leaving enforcement to federal agencies or the market.

Transparency

In some cases, plaintiffs have struggled to challenge algorithmic decisionmaking because they did not know whether algorithmic tools were being used or how they worked.230 Regulators and commentators commonly suggest that algorithmic decisionmaking should be transparent.231 Transparency can mean a number of things, from reporting the use of an algorithm, to sharing information about how the algorithm was trained, to disclosing how it functions, to allowing individuals to correct the data on which an algorithm relies.232 Transparency requirements can direct reporting to regulators, or to the public, or both. As an example of a transparency-focus law, Illinois requires employers to disclose to job applicants when they are using AI to analyze video interviews and to "explain[] how the artificial intelligence works and what general types of characteristics it uses to evaluate applicants."233 Colorado law requires a "deployer" of certain automated decisionmaking systems to give consumers the opportunity to correct "factually inaccurate or materially inaccurate personal data" used by such systems in some circumstances.234

Congress has already imposed transparency requirements to combat discrimination in some circumstances. Under ECOA, creditors must provide written statements telling applicants the "specific reasons" for an "adverse action."235 As several circuit courts have explained, the notice requirement both "discourages discrimination" and "educates consumers" as to how they can improve their creditworthiness.236 In 2022 and 2023, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) issued ECOA guidance clarifying that creditors could not "use complex algorithms when doing so means they cannot provide the specific and accurate reasons for adverse actions."237 The CFPB explained that creditors could not simply explain that an algorithm dictated the decision.238 This guidance was withdrawn in 2025.239

Congress could take several actions around transparency in algorithmic decisionmaking. It could, for example, amend antidiscrimination laws to include transparency requirements; pass stand-alone transparency laws about algorithmic decisionmaking; or direct agencies to issue guidance interpreting existing transparency requirements in the context of algorithms. Congress could also legislate to require less transparency around algorithmic systems, if it were concerned that transparency requirements stifle innovation or expose trade secrets, for example.240 Congress could amend ECOA to allow creditors to report the reasons for adverse actions at a higher level of generality.

Any approach Congress takes could face legal challenges and constitutional scrutiny. Transparency requirements in state laws have been challenged on various constitutional (and other) grounds, including the Takings Clause241 and First Amendment.242 One AI developer, for example, argued that California's Artificial Intelligence Training Data Transparency law constituted a taking because, the developer argued, the law required disclosure of protected trade secrets.243 The developer also argued that the law's compelled disclosures violated the First Amendment.244 The district court accepted that "hypothetically, datasets and details about them could be trade secrets," but it denied a preliminary injunction, reasoning that the developer had not pled the existence of trade secrets with enough specificity.245 On the First Amendment claim, the district court held that the law regulated commercial speech, subjecting it to a lower level of scrutiny than most content-based regulations of noncommercial speech receive, and denied the request for a preliminary injunction.246 The case is in a preliminary posture, and the developer has appealed.247

Audits

Some commentators have expressed skepticism that greater transparency, at least alone, would combat algorithmic "bias."248 Some regulators and commentators have gone further and required or suggested that developers and/or users of algorithmic systems conduct some form of audit or testing to discern whether their programs unfairly or illegally impact certain groups.249 Commentators have observed that one of the advantages of algorithmic tools is that, unlike some human decisionmaking, the rules can (sometimes) be knowable, tested, and changed before being implemented.250 As one example of a regulation requiring auditing, New York City has implemented a law regulating "automated employment decision tools," which prohibits employers from using certain algorithmic tools unless the tool has undergone a "bias audit" within the past year.251 Laws can also incentivize bias testing or auditing without requiring it. California regulations, for example, state that "evidence, or the lack of evidence, of anti-bias testing or similar proactive efforts to avoid unlawful discrimination" is "[r]elevant to any . . . claim or available defense" in employment discrimination matters.252

Auditing requirements could take many forms. In addition to determining what an audit would consist of (or directing agencies to do so), Congress could consider which parties would be responsible for conducting an audit, how often audits would occur, and the consequences following audit results, among other things. Bills have been introduced that would require some kind of evaluation or audit of certain algorithms for bias or discriminatory potential, including the Algorithmic Accountability Act of 2025253 and the Artificial Intelligence Civil Rights Act of 2025.254 The latter also includes disclosure requirements.255

As with transparency requirements, state-level audit requirements have been challenged on constitutional grounds. In April 2026, DOJ sought to intervene in a lawsuit by an AI developer challenging Colorado's then-existing statute requiring developers of certain AI systems to, among other things, "use reasonable care to protect consumers from any known or reasonably foreseeable risks of algorithmic discrimination arising from" their products.256 The law also required "deployer[s]" of certain AI systems to adopt a "risk management policy and program" and engage in "impact of assessment[s]" to analyze and mitigate the risk of algorithmic discrimination.257 DOJ argued that these requirements violated the Equal Protection Clause, contending that they forced developers and AI users to engage in disparate treatment by adjusting their systems to avoid disparate outcomes on the basis of protected characteristics.258 DOJ described this process as "racial (or other protected-class-based) engineering."259 The parties agreed to a stay,260 and Colorado subsequently repealed and replaced the challenged law, removing the "reasonable care," "risk management," and "impact assessment" elements.261 Further discussion of potential constitutional issues that may arise when legislating to avoid disparate impacts is below.262

Design Requirements

Congress might consider laws that would directly control the development of algorithmic systems. Congress could enact policies regulating AI training data or the criteria on which AI models can base outputs, for example. Under Maryland law, for example, software tools used for health insurance utilization review must rely on an individual's "clinical" data and may not rely "solely on a group dataset."263 Congress could also require developers to try to minimize discrimination risk or maximize transparency in algorithmic design.264 The Department of Health and Human Services, for example, requires federally funded health programs to "make reasonable efforts to mitigate the risk of discrimination" from any "patient care decision support tools" that "employ input variables or factors that measure race, color, national origin, sex, age, or disability."265 Some sources assert that there are technical ways to ensure algorithms do not discriminate on the bases of protected characteristics or their proxies;266 or to select among available algorithms those that are substantially equally effective at achieving a legitimate goal but produce less discriminatory outputs.267 Congress could consider requiring such methods to be explored when algorithmic decisionmaking tools are developed or implemented.

Role of Human Decisionmakers

Congress could consider if, when, and how humans are to participate in certain decisions. A bipartisan task force in the House of Representatives recommended that "[h]av[ing] humans in the loop to actively identify and remedy potential flaws when AI is used in highly consequential decision-making" is "necessary to safeguard the constitutional rights and civil liberties of Americans affected by AI systems, whether those systems are used by government or private entities."268 Keeping humans involved can mean anything from forbidding the use of automated tools in certain domains to providing for human review when decisions are challenged. At the federal level, for example, one bill introduced in 2026 would prohibit the federal government and state and local governments receiving certain federal funds from using facial recognition technology or other "biometric surveillance systems" except under specific circumstances.269 A bill introduced in 2025 would require health care entities to adopt policies ensuring that algorithmic "outputs are not substituted for the independent judgment of a health care professional."270 The GAO observes that despite the widespread use of automated valuation tools in the mortgage market, lenders still engage in "traditional appraisals" because they are required to do so by certain federal loan programs.271 At the state level, for example, Montana and New Hampshire passed state laws requiring human review of certain outputs from state government AI systems.272 Colorado law currently requires that a "consumer" be given "an opportunity for meaningful human review" of certain adverse decisions when "commercially reasonable," if those decisions were "materially influence[d]" by automated tools.273 Congress could legislate to clarify when entities may and may not allow for fully autonomous decisionmaking by algorithmic systems and what, if any, human participation is required.

Examining Disparate Impact

The viability of disparate impact doctrine generally is in dispute.274 Federal agencies have historically assumed that algorithmic decisionmaking could result in disparate treatment or (where statutes allow it), disparate impact violations.275 In 2025, President Trump issued an executive order that described disparate impact doctrine as "wholly inconsistent with the Constitution," stating that it "imperils the effectiveness of civil rights laws by mandating, rather than proscribing, discrimination."276 The order declared that "[i]t is the policy of the United States to eliminate the use of disparate-impact liability in all contexts to the maximum degree possible" and instructed agencies to "deprioritize" enforcement of disparate impact liability and take steps to "amend[] or repeal, as appropriate under applicable law," agency rules and guidance imposing disparate impact liability.277 This order, and agency actions implementing it,278 may constrain federal agencies in how they approach the discriminatory potential of algorithmic decisionmaking.

If Congress intends for agencies to pursue disparate impact cases, it could strengthen their statutory mandates to do so. It may, for example, add disparate impact liability to statutes that lack it or require agencies to devote resources to disparate impact enforcement. Alternatively, if Congress endorses the Trump Administration's position on disparate impact, it could repeal disparate impact authorities or specify that disparate impact causes of action are not available.279 Congress could also alter or clarify the standards by which claimants prove disparate impact claims, generally or in cases involving algorithmic decisionmaking. Congress may take different approaches to different statutes or sectors.

The Supreme Court held in Ricci that some attempts to eliminate disparate impacts result in disparate treatment.280 Additionally, some Justices and other federal judges have concluded that majority-group plaintiffs also must be able to bring disparate impact claims if the doctrine is to survive constitutional scrutiny.281 These perspectives could significantly limit how far Congress can go to require algorithms to avoid disparate impacts, at least when regulating discrimination on the basis of constitutionally suspect traits, such as race, national origin, and sex.282 Courts may find, for example, that laws that impose liability without giving defendants enough leeway to proffer nondiscriminatory justifications for their actions cross the line to mandating demographic balancing.283 Similar results could occur with laws that allow plaintiffs to challenge isolated elements of a system, especially given that members of both majority and minority groups can bring disparate impact cases, regardless of the ultimate impact of the system as a whole. The more individual parts of a system can be challenged, the more it exposes a user to liability, and the more it may incentivize users to balance outcomes to avoid the pain of litigation.

Congress does not face these constitutional constraints when addressing discrimination on the basis of traits like disability, age, and family status, which are not constitutionally suspect.284 To the extent disparate impact and disparate treatment frameworks are in tension with regard to discrimination on the basis of such characteristics, Congress has significant leeway to determine how to resolve those tensions.

Balancing the Risks of Discrimination with the Risks of Regulation

Algorithmic decisionmaking is a rapidly changing field. Many commentators, including federal regulators, have emphasized the promise in addition to the risks of automated decisionmaking systems.285 These potential upsides include greater efficiency, cost-effectiveness, and accuracy in decisionmaking.286 The upsides also include potential progress in combating discrimination and creating equal opportunity.287 In 2023, for example, a job-search website agreed in a resolution with EEOC to implement an AI-powered tool to "scrape" its website for job postings that discriminated on the basis of national origin.288 In 2019, the CFPB reported findings from a company using "alternative data and machine learning" for credit decisions showing increased access to credit and lower rates "across all tested race, ethnicity, and sex segments" with "no disparities that require further fair lending analysis."289

If Congress finds that the regulation of AI or algorithmic decisionmaking has inhibited useful innovation, it could try to scale back or eliminate regulations to promote AI use and development, for example, by preempting state laws.290 Congress could also take a nonregulatory approach to incentivizing its preferred developments. Congress could, for example, support further private research into and development of solutions to algorithmic discrimination. It could fund research, incentivize, or compensate parties for auditing algorithms, or use other methods. It could also continue to support the development of technical expertise and standards within federal agencies.291 Examples of prior laws directing federal resources toward the development and deployment of AI are included in other CRS products.292

As commentators have observed, modern algorithmic decisionmaking may be biased, but human decisionmaking and older algorithmic methods can be as well.293 In many instances, the adoption of policies like those described above regarding transparency or auditing would impose more restrictions on algorithmic decisionmaking than currently exist on human decisionmaking.294 If regulators decide to enact barriers to the deployment of algorithmic decisionmaking models to eliminate bias, they may wish to consider whether alternatives to algorithms—like human decisionmaking—are more or less effective at addressing discrimination.
