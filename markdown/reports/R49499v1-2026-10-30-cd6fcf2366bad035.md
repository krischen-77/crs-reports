The United States is a founding member of the IMF and plays a critical leadership role in the
organization. Of the IMF’s 191 members, the United States is the largest financial contributor
and has the largest voting share.

Congress authorized U.S. participation in the IMF and has, at times, enacted legislation directing U.S. representatives to the
IMF to use their “voice and vote” to advocate for specific policies. Congress also authorizes U.S. financial commitments to
the Fund and engages in oversight through reporting requirements and hearings. The U.S. Department of the Treasury
manages day-to-day participation at the IMF.

Key IMF policy issues for Congress include, among others,
    •   the status of presidentially appointed and Senate-confirmed representatives to the IMF and the implications
         for U.S. leadership at the Fund;
    •   the Trump Administration’s IMF reform priorities, including refocusing the organization on its core
        mission;
    •   President Trump’s FY2027 budget request to increase U.S. financial commitments to the IMF’s core
        funding source (quota), with a corresponding decrease in commitments to supplemental IMF funds;
    •   the IMF program in Argentina, which accounts for nearly half of outstanding IMF credit;
    •   proposals to sell IMF gold assets to support low-income countries; and
    •   proposals to sell the Bretton Woods Recreation Center, an IMF-owned country club in Germantown, MD.

Overview of the IMF

The International Monetary Fund (IMF, or the Fund) is an international organization whose three-
pronged mission calls for “furthering international monetary cooperation, encouraging the
expansion of trade and economic growth, and discouraging policies that would harm prosperity.”1
The Fund indicates its role is to promote the stability of the international monetary system—the
global network of rules, institutions, and agreements that govern exchange rates, international
payments, and international capital flows.2

The concept of the IMF was proposed in July 1944 at an international conference in Bretton
Woods, NH. Representatives from 44 nations, including the United States, gathered to discuss the
post-World War II recovery of Europe. As part of the discussions, representatives focused on
creating a set of international institutions to address many of the economic issues, such as
protectionist trade policies and unstable exchange rates, that characterized the international
economy between the two world wars. The conference led to the creation of the IMF, the World
Bank, and the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT), the predecessor to the World
Trade Organization (WTO). The World Bank focuses on economic development, and the GATT
was, and the WTO is, focused on trade between countries.

The IMF was formally established in December 1945 by 29 countries. Its membership has grown
to 191 countries as of 2026.

Purpose and Operations

The IMF has six purposes under Article I of its charter, the Articles of Agreement:
    •   promoting international monetary cooperation through a permanent institution
         that provides the machinery for consultation and collaboration on international
       monetary problems;
    •   facilitating the expansion and balanced growth of international trade, thereby
        contributing to high levels of employment, real income, and the development of
        productive resources of all members;
    •   promoting exchange rate stability, thereby maintaining orderly exchange
       arrangements among members;
    •   assisting the establishment of a multilateral system of payments and eliminating
        foreign exchange restrictions;
    •   giving confidence to members by making the general resources of the Fund
        temporarily available to them under adequate safeguards; and
    •   shortening the duration and lessening the degree of economic imbalances among
       members.3

Although the IMF’s purpose has remained constant since 1945, its operations have evolved with
changes in the global economy. When the IMF was created, it focused on coordinating a new
system of fixed exchange rates to help the international economy recover from two world wars
and the economic instability in the interwar period. In the 1970s, the United States and other

Crisis Lending

IMF loans, extended to governments at market-based interest rates, are intended to stabilize
economies and reduce future instability.4 Since the 2000s, the IMF has extended loans less
frequently, at higher funding levels, and over longer disbursement periods compared with the
1980s and 1990s (Figure 1).

The IMF finances its loans by drawing on resources committed by its members. All members
make a financial commitment, called quota, when they join the IMF. Quotas are essentially lines
of credit that members extend to the IMF. They are tied to the size of a country’s economy; larger
economies usually have larger quotas. IMF quotas total about $644 billion (Figure 2).8 The U.S.
quota share (17.42%, about $112 billion) is the largest of any member.

The IMF also supplements quota resources through voluntary borrowing agreements with some
members. The IMF has a multilateral borrowing agreement (the New Arrangements to Borrow
[NAB]) with 40 countries, totaling about $493 billion, and bilateral borrowing agreements with
42 countries, totaling about $185 billion.9 The United States has committed about $76 billion to
the NAB, the second-largest contribution after Japan.10 The United States has never had a
bilateral borrowing agreement with the IMF.

Surveillance. The IMF monitors the global economy and the economic and financial policies of
its member countries. IMF surveillance strives to identify risks to macroeconomic stability, advise
governments on policy adjustments, and prevent financial crises. The IMF conducts surveillance
of individual countries, regions that share economic policies and currencies (such as the
eurozone), and global financial markets.

Technical assistance. The IMF provides technical assistance and training to help member
countries strengthen their ability to design and implement effective policies. Such assistance
focuses on monetary and financial policies, fiscal policy and management, data compilation, and
economic and financial legislation.

Special drawing rights (SDRs). SDRs are international reserve assets created by the IMF in
1969. SDRs are not physical assets (like gold or silver) or a currency that governments can use to
directly purchase things. Instead, SDRs are designed to be traded for “hard currencies,” such as
the U.S. dollar, euro, yen, and pound sterling. For example, if a government finds itself unable to
obtain from private capital markets the U.S. dollars it needs to make a debt payment or pay for
imports, the government can sell SDRs to another government in exchange for U.S. dollars. The
IMF creates and allocates SDRs to members, maintains the ledger recording SDR transactions,
and matches SDR sellers with buyers. The IMF has created and allocated about $920 billion of
SDRs to members, primarily in proportion with their quota shares, since 1969, including about
$155 billion to the United States.11 The United States periodically purchases and sells SDRs to
other governments seeking liquidity support.12

Trust funds. The IMF serves as the trustee for two smaller trust funds that provide concessional
(low-cost) loans (Figure 3). The first trust fund, the Poverty Reduction and Growth Fund
(PRGT), has roots back to the 1980s. It provides concessional loans to low-income countries
(LICs). The second trust fund is the Resilience and Sustainability Trust (RST), which was created
in 2022. It provides long-term, concessional financing to LICs and vulnerable middle-income
countries undertaking reforms related to climate change and pandemic preparedness. The PRGT
and RST are resourced through the voluntary contributions of members. The United States
periodically contributes to the PRGT, most recently loans totaling $21 billion in 2024.13 The
United States has not contributed to the RST.

Governance

The IMF is a specialized agency of the United Nations, with its own governance, staff, and
budget. Its headquarters is in Washington, DC, and there are IMF offices around the world. It has
a staff of about 3,100 people.14 The IMF operates according to procedures established by
its Articles of Agreement, a charter document that outlines the organization’s purpose,
governance structure, obligations of member countries, and operational rules. The IMF’s highest
decisionmaking authority is the Board of Governors. All member countries are represented on the
Board of Governors, usually by a finance minister or central bank governor.

The Board of Governors has delegated day-to-day authority over operational policy, lending, and
other matters to the Executive Board. The Executive Board has 25 Executive Directors. The
members with the five largest financial commitments to the Fund (currently the United States,
Japan, China, Germany, France, and the United Kingdom) maintain Executive Director positions
on the Executive Board and appoint their own Executive Directors.15 The other Executive
Directors are elected every two years, usually by groups of members.

Issues for Congress

U.S. policymakers and analysts have long debated the merits of the organization, as well as U.S.
participation in the IMF. Proponents have argued that U.S. financial contributions to the IMF are
beneficial as they enable IMF to leverage them to pool funding from multiple donors, support
U.S. exporters and investors, stabilize foreign markets, and provide financial support to allies.17

Others have argued, for example, that IMF emergency loans encourage governments and private
lenders to take excessive financial risks, thereby increasing the frequency of crises; that IMF
programs fall short of their stated goal of resolving borrowers’ economic crises; and that the
United States also has less control over economic assistance provided through the IMF compared

Congress authorized U.S. membership in the IMF through the Bretton Woods Agreements Act
(BWAA) in 1945.20 Both Congress and the executive branch shape U.S. engagement with the
Fund. Congress authorizes and, as needed, appropriates funding for new U.S. financial
commitments to the IMF.21 The commitments are essentially lines of credit and, once established,
do not require annual authorization or annual appropriations. Congress has exercised its “power
of the purse” to advance certain IMF reforms. For example, in 1998 Congress conditioned an
increase of the U.S. quota on greater IMF transparency, and in 2015 on reform of IMF policies
governing large financial assistance packages.22

Congress has also enacted legislation at times that directed U.S. representatives at the IMF use
their “voice and vote” to advance specific priorities—for example, to oppose IMF financing to
countries that have expropriated property from U.S. persons,23 to notify key congressional
committees with respect to any international negotiations to increase U.S. quota,24 and to increase
IMF technical assistance for preventing money laundering and terrorist financing,25 among others.
The United States has considerable informal influence at the IMF and has in some cases used
legislation to advance significant policy changes at the Fund. For example, a “voice and vote”
legislative mandate in 1998 to support independent evaluations of IMF operations preceded the
creation of a permanent Independent Evaluation Office at the IMF in 2001.26

Select Policy Issues and Options for Congress
Members of the 119th Congress might consider a variety of issues related to the IMF, including
the status of U.S. leadership and priorities at the Fund, proposals to increase IMF core funding,
IMF engagement with Argentina, the IMF’s gold holdings, and the status of the Bretton Woods
Recreation Center, located in Germantown, MD.

U.S. Leadership

In 2025 and 2026, President Trump nominated Scott Bessent, the Treasury Secretary, to serve as
the U.S. Governor to the IMF. President Trump also submitted a nomination for the U.S.
Executive Director to the IMF in 2026. These nominations are pending before the Senate. The
President has not submitted nominees for the Alternate Governor or the Alternate Executive
Director. As of October 2026, no position representing the United States on the IMF Board of
Governors or the Executive Board was filled by an individual nominated by the President and
confirmed by the Senate for that position.34 Three of the four positions have not been filled by
such an individual in more than a decade (Figure 5).

As of October 2026, the acting U.S. Governor to the IMF is the U.S. Deputy Secretary of the
Treasury Francis Brooke.35 The Governor position has also been previously filled by lower-level
Treasury officials and civil servants on an acting basis. A Treasury civil servant is also filling the
U.S. Executive Director position on an acting basis, and the Alternate Governor and Alternate
Executive Director positions are vacant.36 The United States is the only G20 country to have a
vacancy on the Board of Governors, and the only country to have a vacancy on the Executive
Board.

Acting officials might not have the same gravitas or influence in international negotiations as
confirmed officials—a limitation that may be particularly relevant on the Board of Governors,
where countries are usually represented by their finance ministers and central bank governors.
Additionally, individuals can fill a position on an acting basis for a limited time, which could
make it harder for the United States to advance its priorities at the institution. At the same time,
the executive branch and the Senate balance numerous policy priorities. Given competing
demands on time and resources, some might assess that having lower-level Treasury officials and
civil servants to represent the United States on an acting basis, particularly in the absence of a
major global economic crisis, is an adequate alternative.

Treasury Secretary as U.S. Governor of the IMF and the Chairman of the Federal Reserve as U.S.
Alternate Governor of the IMF. Congress could consider amending U.S. law to allow these
positions to be filled by the Treasury Secretary and the Chairman of the Federal Reserve on an ex
officio (by virtue of the office) basis, without a separate confirmation process.37 Such an
amendment would allow the positions to be filled quickly by officials that have already been
subject to Senate confirmation and have historically always held the positions. However, such an
amendment would limit the President’s flexibility to consider alternative candidates in the future.
Congress could also request updates on the current Administration’s timeframe for submitting
nominations for these positions and facilitate expeditious consideration of IMF nominations.

Since President Trump assumed office in 2025, the Administration has withdrawn the United
States from over 60 international organizations and ended the role of the U.S. Agency for
International Development (USAID) in administering U.S. foreign assistance. The Trump
Administration has been comparatively more supportive of U.S. participation in the international
financial institutions (IFIs). In April 2025, Treasury Secretary Bessent emphasized that the IMF
and World Bank have “enduring value,” that “America First does not mean America alone,” and
that the United States would be seeking to expand U.S. leadership in the IFIs.38

Gold Reserves

The IMF holds 90.5 million ounces of gold as a reserve asset, equivalent to about $370 billion at
the market price of gold on October 28, 2026.56 The Fund physically stores its gold in designated
depositories around the world. Over the past two years, some experts and civil society groups,
including the Pontifical Academy of Social Sciences, have called on the Fund to sell a portion of
its gold and use the proceeds to support low-income countries (LICs), similar to sales in 1976-
1980, 1999, and 2009.57 Concerns about IMF gold sales include the implications for the IMF’s
financial position and potential disruptions to the global gold market. Under U.S. law,
congressional authorization is required for the United States to approve any disposition of Fund
gold, with some exceptions.58

Options for Congress. If Members are interested in selling IMF gold to support LICs, they could
request the Treasury and experts outside the Administration to assess the implications for the
financial standing of the Fund and for LIC recipients. Members could also consider legislation
that directs the U.S. Executive Director at the IMF to advocate for the development of a strategy
for the Fund’s gold holdings over the next 5, 10, 20, or even 30 years. If Members are concerned

Bretton Woods Recreation Center

The IMF established the Bretton Woods Recreation Center (BWRC)—a country club in
Germantown, MD—in 1968. The IMF owns the property, and the facility is operated by the
Bretton Woods Recreation Center INC, a nonprofit organization. According to reporting by the
New York Post in December 2024, initiation fees are waived for IMF and World Bank staff, who
make up 80% of the membership, and high-ranking IMF staff have served on the BWRC board.59
According to the same news report, an IMF spokesperson emphasized that the BWRC is
operationally and financially independent from the IMF.60 In October 2025, Treasury Secretary
Scott Bessent urged the IMF to sell the facility.61

Options for Congress. If Members of Congress are concerned about the financial implications of
the BWRC, they could consider legislation directing U.S. representatives at the IMF to advocate
for a public financial assessment of the value and cost of the BWRC to the IMF, an assessment of
the benefits of the BWRC to recruiting and maintaining IMF staff, and an evaluation of different
ways in which the proceeds from a potential future sale of the BWRC could be used to support
IMF operations. Members could also consider legislation directing U.S. representatives to
advocate for a sale of the property.
