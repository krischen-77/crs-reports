Summary

Aluminum—a lightweight, ductile, malleable, and corrosion-resistant metal—is used in a variety of consumer, industrial, and technological goods, including aircraft, food packaging, building materials, motor vehicles, and several types of military equipment. The wide array of products and infrastructures containing aluminum make the metal essential to the economy and national security of the United States.

Aluminum production broadly goes through two processes. Primary aluminum production is an energy-intensive process that dissolves alumina—a chemical compound made from refining bauxite—to form aluminum. Secondary aluminum production melts scrap and recycled aluminum to form new products.

Since 2000, primary aluminum production has generally decreased in the United States. A contributing factor to this decline is the relatively high cost of primary aluminum production in the United States compared with countries that have lower energy costs, including Canada, Russia, and the United Arab Emirates.

The United States is a major producer of secondary aluminum. Secondary aluminum production is less energy-intensive than primary aluminum production, and an abundance of domestic scrap provides advantages to U.S. producers. Secondary aluminum often contains more impurities than primary aluminum, as it is derived from recycled aluminum products or scrap. Thus, manufacturers of components used in electronics and aerospace manufacturing often favor primary aluminum that meets specified purity standards. Many defense-related products must comply with purity standards and therefore contain aluminum alloys made with refined primary aluminum.

U.S. aluminum manufacturers function within a global market that affects the prices and availability of their products. The U.S. and Canadian aluminum industries are highly integrated, as each market is a major trading partner of the other across the aluminum value chain. The People's Republic of China holds over half of the global primary aluminum smelting capacity and has become the world's leading producer of aluminum over the past two decades.

Tariff increases, global supply chain disruptions, and federal investments might be changing the global and national competitive landscape in the aluminum industry. Such tariff increases and disruptions are contributing to higher prices, which can make it profitable for aluminum producers to expand production in the United States. Concurrent to these changing market conditions, a federal grant is contributing to the construction of the first primary aluminum smelter in the United States since 1980. These developments could restore domestic capacity to levels not seen in decades.

Simultaneously, the rise in the price of aluminum may adversely affect industries that use it, such as manufacturers of transportation equipment and food. In addition, increases in the cost of producing end products with aluminum content, such as automotive vehicles and beverage cans, may raise the cost of living.

Policies that raise aluminum prices and foster domestic aluminum production may negatively affect consumers and industries that use aluminum. Congress might consider whether and, if so, how it might negotiate this tension. Among other options, Congress could assess public support, such as tax credits or other financial support, for primary aluminum smelters to acquire energy-efficient technologies that could lower the domestic cost of production. Congress also could consider options that increase the consumption of U.S. aluminum scrap by domestic producers. Additionally, Congress may consider support for research and development of innovations that might improve domestic aluminum production or affect the demand from industries that rely on specific aluminum products.

Introduction

Aluminum—a lightweight, ductile, malleable, and corrosion-resistant metal—is used in a wide variety of consumer, industrial, and technological goods, including food packaging, aircraft, building materials, motor vehicles, and many types of military equipment.

In recent years, some observers have raised concerns about the fall in the domestic production of aluminum. In 2025, the U.S. Geological Survey estimated that the United States relied on imported aluminum—mainly from Canada (56% of imports), the United Arab Emirates (UAE; 8%), Bahrain (4%), and the People's Republic of China (China; 3%)—for 60% of its consumption.[1](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn1 "See \"Aluminum\" in U.S. Geological Survey (USGS), Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026 , February 2026, https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026-aluminum.pdf (hereinafter USGS, \"Aluminum,\" February 2026).") In addition, the domestic production of certain aluminum products needed for national security takes place in few locations. This may pose risks to national security if any single site were to experience an unplanned outage.

Some U.S. aluminum producers have claimed that recent changes in U.S. tariff policies contributed to their decisions to invest in expanding domestic production capacity. These tariff policies—alongside disruptions in international trade, such as the drop in ship traffic through the Strait of Hormuz—have contributed to increases in the domestic price of aluminum. High domestic aluminum prices benefit domestic aluminum makers. Observers have noted that the high cost of aluminum also may negatively affect consumers and industries that use aluminum. Members of Congress might consider whether and, if so, how they might assess or negotiate the emerging tension between supply chain resilience and the cost of domestic production borne by consumers and industries that use aluminum.

This report provides an overview of aluminum production in the United States and factors affecting its price. The report then discusses selected issues that may be of interest to Congress and some policy options.

Aluminum Manufacturing

Aluminum production occurs through two types of processes:

* *Primary* *production*yields aluminum by dissolving alumina—a chemical compound made from refining bauxite—using an electric current. Aluminum made from primary production can be further refined to achieve a higher concentration of elemental aluminum than is possible through secondary production.
* S*e**condary* *production* yields aluminum by melting scrap metal. The scrap may be old and recovered from consumer or industrial products, such as cans and auto parts. It may also be new scrap left over from processing aluminum into consumer or industrial products.

Aluminum made from these two processes is called *unwrought alu**minum*. Aluminum mills extrude, roll, and mechanically process unwrought aluminum into desired shapes such as sheets, tubes, and foil[2](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn2 "The Aluminum Association, \"Processing 101,\" accessed July 1, 2026, https://www.aluminum.org/processing-101 ; Roy Woodward, \" The Rolling of Aluminium: T he Process and the Product ,\" lecture prepared for the Training in Alumin i um Application Technologies, 1994; and North American Industry Classification System (NAICS) Association, \" 331318 - Other Aluminum Rolling, Drawing, and Extruding ,\" https://www.naics.com/naics-code-description/?v=2022&code=331318 .") that are then referred to as *wrought aluminum*. Unwrought aluminum may also be melted and cast into shapes. Cast and wrought aluminum are also referred to as *semi**-**fabricated* products.[3](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn3 "The Aluminum Association defined semi-fabrication as follows: \" Aluminum semi-fabrication is a forming process to transform aluminum ingots into a semi-finished shape. Typical aluminum semi-fabrication processes include rolling, extrusion, forging and casting. Product of a semi-fabrication process often requires further manufacturing (fabrication, finishing, and assembly) to turn it into a final product for end use. \" See Marshall Jinlong Wang, \" The Environmental Footprint of Semi - Fa...") Producers of semi-fabricated products in the United States accounted for about 75% of the domestic aluminum industry's $41.1 billion sales in 2024.[4](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn4 "Calculation achieved using da t a from U.S. Census Bureau . See U.S. Census Bureau, \"Annual Integrated Economic Survey,\" accessed October 20, 2026, https://data.census.gov/table/AIESBASIC2024.AIES31BASIC01?q=AIES31BASIC01&code...")

Semi-fabricated products are used in the manufacture of consumer, industrial, and defense products.[5](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn5 "Aluminum Extruders Council, \"Industries,\" accessed July 1, 2026, https://aec.org/industries .") Domestic Primary Aluminum Production

In 2000, the United States ranked as the world's top producer of primary aluminum. Domestic primary aluminum production began decreasing thereafter (see [**Figure 2**](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#_Ref113350933)). In 2025, the United States produced 660,000 metric tons of primary aluminum, below a previous domestic output peak of 4.7 million metric tons in 1980 and the lowest level recorded since 1950.[6](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn6 "USGS, National Minerals Information Center (NMIC) , Aluminum – Historical Statistics (Data Series 140) , February 26, 2024, https://www.usgs.gov/media/files/aluminum-historical-statistics-data-series-140 ; and USGS, \" Aluminum , \" February 2026.") Currently, it is not among the top 10 producers and accounts for less than 1% of global primary aluminum production.[7](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn7 "USGS, \" Aluminum , \" February 2026.")

A contributing factor to the decline in U.S. primary aluminum production is competition from countries that have lower energy costs than the United States. Primary aluminum production is energy-intensive, with electricity estimated to account for up to 40% of the cost.[8](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn8 "The Aluminum Association, \" Primary Production 101 ,\" https://www.aluminum.org/primary-production-101 .") In 2025, U.S.-headquartered aluminum producer Alcoa reported that electric power represented approximately 24% of the company's primary aluminum production costs.[9](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn9 "Alcoa Corporation, Form 10-K for the Y ear E nding December 31, 2025, p. 6, https://www.sec.gov/ix?doc=/Archives/edgar/data/0001675149/000119312526077167/aa-20251231.htm .") U.S.-headquartered Century Aluminum cited electricity as one of the company's largest operating costs and attributed the closure of its primary aluminum facility (or smelter) in Hawesville, KY, directly to an increase in such costs.[10](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn10 "Century Aluminum Company, Form 10-K for the Y ear E nding December 31, 2025, https://www.sec.gov/ix?doc=/Archives/edgar/data/0000949157/000162828026013788/cenx-20251231.htm .") Generally, countries with the largest aluminum sectors include those with lower energy costs than the United States, such as Canada, Russia, and the UAE. According to the World Bank, one kilowatt-hour of electricity cost, on average, $0.181 in the United States in 2019 compared with $0.123 in Canada, $0.084 in Russia, and $0.109 in the UAE.[11](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn11 "The most recent year for which data are available is 2019. World Bank Group , DataBank, \"DataBank, Doing Business : Price of E lectricity ,\" 2019, https://databank.worldbank.org/Electric-Prices-by-Country/id/7b12e700 .") A May 2025 report from The Aluminum Association—an industry trade group representing U.S.- and foreign-based aluminum producers, including Alcoa, and providers of services to the aluminum industry—noted that U.S. aluminum producers pay between $32 and $46 more per megawatt-hour compared with Canadian counterparts.[12](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn12 "The Aluminum Association, \" Powering Up American Aluminum: A Roadmap for Next Generation Supply Chain Resilience , \" May 2025, https://www.aluminum.org/sites/default/files/2025-05/PoweringUpAluminum_WhitePaper_2025.pdf .")

As domestic primary aluminum production has decreased, so has the number of active smelters in the United States. In 2000, 12 companies operated 23 primary smelting facilities in the United States.[13](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn13 "\" Aluminum\" in USGS, Mineral Commodity Summaries 2001 , January 2001, https://d9-wret.s3.us-west-2.amazonaws.com/assets/palladium/production/mineral-pubs/aluminum/050301.pdf .") As of October 2026, two companies—Alcoa and Century Aluminum—each operate two primary smelters that actively produce aluminum (see [**Figure 3**](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#_Ref236307400)).[14](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn14 "USGS, \" Aluminum , \" February 2026.") The total production capacity across these four smelters is about 795,000 metric tons per year. [**Figure 3**](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#_Ref236307400) also shows three facilities that were shut down in recent years—one by each of Alcoa, Century Aluminum, and Magnitude 7 Metals—and their most recent status.

Domestic Secondary Aluminum Production

Since the 2000s, most unwrought aluminum production in the United States has been secondary aluminum (see [**Figure 2**](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#_Ref113350933)). Unlike primary production, secondary aluminum production in the United States has increased overall since the early 1980s; in 2025, it was over twice the level of production in 1985.[31](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn31 "USGS, NMIC , \" Aluminum - Historical Statistics (Data Series 140) ,\" February 26, 2024, https://www.usgs.gov/media/files/aluminum-historical-statistics-data-series-140 .") However, from 2000 to 2025, production fluctuated and was about the same level in 2000 and 2025.

Secondary aluminum producers face a different cost structure from primary producers. Notably, secondary production is roughly 95% less energy-intensive than primary production,[32](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn32 "The Aluminum Association, \" Secondary Production 101 ,\" https://www.aluminum.org/secondary-production-101 .") and the capital cost of secondary production is comparatively low.

Secondary aluminum accounted for 85% of the 4.3 million metric tons of unwrought aluminum produced by U.S. smelters in 2025. Of that secondary production, 56% came from new scrap, which is generally excess material generated by aluminum product manufacturers, and 44% came from old scrap, which is generally post-consumer or post-industrial scrap.[33](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn33 "USGS, \" Aluminum , \" February 2026.")

According to The Aluminum Association, "a recycled aluminum beverage can, car door, or window frame is often recycled directly back into itself."[34](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn34 "The Aluminum Association, \"Infinitely Recyclable,\" https://www.aluminum.org/Recycling .") The Aluminum Association highlighted the value of certain types of post-consumer scrap materials—such as used beverage containers—for secondary aluminum production due to their ease of collection and readiness for smelting.[35](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn35 "The Aluminum Association, \"Scrap the Exports, Save U.S. Supply: Treating Aluminum Scrap as a Strategic Asset ,\" October 14, 2025, https://www.aluminum.org/sites/default/files/2025-10/Scrap-Export-Policy-White-Paper_Oct2025.pdf (hereinafter The Aluminum Association, \"Scrap the Exports, Save U.S. Supply \") .") The trade group noted that increased recycling of these scrap materials could grow domestic secondary aluminum production and related employment.[36](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn36 "The Aluminum Association, \"Scrap the Exports, Save U.S. Supply . \"")

Post-consumer aluminum scrap sometimes requires separation and processing to become usable by smelters. Some industry observers have noted that emerging technologies may improve aluminum recovery and lower costs for smelters looking to utilize certain types of post-consumer scrap.[37](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn37 "An e xample of such technologies i s Laser-Induced Breakdown Spectroscopy . See SAFE, \" Turning Scrap into Strength: The Strategic Importance of Aluminum Recycling ,\" February 19, 2026, https://secureenergy.org/turningscrap-intostrength-aluminumrecycling/  .") Generally, the mixing of different aluminum scrap products, as well as the presence of other materials in aluminum scrap, can contribute to secondary aluminum sometimes having lower purity levels than primary aluminum. Secondary aluminum sometimes goes through additional processing to achieve higher purity levels. One method for secondary aluminum to reach higher purity is by mixing it with primary aluminum.

Some industrial and consumer product manufacturers have performance requirements that limit the use of secondary aluminum. For example, certain aerospace components require aluminum alloys with a specific chemical composition that can be more predictably refined from primary aluminum than from secondary aluminum.

Applications of Aluminum

Many parts of the U.S. economy rely on aluminum. Consumer products that utilize aluminum include canned beverages, automotive vehicles, and home appliances. Industry also relies on products containing aluminum, from electrical transformers to machinery. Manufacturers of hardware with military applications, such as aircraft and munitions, also use aluminum. The section below addresses selected trends among users of aluminum, including current demand and emerging bottlenecks.

Consumption of aluminum fluctuates ([**Figure 4**](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#_Ref244179898)) and partly depends on the state of the manufacturing sector. The three largest domestic markets for aluminum in 2025 were transportation equipment, packaging, and building and construction. Aluminum Market

U.S. aluminum producers compete in a global aluminum market that is affected by factors such as global supply and U.S. trade policies. The interaction of these factors determines the availability and price of aluminum. This section discusses recent developments that have affected these factors.

Global Capacity

The global aluminum sector saw major changes over the last two decades as China became the leading producer of aluminum. China's output of primary aluminum grew by approximately 1,631%, from 2.6 million metric tons in 2000 to 45 million metric tons in 2025, accounting for 60.6% of global primary aluminum output (see [**Figure 5**](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#_Ref244180170)).

U.S. Aluminum Trade and Tariff Policy

U.S., Canadian, and Mexican aluminum markets are interconnected. More than half of U.S. aluminum imports in 2025 came from Canada.[60](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn60 "USGS, \" Aluminum , \" February 2026.") After Canada, the leading foreign suppliers of aluminum to the U.S. market included the UAE, Mexico, and South Korea (among others not listed).[61](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn61 "\"Aluminum in December 2025 \" in USGS, Mineral Industry Surveys , April 2026, https://d9-wret.s3.us-west-2.amazonaws.com/assets/palladium/production/s3fs-public/media/files/mis-202512-alumi.pdf .") Mexico was the leading destination for U.S. export of aluminum in 2025, accounting for 17% of total aluminum exports. After Mexico, leading foreign buyers of U.S. aluminum included Thailand, India, and Canada (among others not listed). Scrap composed 72% of U.S. aluminum exports.

Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 (TEA)[62](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn62 "Codified as amended at 19 U.S.C. §1862 .") has been used to investigate and take action to adjust aluminum imports. Section 232 allows restrictions to be placed on imports that have been found to "threaten to impair" national security.[63](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn63 "A statutory definition of national security is \" the national defense and foreign relations of the United States. \" 10 U.S.C. §801(16) .") The law states that when using this authority, the Secretary of Commerce and the President shall "recognize the close relation of the economic welfare of the Nation to our national security" and consider "the impact of foreign competition on the economic welfare of individual domestic industries."[64](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn64 "19 U.S.C. §1862 (d).") In March 2018, the first Trump Administration used this authority to impose a 10% tariff on aluminum imports (but temporarily excluded aluminum imports from some countries, including Canada, Australia, and Brazil).[65](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn65 "For more information on Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, see CRS In Focus IF13006, Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 , by Kyla H. Kitamura ; Executive Office of the President, \"Adjusting Imports of Aluminum i nto the United States,\" 83 Federal Register 11619, March 8, 2018 ; and Executive Office of the President, \"Adjusting Imports of Aluminum i nto the United States,\" 83 Federal Register 13355, March 22, 2018.") Generally, the economic welfare, economic security,[66](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn66 "A statutory definition of economic security is \"t he condition of having secure and resilient domestic production capacity, combined with reliable access to the global resources necessary to maintain an acceptable standard of living and to protect core national values. \" 6 U.S.C. §474(c)(2).") and national security of the United States were themes in DOC's 2018 investigation that supported these Section 232 tariffs.[67](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn67 "DOC , The Effect of Imports of Aluminum on the National Security , January 17, 2018.") This DOC investigation would go on to be cited in subsequent Section 232 actions.

In 2025, President Trump generally increased the Section 232 aluminum tariff rates and their coverage. Pursuant to a proclamation issued by President Trump in February 2025,[68](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn68 "DOC, BIS , \"Implementation of Duties on Aluminum Pursuant to Proclamation 10895 Adjusting Imports of Aluminum i nto the United States,\" 90 Federal Register 11251, March 5, 2025.") imports of aluminum from "all countries" became subject to a Section 232 tariff, effective March 12, 2025.[69](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn69 "For discussion of elimination of certain trading partner exemptions and alternative tariff rate quotas , see CRS Insight IN12519, Section 232 Tariffs on Steel and Aluminum , by Kyla H. Kitamura .") Additionally, President Trump increased the general Section 232 tariff rate on imports of aluminum to 25%,[70](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn70 "Primary a luminum imports that were produced in Russia continue to be subject to a 200% Section 232 tariff.") effective March 12, 2025, and then further increased the general Section 232 tariff rate on imports of aluminum to 50% for nearly all countries,[71](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn71 "Primary a luminum imports that were produced in Russia continue to be subject to a 200% Section 232 tariff, and aluminum imports from the United Kingdom continue to be subject to a 25% Section 232 tariff. Executive Office of the President, \"Adjusting Imports of Aluminum and Steel i nto the United States,\" 90 Federal Register 24199, June 9, 2025.") effective June 4, 2025.[72](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn72 "For discussion of timeline and coverage , see CRS Insight IN12519, Section 232 Tariffs on Steel and Aluminum , by Kyla H. Kitamura .") Subsequently, President Trump made modifications to tariff rates on certain products and on imports from certain trading partners.[73](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn73 "For additional discussion of Section 232 modifications and their timeline, see CRS Insight IN12519, Section 232 Tariffs on Steel and Aluminum , by Kyla H. Kitamura ; and B IS , \" Section 232 Steel and Aluminum , \" accessed June 11, 2026, https://www.bis.gov/about-bis/bis-leadership-and-offices/SIES/section-232-investigations/section-232-steel-aluminum .")

Research indicates a range of potential effects from Section 232 aluminum tariffs. Some research analyzing Section 232 tariffs implemented by the first Trump Administration on steel and aluminum found that the immediate effect was an increase of tariff-inclusive U.S. import prices of those metals.[74](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn74 "Mary Amiti et al., \"The Impact of the 2018 Tariffs on Prices and Welfare,\" Journal of Economic Perspectives , vol. 33, no. 4 (Fall 2019), https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/pdfplus/10.1257/jep.33.4.187 .") By increasing the price of aluminum, the tariffs may have also resulted in domestic manufacturers that use aluminum facing higher input costs. Price Determination

Aluminum is a globally traded commodity, so its price is affected by global demand and supply. The balance between global demand and supply is reflected in the price of futures contracts traded on the London Metal Exchange (LME).[86](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn86 "A futures contract is a legally binding agreement to buy or sell something at a specific price on a future date.") The U.S. benchmark aluminum price, the *Midwest* *price*, establishes the base price of aluminum in the United States. This price is usually higher than the LME price, as it reflects the transportation cost of delivery to LME-approved warehouses in the United States, tariffs and other duties,[87](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn87 "A tariff is a type of duty.") supply chain conditions, and U.S. demand conditions. The difference between the LME and the Midwest price is called the *Midwest Premium*. For example, in October 2024, the LME price of primary aluminum was 117.840 cents per pound, and the Midwest price of aluminum was 136.848 cents per pound. The Midwest Premium was therefore around 19 cents per pound, or around 16% of the LME price.

For much of the 21st century, the Midwest Premium was relatively stable, and U.S. aluminum price variation was largely explained by global demand and supply changes reflected in the global LME price. Disruptions and frictions in global trade in the 2020s have somewhat weakened the link between global and U.S. aluminum prices. Price trends have diverged between U.S. and global markets as tariff increases and supply chain frictions—such as logistical bottlenecks during the COVID-19 pandemic—have erected barriers between global and U.S. markets for aluminum. This divergence between U.S. and global aluminum price trends is reflected in a higher Midwest Premium as a percent of LME price, which is shown alongside the estimated average effective duty percentage for aluminum in [**Figure 6**](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#_Ref232100055). The data show a widening gap between U.S. and global aluminum prices (represented by the growth in aluminum Midwest Premium as a percent of LME) in 2025 that corresponds with increases in estimated average effective duties of aluminum imported to the United States. In December 2025, for example, the Midwest Premium was nearly 70% of the LME price.

Because tariffs add a cost on top of the market import price, they tend to increase the gap between the average tariff-inclusive U.S. price and the average global LME price (i.e., increase the Midwest Premium).

In 2026, supply chain disruptions contributed to increases in the LME price and potentially affected the Midwest Premium. Generally, disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz have affected the global supply of aluminum[89](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn89 "For discussion of the logistical effects of blockages in the Strait of Hormuz, see CRS Report R48903, The Strait of Hormuz in Brief: Non-Oil Shipments and Effects on U.S. Shippers , by John Frittelli and Ben Goldman .") and contributed to increases in the LME price in 2026. On May 29, 2026, *Bloomberg* reported that global aluminum inventories tracked by major metal exchanges had fallen to "less than five" days' worth of supply and that "supply is running short of demand."[90](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn90 "Mark Burton, \"Aluminum Squeeze Deepens as Spreads Tighten and Inventories Drop,\" Bloomberg , May 29, 2026, https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-05-29/aluminum-squeeze-deepens-as-spreads-tighten-and-inventories-drop .") Additionally, logistical frictions in the Strait of Hormuz could have increased the Midwest Premium in part because such frictions are reportedly increasing freight rates.[91](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn91 "Greg Miller, \"Hormuz Crisis Side Effect: A Sharp Rise in Container Shipping Rates,\" Lloyd's List Intelligence , May 29, 2026, https://www.lloydslist.com/LL1157327/Hormuz-crisis-side-effect-a-sharp-rise-in-container-shipping-rates .") Higher freight rates generally mean the cost to deliver aluminum to U.S. markets will be higher; if such delivery costs increase more than global averages, they could increase the Midwest Premium.

Selected Considerations for Congress

Ongoing global supply chain disruptions, tariff increases, and certain industrial policies of the United States have seemingly incentivized the expansion of domestic primary aluminum production capacity. This market condition might support supply chain resilience and reduce the likelihood of aluminum shortages in the United States. It could also contribute to a general increase in costs for aluminum-consuming manufacturers and consumers because the cost of producing aluminum—particularly, primary aluminum—in the United States is generally higher than in other aluminum-producing countries. Congress might consider whether and, if so, how it might negotiate the tension between supply chain resilience and private sector costs. Potential policy responses include consideration of public support for primary aluminum production, expansion of domestic scrap consumption, and funding of research and development (R&D). Additional actions may be taken regarding the Section 232 tariffs or other trade remedies,[92](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn92 "In June 2026, T he Aluminum Association, alongside associations of aluminum producers from Canada, Europe, and Japan , advocated for collective action among nations, through mechanisms such as common tariffs, to offset China's subsidies to its domestic industry. See The Aluminum Association, \"Global Aluminium Associations Welcome New OECD Data on Industrial Subsidies,\" press release, June 1, 2026, https://www.aluminum.org/news/global-aluminium-associations-welcome-new-oecd-data-industri...") which other CRS reports have addressed.[93](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn93 "CRS Insight IN12519, Section 232 Tariffs on Steel and Aluminum , by Kyla H. Kitamura .") Congress may also take no action while continuing to monitor market responses to current events.

Public Support for Primary Aluminum Production

Congress could consider whether to support primary aluminum production by offering smelters financial resources, such as grants, tax credits, or other incentives. As discussed in "[Domestic Primary Aluminum Production](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#_Ref241762746)," above, electricity represents a sizable share of primary aluminum production costs. Congress could assess whether to allocate funding for grants that would subsidize the acquisition and installation of new equipment that could improve primary aluminum smelters' energy efficiency. An example of past legislation that created such a program is Section 50161 of the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022.[94](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn94 "See § 50161 in P.L. 117-169 . The DOE g rant for the planned primary smelter in Inola, OK , originated from this program.")

In addition, Congress could evaluate whether to expand existing tax credits that manufacturers can access when investing in equipment such as on-site electricity generation capacity or power storage.[95](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn95 "CRS Insight IN12719, Technology-Neutral Clean Electricity Tax Credits Under the IRA and the OBBBA , by Nicholas E. Buffie .") An example of an existing tax credit is the Clean Electricity Investment Tax Credit (26 U.S.C. §48E). Smelters sourcing some of their energy needs through on-site solar panels or managing the purchase of electricity by using batteries may lower the costs of producing aluminum.[96](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn96 "For an example of a manufacturing facility using on - site energy generation to supplement its electricity consumption , see Robyn Griggs Lawrence , \" World's L argest S olar- P owered S teel M ill B reaks G round in Colorado , \" Construction Dive , March 2, 2022, https://www.constructiondive.com/news/worlds-largest-solar-powered-steel-mill-breaks-ground-in-colorado/619381/  .")

These vehicles of public support may decrease federal government revenues or increase government expenditures. Depending on how the public support is structured, potential public financial support may additionally raise concerns in certain U.S. trade agreements as they relate to subsidies.[97](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn97 "For example, World Trade Organization (WTO) agreements. For more information on the WTO, see CRS In Focus IF10002, World Trade Organization , by Cathleen D. Cimino-Isaacs .")

Domestic Scrap Consumption

Another option to ease the potential tension between supply chain resilience and private sector costs could be to increase the domestic consumption of aluminum scrap. Currently, the United States exports a substantial amount of aluminum scrap. Policymakers could consider options that would increase the amount of U.S. aluminum scrap that is directed to U.S. secondary aluminum smelters, which may lower the costs of goods containing secondary aluminum.

One bill introduced in the 119th Congress—the CANS Act of 2026 ([H.R. 7992](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119/hr/7992), as introduced)—would direct the executive branch to study options to redirect would-be scrap exports back to domestic aluminum supply chains. Another bill—the Secure Aluminum Supply Chains Act ([H.R. 9161](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119/hr/9161), as introduced)—would direct the executive branch to study economic security risks posed by the export of aluminum scrap to certain countries and options to address such risks.

Generally, a policy that would limit U.S. aluminum scrap exports might reduce costs for U.S. aluminum smelters while decreasing revenues for U.S. entities that sell scrap, such as aluminum fabricators and recyclers. A policy that would subsidize (e.g., with consumption tax credits) U.S. smelters for purchasing U.S. scrap would increase demand for U.S. scrap and therefore may increase revenues for fabricators and recyclers.

Research and Development

Congress might consider the extent of support for R&D funding to commercialize technologies that could improve the productivity of the domestic aluminum industry or lower demand for aluminum from certain sectors. Examples may include technologies that allow facilities to produce HPA using scrap. Such breakthroughs could enable the U.S. aluminum sector to utilize abundant domestic scrap and more competitively produce higher-value aluminum products. Alternatively, research into new materials that could substitute aluminum in industrial or military applications may also reduce the national security and economic risks posed by disruptions in domestic supply.

Congress may assess current levels of funding for agencies and public-private partnerships that advance R&D relating to the aluminum industry. For example, the U.S. Department of Energy's Advanced Research Projects Agency-Energy (ARPA-E) is authorized to "to enhance the economic and energy security of the United States through the development of energy technologies that … improve the energy efficiency of all economic sectors."[98](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn98 "42 U.S.C. §16538 .") Past ARPA-E projects have focused on aluminum production.[99](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn99 "D OE , Advanced Research Projects Agency-Energy , \" Energy Research Company (ERCo) , Integrated Minimill to Produce Aluminum from Scrap , \" accessed September 23, 2026, https://arpa-e.energy.gov/programs-and-initiatives/search-all-projects/integrated-minimill-produce-aluminum-scrap .") An example of a public-private partnership is the Manufacturing USA program, which is authorized to "facilitate the transition of innovative technologies into scalable, cost-effective, and high-performing manufacturing capabilities."[100](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49496.html#fn100 "15 U.S.C. §278s .") There might be additional programs, or programs that Congress might consider creating, that could advance aluminum manufacturing, identify alternative materials to substitute aluminum, or innovate in other ways.
