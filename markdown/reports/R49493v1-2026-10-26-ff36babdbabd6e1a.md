## Summary

The Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) started its hate crime statistics program pursuant to the Hate Crime Statistics Act of 1990 (HCSA; P.L. 101-275). The HCSA directs the U.S. Department of Justice (DOJ) to acquire and report data on crimes that "manifest evidence of prejudice based on race, gender and gender identity, religion, disability, sexual orientation, or ethnicity, including where appropriate the crimes of murder, non-negligent manslaughter; forcible rape; aggravated assault, simple assault, intimidation; arson; and destruction, damage or vandalism of property." These data are collected by the local, state, federal, and tribal law enforcement agencies to which the offenses were reported and submitted to the FBI's Uniform Crime Reporting (UCR) program.

In addition to the FBI's hate crime data collection efforts, the Bureau of Justice Statistics (BJS) collects data on hate crime victimizations as part of the National Crime Victimization Survey (NCVS). NCVS respondents self-report their personal experiences with certain non-fatal crimes (e.g., robbery, aggravated assault) and, among responses to many other questions, whether they believe their criminal victimization was bias-motivated. In contrast to the FBI's data, NCVS victimizations include incidents that were reported and not reported to the police. This report presents analyses of hate crime data collected from 2015 to 2024 by both the FBI and NCVS.

Both the UCR and NCVS data indicate that hate crime rates have generally increased from 2015 to 2024. The UCR hate crime data show an 89% increase in the police-verified hate crime victimization rate over this 10-year period, rising from 1.8 hate crime victimizations per 100,000 people in 2015 to 3.4 per 100,000 in 2024. Similarly, the NCVS data show a 225% increase over this same 10-year period, rising from 55 self-reported hate crime victimizations per 100,000 people in 2015 to 179 per 100,000 in 2024. This trend of increasing hate crime rates observed in both datasets contrasts with current trends in non-bias-motivated crime rates, which have been generally decreasing in recent years.

The UCR and NCVS hate crime data show that most reported victimizations from 2015 to 2024 were motivated by race/ethnicity/ancestry. This was the bias category for 62.7% of victimizations reported in the UCR and 66.3% of victimizations reported in the NCVS. Other common bias categories were sexual orientation, religion, and gender identity. The UCR, but not the NCVS, reports more specific bias motivations within these broader categories. From 2015 to 2024, 51.0% of the 56,512 race/ethnicity/ancestry-motivated victimizations in the UCR data were anti-Black/African American, 56.9% of the 17,074 sexual-orientation-motivated offenses were anti-gay (male), 54.9% of the 13,098 religion-motivated offenses were anti-Jewish, and 78.3% of the 3,049 gender-identity-motivated offenses were anti-transgender.

## Federal Hate Crime Data Collection

The Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) started its hate crime statistics program pursuant to the Hate Crime Statistics Act of 1990 (HCSA; P.L. 101-275). Currently, the act requires the U.S. Department of Justice (DOJ) to acquire and report data on crimes that "manifest evidence of prejudice based on race, gender and gender identity, religion, disability, sexual orientation, or ethnicity, including where appropriate the crimes of murder, non-negligent manslaughter; forcible rape; aggravated assault, simple assault, intimidation; arson; and destruction, damage or vandalism of property."[1](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn1) Since the enactment of the HCSA, the FBI has continually collected hate crime (alternatively referred to as *bias-motivated crime*) offense data submitted by state, local, federal, and tribal law enforcement agencies (LEAs) as part of its Uniform Crime Reporting (UCR) program. The FBI then compiles and publishes hate crime data annually, currently spanning the years 1991-2025.[2](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn2)

The Bureau of Justice Statistics (BJS) also collects data on hate crime victimizations as part of the National Crime Victimization Survey (NCVS). NCVS data are collected in partnership with the Census Bureau through semiannual interviews with residents of a nationally representative sample of households. Twice each year, the NCVS collects responses from approximately 240,000 individuals age 12 or older in 150,000 households.[3](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn3) Respondents are asked about their personal experiences with certain non-fatal person crimes (i.e., rape or sexual assault, robbery, aggravated assault, simple assault, and personal larceny) and household property crimes (i.e., burglary/trespassing, motor vehicle theft, and household thefts) within the previous six months.

The NCVS also collects demographic information about individual victims (e.g., age, race, sex, disability status) and details about each crime incident, such as injuries to the victim, use of a weapon, and reporting to the police. This data collection effort intends to estimate the number of criminal victimizations in the United States each year, regardless of whether the victimizations were reported to police.

As part of the survey, the NCVS asks respondents who report victimizations if they believe their victimization was bias-motivated. If respondents report that they believe they were, they are then asked what evidence they have that the incidents were bias-motivated. Historically, BJS has identified an incident as a hate crime if the victim reported one of the following: (1) the police confirmed the victim was targeted for his or her characteristics or religious beliefs, (2) the offender(s) left behind hate symbols, or (3) the offenders(s) used hate language.[4](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn4) For the purposes of this report, CRS used the same operational definition for identifying hate crimes as the NCVS.

Some policymakers have shown concern about the number and nature of hate crimes in the United States.[5](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn5) This report explores data from the UCR and NCVS from 2015 to 2024 on the nature of hate crimes and describes the victims and perpetrators of these offenses.[6](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn6) Though 2025 UCR hate crime data are available, as of the date of this report, the 2025 NCVS data have not been released. Thus, for the sake of equivalent comparison between the two data sources, CRS elected to use the 10-year range of 2015 to 2024 for the data in this report. The UCR and NCVS hate crime data were analyzed separately, but results (along with associated figures and tables) are presented in parallel to provide a more complete picture of hate crime in the United States. Following the review of the hate crime data, policy implications from the UCR and NCVS findings are briefly discussed.

When discussing findings from the present data, three different units of measurement will be used in this report, depending on the appropriate context:   * ***Incident******:*** "One or more offenses committed by the same offender, or group of offenders acting in concert, at the same time and place."[12](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn12) Thus, a single incident can have multiple offenses, offenders, and victims. Crime incidents are distinguished from victimizations in that one crime may have multiple victims."[13](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn13) Given that the number of victimizations is the primary unit of analysis in the NCVS data, the majority of UCR analyses in this report will use number of victimizations as the unit of analysis to allow for more meaningful comparisons between UCR and NCVS results. * ***Offense******:*** For UCR hate crime reporting purposes, criminal offenses are broadly grouped into different *offense categories* (e.g., fraud offenses) consisting of distinct offenses (e.g., wire fraud).[14](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn14) Occasionally, the number of offenses will be used as the unit of analysis in both UCR and NCVS data, when appropriate. |

### Caveats and Limitations to Federal Hate Crime Data

#### UCR Hate Crime Data

There are important caveats and limitations to consider when interpreting the UCR hate crime data. First, these data represent only hate crimes reported to the police and subsequently reported to the FBI's UCR hate crime statistics program. UCR data do not account for criminal incidents not reported to the police, which may represent a substantial number of offenses. For example, BJS estimated that from 2015 to 2019 approximately 42% of hate crime victimizations were not reported to police.[15](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn15)

In addition to underreporting to police, the standards for vetting and investigating bias motivation in criminal incidents may affect the number of hate crimes reported to the UCR program each year. Specifically, the FBI lays out a *two-tier decisionmaking process* for investigating hate crimes. In the first tier, the responding officer must flag a criminal incident as a "suspected bias-motivated crime" if any bias indicators (e.g., defacing a building with a swastika) are present. In the second tier, a specially trained officer or unit must review the facts of the incident to make a final determination of whether a hate crime occurred.[16](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn16)

This comparatively higher standard of vetting and reporting (compared to non-bias-motivated crimes) may result in undercounting potential hate crimes reported to the FBI, particularly in smaller jurisdictions that lack funding, training, or staff to employ a unit of officers or even a single officer specially trained in hate crimes to complete the second tier of investigation. Furthermore, this higher standard of evidence may lead the UCR data to undercount hate crimes against groups that are not associated with obvious bias indicators. For example, the swastika is a well-known bias indicator for anti-Jewish sentiment, but few equally well-known symbols represent other religion-based bias motivations (e.g., anti-Buddhist, anti-Protestant). Thus, the two-tier decisionmaking process may systematically undercount certain bias-motivated crimes against certain populations with less obvious bias indicators.

Another caveat is that these data represent submissions only from the LEAs that participate in the FBI's UCR program and submit hate crime data. Law enforcement participation in the FBI's UCR program is voluntary, and not all LEAs participate. However, with the exception of a few years, there has been a general trend of increasing numbers of LEAs participating in the UCR hate crime statistics program each year over the past decade.[17](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn17) In its *UCR* *Summary of Reported Crimes in the Nation, 2024*, the FBI reported that 16,419 LEAs—or 84.9% of all LEAs enrolled in the UCR program—participated in the hate crime statistics program in 2024. This amounted to a 2.6% increase from the previous year's report, and, taken together, all participating agencies cover 95.1% of the total U.S. population.[18](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn18) However, as some researchers have noted, jurisdiction-specific hate crime reporting policies vary.[19](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn19) Some states (e.g., New Jersey) have policies that explicitly require their LEAs to investigate hate crimes according to specific standards and report these incidents to the state agencies responsible for submitting these data to the FBI.[20](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn20)

When an agency reports that no known hate crimes occurred in its jurisdiction for a given year, this is known as a *zero report*. Of the 16,419 LEAs that participated in the UCR program in 2024, 81% submitted zero reports. This is not anomalous, as most LEAs participating in the UCR hate crime statistics program in any given year submit zero reports as opposed to reporting data on hate crimes. Thus, even though participation among LEAs has generally increased in recent years, a relatively small percentage of them (about 2,127 per year on average) contribute data pertaining to reported hate crime incidents each year (see [**Figure A-1**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref215060105) in [**Appendix A**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref215747900) for a visual depiction of hate crime reporting from 1996 to 2024).[21](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn21)

Additionally, since 2015, the percentage of agencies reporting at least one hate crime incident has varied considerably depending on the type of agency (e.g., city, county, state). The percentage of state police agencies reporting one or more hate crimes was higher than any other LEA type from 2015 to 2024. However, the relatively high annual percentage of state LEAs reporting at least one hate crime is partially due to the relatively low number of state police agencies compared to most other types of LEAs (see [**Figure A-2**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref215757901) in [**Appendix A**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref215747900) for a visual depiction of hate crime reporting by agency type from 2015 to 2024).

There are also limitations on the information available from the FBI's publicly available hate crime data. For instance, though some offender demographics (e.g., race) are included, little to no information about victims is included. Additionally, no arrest information is included, meaning the data do not indicate whether any offenders were arrested and charged for the reported hate crime incidents. Thus, the UCR hate crime data primarily provide information about the characteristics of the criminal incident (e.g., bias motivation, number and type of offense, number of victims) rather than the persons involved.

#### NCVS Hate Crime Data

There are also important caveats and limitations to consider when interpreting the NCVS hate crime data. First, individuals self-report their criminal victimizations through structured interviews with Census Bureau representatives. Though interviewing crime victims helps account for incidents that were not reported to the police, there are data quality concerns associated with this approach, such as victims potentially misremembering relevant details about criminal incidents. BJS tries to limit recall bias by (1) having interviewers clearly explain to the respondent what a bias-motivated crime is and (2) conducting interviews at six-month intervals (rather than annually). In the case of bias-motivated crimes specifically, there is also the issue that, unlike the UCR data, the NCVS does not employ the two-tier decisionmaking process that public safety officials use to determine whether a criminal incident was bias-motivated.

The NCVS interviews only individuals age 12 and older living in non-institutional settings.[22](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn22) It therefore does not capture crimes committed against young children, people experiencing homelessness, or people living in correctional facilities, nursing homes, military bases, and other institutional settings.[23](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn23) The NCVS also covers fewer types of crimes than UCR data does. Additionally, because these data are collected from a sample, statistical estimates produced from NCVS data have a margin of error that is important to consider when comparing estimates of specific variables.[24](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn24) These data cannot always produce reliable estimates for subpopulations with small sample sizes (e.g., people with disabilities).

In 2024, BJS redesigned the NCVS in an effort to improve the efficiency, reliability, and utility of the survey.[25](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn25) The survey was administered using a split sample design, where approximately half of the surveyed households were interviewed using the legacy survey instrument (the questions that were asked in previous years) and the other half was surveyed using the redesigned instrument. The split was designed so that each half sample would be similar to the samples from recent years, and BJS included a set of survey weights that adjusts the half samples to U.S. population totals. In this report, CRS used the legacy instrument to avoid concerns about whether the data was comparable to previous years. However, the 2024 half sample was smaller (109,341 persons age 12 or older in 69,396 households) than the sample from previous years (approximately 240,000 persons in approximately 150,000 households), so the standard errors for 2024 are larger.

### Overall Trends from 2015 to 2024

According to UCR hate crime data from 2015 to 2024, there were 91,559 hate crime incidents reported to the police, with 95,222 associated offenses affecting a total of 90,143 individual victims.[26](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn26) [**Figure 2**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref214363348) shows that hate crime incident rates (per 100,000 people) rose over this 10-year period, increasing from 1.8 incidents per 100,000 in 2015 to a peak of 3.5 per 100,000 in 2023. However, the rate declined from 3.6 incidents per 100,000 people in 2023 to 3.4 per 100,000 in 2024, amounting to a 3% decrease in number of incidents. Academic researchers[27](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn27) and advocacy groups[28](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn28) have also noted the increasing rate of hate crime incidents over this period.

UCR victimization and offense rates have largely followed the trend in incident rates, with offense rates generally being slightly higher than incident or victimization rates ([**Figure 2**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref214363348)). The similarity among these rates is important to keep in mind: Though victimizations will be the primary unit of analysis from the UCR data throughout the rest of this report (except where indicated otherwise), patterns from the UCR hate crime data remain largely consistent whether looking at incidents, victimizations, or offenses (see the Key Definitions text box in the "[Federal Hate Crime Data Collection](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref236065975)" section for further explanation of these units of analysis).

Further analysis revealed that this trend of increasing hate crime incident rates was statistically significant, even when accounting for overall crime rates (violent and property crime),[29](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn29) with the hate crime incident rate increasing by 0.26 incidents per 100,000 people per year on average (see [**Table B-2**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref243472360)). Parallel analyses confirmed both the victimization and offense rate increases to also be statistically significant, even when controlling for overall crime rates. In other words, hate crime incident, victimization, and offense rates all increased from 2015 to 2024 independently from overall crime rates across this period (see [**Table B-2**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref243472360)).

More recently, however, the 2025 UCR data—which is not covered in the analyses in this report for reasons discussed in "[Federal Hate Crime Data Collection](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref241673762)"—show that hate crime incidents have decreased by 7.0% from 2024 to 2025. Offenses and victimizations similarly decreased from 2024 to 2025.[30](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn30) Thus, the trend of increasing hate crime rates described herein may be limited to the period of time (i.e., 2015 to 2024) analyzed in this report and should thus be interpreted with this potential caveat in mind.

Of the 91,559 total UCR hate crime incidents reported to the police from 2015 to 2024, individuals were by far the most common victim type (79.6% of incidents), with businesses being the next most common (7.4%). Most of the UCR hate crime incidents during this period (68.6%) involved single individual victims.

### Incident Characteristics

From 2015 to 2024, most bias-motivated offenses reported to the UCR program involved crimes against persons (58,344 offenses, or 61.5% of all offenses), followed by crimes against property (34,432 offenses, or 36.3% of all offenses) and crimes against society[38](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn38) (2,125 offenses, or 2.2% of all offenses). Most bias-motivated crimes against persons were assault offenses,[39](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn39) whereas destruction of property (which includes vandalism and damage of property) was the most common bias-motivated offense against property. ### Offender Characteristics

Limiting the analysis of 2015-2024 UCR and NCVS hate crime data to incidents with single offenders (as opposed to multiple offenders),[43](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn43) [**Figure 14**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref222236116) compares the race/ethnicity of offenders in the two datasets for that year. Though the raw numbers differ, the patterns are nearly identical between the UCR and NCVS, with non-Hispanic Whites making up most hate crime offenders.

There is a particular caveat to bear in mind when interpreting UCR offender-level data in the current analyses. When offender information is reported to the police (referred to in the data as a "known offender"), that does not mean that the offender or offenders have been positively identified. Rather, law enforcement gather this information from victims, witnesses, and other sources at the time of the offense. Thus, most offender-level information, such as race, is presumptive in nature and based on information available to officers at that time. As the FBI explains:

In the UCR Program, the term *known offender* does not imply the suspect's identity is known; rather, the term indicates some aspect of the suspect was identified, thus distinguishing the suspect from an unknown offender. Law enforcement agencies specify the number of offenders and, when possible, the race, ethnicity, and age of the offender or offenders as a group.[44](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn44)

The NCVS contains questions about offenders involved in criminal incidents. Respondents are first asked the following question: "Do you know or have you learned anything about the offender(s)—for instance, whether there was one or more than one offender involved, whether it was someone young or old, or male or female?"[45](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn45) If respondents answer yes, they are asked a series of funneled follow-up questions, such as how they learned about the offender (e.g., firsthand seeing or hearing offender, learned from another eyewitness, from police), whether they knew the offender, and basic demographic information (if known). Thus, like the UCR offender information, these NCVS offender characteristics, such as sex or race/ethnicity, reflect the respondents' own knowledge and memories of the facts.

## Proportionality of Hate Crime Victimizations by Race/Ethnicity and Religion Compared to the U.S. Population

Looking at the number of victimizations by bias motivation from both the UCR and NCVS data (see [**Figure 12**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref227682221)) might raise questions for policymakers about the degree to which some groups are disproportionately targeted by bias-motived crimes compared to their relative shares of the population.

Using five-year estimates from the Census Bureau's 2024 American Community Survey (ACS),[46](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn46) CRS compared race/ethnicity/ancestry-motivated hate crimes targeting individuals[47](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn47) in the 2020-2024 UCR and NCVS data to ACS population demographics for the corresponding groups.[48](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn48) More specifically, UCR race/ethnicity/ancestry victimizations were compared to total ACS population estimates broken down by race, whereas NCVS victimizations were compared to the ACS population estimates limited to those 12 years and older in order to more accurately compare to the NCVS sample parameters. [**Figure 16**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref242782258) shows two key patterns when comparing 2020-2024 UCR race/ethnicity/ancestry victimizations, 2020-2024 NCVS race and ethnicity victimizations, and the corresponding ACS population estimates.

First, of all hate crime victimizations motivated by race/ethnicity, the percentage of anti-White victimizations from 2020 to 2024 as measured by both the UCR and NCVS data were disproportionally low compared to the average percentage of non-Hispanic Whites in the population of the United States during the period. However, the pattern is more complex looking at anti-Black and anti-Hispanic victimizations. Specifically, the NCVS data showed that of all hate crime victimizations motivated by race/ethnicity, the percentage of anti-Black victimizations was roughly proportional to the Black share of the U.S. population age 12 and older, whereas the UCR data showed a disproportionately high percentage of anti-Black victimizations compared to their share of the U.S. population. An opposite pattern emerged between the two data sources concerning anti-Hispanic victimizations, with the UCR data indicating that the percentage of anti-Hispanic victimizations was roughly proportional to the Hispanic share of the U.S. population, whereas the NCVS data showed a disproportionately high percentage of anti-Hispanic victimizations compared to the Hispanic share of the U.S. population age 12 and older. A statistical analysis confirmed that both the UCR and NCVS racially motivated victimizations overall are disproportionate to corresponding Census estimates (see [**Table B-1**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref229133343)).

Second, the number of 2020-2024 UCR and NCVS race/ethnicity/ancestry victimizations were disparate from each other in some cases. Specifically, proportionally more anti-Black bias-motivated victimizations were reported to the UCR program than to the NCVS, whereas proportionally more anti-White and anti-Hispanic bias-motivated victimizations were reported to the NCVS than to the UCR. One important caveat is that for the NCVS data, specific bias motivations were inferred based on the respondent's own self-reported race.

The Census Bureau does not collect data on religious affiliation. However, other nongovernment entities conduct surveys on this topic that can provide an estimate of population-level religious affiliation in the United States. One commonly cited religious affiliation survey is Pew Research Center's Religious Landscape Study, which surveyed approximately 36,000 respondents across the United States in 2023 and 2024 and asked various questions about religious affiliation and practice.[49](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn49) These religious affiliation estimates from Pew and the corresponding percentages of religion-specific hate crimes targeting individuals[50](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn50) in the 2023 and 2024 UCR data were compared.[51](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn51) As shown in [**Figure 17**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref219988133), the percentage of all hate crime victimizations motivated by anti-Protestant, Catholic, and Atheist/Agnostic bias (1.4%, 1.4%, and 0.8% of all religion-motivated hate crime victimizations, respectively) was disproportionately low compared to the estimated percentage of the population claiming Protestant, Catholic, and Atheistic/Agnostic affiliation (40.0%, 18.9%, and 11.0% of the weighted survey respondents, respectively). On the other hand, the percentage of all hate crime victimizations motivated by anti-Jewish and Muslim bias (70.8% and 14.5% of all religion-motivated hate victimizations, respectively) was disproportionately high compared to the surveyed Jewish and Muslim populations of the United States (1.7% and 1.2% of the weighted survey respondents, respectively). All other religion bias motivations were approximately proportional to those religions' estimated share of the population in 2023 and 2024. A statistical analysis conducted by CRS also indicated that the UCR religion-motivated victimizations were overall disproportionate to the corresponding Pew estimates (see [**Table B-1**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref229133343)).

There are many important caveats and limitations to the data presented in this section. Due to the nature of Census and Pew data collection, these data represent very specific demographic moments. U.S. racial demographics and religious affiliation trends are dynamic and may continue changing in the future, limiting the future generalizability of these patterns. Thus, these findings should be interpreted as a snapshot of these points in time rather than stable trends over time.

There are some possible explanations for the patterns of hate crime victimization discussed in this section and throughout the rest of this report. The first is that broader social and environmental factors may be at least partially influencing the hate crime victimization patterns presented in this report. Other researchers have observed that certain racial, ethnic, and religious groups are disproportionately targeted for hate crimes depending on societal trends.[52](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn52) For instance, some research has found that as minority groups increase as a proportion of the population in an area, so too does their risk of hate crime victimization.[53](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn53) Furthermore, influential factors may differ based on the specific bias motivation. In its guide on hate crimes, the Center for Problem-Oriented Policing offers the following analysis of the issue:

Anti-black hate crime usually occurs in relatively organized communities with high levels of informal social control. In contrast, anti-white hate crimes are more likely to occur in disorganized locations where residential turnover is more common. Meanwhile, anti-gay hate crimes are more likely to occur in areas where gays are more numerous. Again, context is important. It has been argued that local hate crimes against Jews are linked to a rise in tension or a specific event in the Israel-Palestine conflict or other tensions occurring in the Middle East. Similarly, some find a correlation between the demonization of Muslims in the media and the victimization of local Muslims by hate crimes.[54](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn54)

A second explanation to consider is that these patterns could be at least partially reflective of group differences in reporting hate crimes. For instance, past research has found that Black individuals, particularly Black women, are more likely than persons in other demographic groups to report criminal victimizations to the police.[55](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn55) Exploratory analyses of victim race/ethnicity in the 2020-2024 NCVS data also suggested that Black victims were the most likely of any racial group to report their hate crime victimizations to police (see [**Table A-4**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref238560454)). This possibility, some could argue, may partially explain why anti-Black victimizations comprised the most common bias motivation in the 2015-2024 UCR data.

These trends could also reflect the inherently empirical issues related to identifying and reporting hate crime victimizations. For example, in its *Hate Crime Data Collection Guidelines and Training Manual*, the FBI states that "bias-related oral comments" such as racial slurs and epithets, among other aspects of the crime, can be objective evidence of a crime being motivated by bias.[56](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn56) Certain racial or religious groups have a wider lexicon of slurs and hate symbols directed against them, meaning it is easier to identify that a criminal act was bias-motivated against that group. For instance, if someone wearing a shirt depicting a swastika assaults a Jewish person leaving a synagogue, it may be much easier for law enforcement and the victim to determine that it was an anti-Jewish-motivated assault. In comparison, if someone assaults a Buddhist leaving a temple, it might be more difficult to determine whether it was a bias-motivated crime given that there is not a symbolic equivalent to the swastika for anti-Buddhist sentiment that is a widely known one used in a Western context.

## Policy Considerations for Congress

As shown in the analyses throughout this report, increasingly more hate crime incidents have been reported annually to the UCR hate crime statistics program and the NCVS over the course of the past 10 years (see [**Figure 2**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref214363348) and [**Figure 3**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref243466408)). This trend coincides with a time when rates of reported non-bias-motivated property and violent crimes have been decreasing.[57](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn57) The number of agencies participating in the UCR program increased from 2015 to 2024 as well (see [**Figure A-1**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref215060105) and [**Figure A-2**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref215757901)). Thus, it is plausible that the increase in reported hate crimes over the past 10 years in the UCR data could be partially due to improved LEA reporting during this time. However, the greater number of LEAs reporting hate crime data to the UCR program does not account for the increase in self-reported hate crime victimization found in the NCVS data during this same period.

As shown in the present findings, bias-motivated crimes seem to be distinct from non-bias-motivated crime, and, at times, the two types of crime show disparate patterns. CRS analyses indicated that overall crime rates were not statistically significantly associated with UCR bias-motivated crime rates from 2015 to 2024 (see [**Table B-2**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref228713553)). The analysis of the data also shows divergent trends among hate crime as compared to other types of crime, particularly in more recent years. For instance, analysis of the 2024 NCVS data showed different victim sex ratios for bias-motivated offenses compared to what is typically observed in similar non-bias-motived crimes. Additionally, the fact that juveniles and women made up such a sizeable portion of the NCVS victim pool suddenly in 2024 (see [**Figure 5**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref228267220), [**Figure 6**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref227675979), and [**Figure 7**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref227676066)) may be noteworthy. These findings might indicate something about juvenile delinquency, school bullying, and violence against women intersecting with increasing online radicalization, extremism, and hate.[58](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn58) The data also seem to indicate that certain groups (e.g., people of Jewish or Muslim faith) are disproportionately targeted in hate crimes, particularly considering their relative share of the U.S. population (see [**Figure 16**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref242782331) and [**Figure 17**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref219988133)).

There are several issues policymakers might consider if they choose to address issues around hate crimes and hate crime reporting. First, Congress could address the potential issues with how LEAs report hate crime to the FBI. Recent legislative efforts to incentivize better reporting among local LEAs include, for example, the Improving Reporting to Prevent Hate Act of 2025 (H.R. 2588, S. 3724), which would require DOJ to proactively audit hate crime data submissions from LEAs serving jurisdictions of more than 100,000 residents for accuracy. Under the proposal, any LEAs found not to be "credibly" reporting hate crimes would risk losing their Edward Byrne Memorial Justice Assistance Grant funding.[59](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn59)

Congress could also develop legislation that would specify which types of bias motivations are included in hate crime data collected by the FBI and/or BJS. Over the past 25 years the FBI has added new bias categories and types (e.g., adding the anti-Arab bias motivation in 2015) to its hate crime reporting program. Further changes may come considering the Office of Management and Budget (OMB) issued a new Statistical Policy Directive that updates federal standards for collecting and presenting data on race and ethnicity. Of note, OMB added two new racial categories for federal statistical reporting: Hispanic or Latino (which was previously categorized as an ethnicity) and Middle Eastern or North African (which includes groups that had previously been grouped under White).[60](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn60) Still, Congress could pass legislation requiring either agency to collect data on additional bias motivations. For instance, under some state hate crime laws, political expression (e.g., Utah) and immigration status (e.g., Illinois) are considered bias categories.[61](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn61)

Congress could also consider legislation to address hate crimes themselves more directly. For example, given the findings of the disparity analysis presented in the "[Proportionality of Hate Crime Victimizations by Race/Ethnicity and Religion Compared to the U.S. Population](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref235804635)" section, policymakers may consider targeting resources and programs for groups disproportionally at risk of hate crime victimization. An example of an existing grant program that has promoted similar policy aims by focusing resources on certain at-risk populations is the Nonprofit Security Grant Program. According to the Federal Emergency Management Agency, this program "provides funding support for target hardening and other physical security enhancements and activities to nonprofit organizations that are at high risk of terrorist attack."[62](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn62)

Funds could also be designated toward grant programs aimed at training police officers in investigating and preventing hate crimes. This could be especially helpful for smaller LEAs that lack training on how to investigate bias motivation. Up-to-date training could also keep officers vigilant of the high-risk targets of hate crime (e.g., increased anti-Jewish or anti-Muslim hate crimes in response to conflict in the Middle East) and ever-evolving indicators of bias motivation (e.g., new hate symbols or slurs circulated on the internet). Some of these funds could also be directed toward community outreach, encouraging people to report hate crimes to police. Exploratory analyses of the 2015-2024 NCVS data showed that, compared to overall crime victimizations, the annual average rate of reporting of hate crime victimizations to police was more inconsistent and seems to be trending downward in recent years (see [**Figure A-3**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref243468186)). Though this trend is difficult to interpret without further data, it may be an indicator of fluctuating trust some communities have in the ability of law enforcement to effectively handle hate crime investigations. Proactive law enforcement outreach on the issue of hate crime may encourage more consistent reporting from victims.

Congress could also provide funding for grants programs to prevent and deter crimes against potentially vulnerable populations. The Matthew Shepard and James Byrd, Jr. Hate Crimes Program is one that has been directed at this purpose.[63](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#fn63) Given the finding from the NCVS data that schools are a common location for hate crime victimizations (see [**Figure 13**](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49493_d606e928f4a82bf49fd4f8ba19023abd1569c3d0.html#_Ref215751341)), Congress could also consider legislation to provide funding to public schools for hate crime awareness, de-radicalization training, and increased security measures. Programs could focus on training teachers and administrators on which groups of students may be at highest risk and how to watch for signs of hate ideology on school grounds and among their students, such as internet-sourced racial slurs.

Congress might also decide to defer to state-level policymakers to address hate crimes in their respective states. Like most other crimes, hate crimes by and large are investigated and prosecuted by state and local authorities. While some of the findings presented in this report indicate a recent increase in hate crime incidents, bias-motivated crimes are still reported to the UCR and NCVS less frequently compared to non-bias-motivated crimes. Thus, Congress could decide to leave further legislation on this topic up to the individual states.

Appendix B.
Inferential Statistics

Glossary

|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| Relative Standard Error | This is a measure of the reliability of an estimate. In mathematical terms, it is equal to (standard error/estimate\*100). Estimates with a relative standard error of more than 50 are generally considered unreliable. |
| Standard Error | This is a measure of the variability of an estimate (e.g., the number of hate crimes). When surveying the population, different samples can be drawn, and each sample will produce its own estimate. The standard error answers the question: *H**ow much* *is* *this estimate expect**ed* *to* *differ from the result of* *another random sample of the population?* A small standard error indicates that the estimate is precise and that little variability would be expected if another sample were drawn. |
| Statistically Significant | By most conventions, this refers to a test statistic yielding a *p*-value of less than .05. In practical terms, this means that the pattern seen in the data would be unlikely if it were only due to pure chance. Note that this is not the same as practical significance—small effects with little real-world impact can still be statistically significant under certain circumstances (e.g., with a large sample size). |
| 95% Confidence Interval | This is another measure of an estimate's variability. It reports a range of values calculated using the standard error. If multiple samples of the population were drawn and used to produce an estimate, 95% of the estimates would fall in this range. |
