Summary

*Decommissioning* offshore oil and gas platforms and related infrastructure generally means removing these facilities from the seafloor, permanently plugging all wells, plugging or removing pipelines, clearing the seafloor of obstructions, and taking related actions associated with the end of oil and gas production from an offshore lease. Pursuant to the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA; 43 U.S.C. §§1331-1356c), the Department of the Interior (DOI) has issued decommissioning regulations for oil and gas operators on the U.S. outer continental shelf (OCS). Regulating OCS decommissioning has posed challenges for DOI's ocean energy management agencies—the Bureau of Ocean Energy Management (BOEM) and the Bureau of Safety and Environmental Enforcement (BSEE), which are being merged into a new DOI agency called the Marine Minerals Administration.

Although DOI's regulations and lease provisions make offshore oil and gas lessees responsible for infrastructure decommissioning, lessees have not always fulfilled their decommissioning responsibilities. In 2024, the Government Accountability Office (GAO) reported that, of roughly 1,600 platforms and 8,000 wells existing in the Gulf of Mexico (now Gulf of America) at that time, more than 500 platforms and 2,700 wells were overdue for decommissioning under DOI regulations. In some cases, offshore operators have been unable to meet their decommissioning responsibilities—for instance, due to bankruptcy—leaving the federal government responsible for decommissioning such so-called *orphaned* infrastructure. Financial bonds required by DOI from offshore operators to cover decommissioning costs have not been sufficient to cover all liability in such cases.

Congress and successive presidential Administrations have considered how to address offshore decommissioning needs and how to reduce federal financial liability for decommissioning. Some approaches have involved changes to DOI's financial assurance and supplemental bonding regulations. Other approaches have considered ways to reduce lessees' decommissioning costs, especially through options for *decommissioning in place*. Under these approaches, lessees do not need to fully remove all infrastructure, but can leave some structures on the seafloor to serve various purposes. A prominent existing framework for decommissioning in place is the Rigs-to-Reefs program, under which some structures may remain in the ocean to serve as artificial reefs for marine life. Some Members of Congress and other stakeholders seek to grow this program, citing potential enhancements to fisheries and some marine industries, while others question whether the structures could pose environmental or navigational hazards. Other potential uses for legacy offshore infrastructure, such as repurposing for other offshore energy development, offshore aquaculture, or sea-based space launches, also have been proposed. In a regulatory agenda published in August 2026, DOI announced a proposal to make *toppling in place* (one method of decommissioning in place) the default standard for OCS infrastructure, under specified circumstances. Also, in September 2026, President Trump issued an executive order, "Restoring American Saltwater Angling and Recreation," which among other provisions directed DOI to establish a program to facilitate conversion of decommissioned offshore oil and gas and other-use structures into permanent artificial reefs through a variety of reefing methods.

Congress may continue to consider how to address economic and environmental issues related to offshore decommissioning and whether to promote alternatives to full infrastructure removal. Bills under consideration in the 119th Congress would address decommissioning enforcement ([H.R. 8099](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119/hr/8099), [S. 4239](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119/s/4239)), offshore lessees' financial strength and bonding requirements ([H.R. 9034](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119/hr/9034), [H.R. 9035](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119/hr/9035), [S. 4715](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119/s/4715)), federal appropriations for decommissioning activities ([H.R. 9171](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119/hr/9171)), and artificial reefing and the Rigs-to-Reefs program ([H.R. 5745](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119/hr/5745)).

What Is Offshore Decommissioning?

Decommissioning generally occurs when an offshore lease ceases to produce oil and gas in paying quantities, thus terminating the lease (unless a lease suspension has been approved or other specified conditions are met).[11](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn11 "30 C.F.R. §556.601; and 30 C.F.R. Part 250, Subpart Q.") DOI's regulations state that within one year after a lease terminates, all wells must be permanently plugged and all infrastructure and equipment used in the exploration and production of oil and gas ([**Figure 1**](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#_Ref223008468)) must be removed from the marine environment.[12](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn12 "30 C.F.R. Part 250, Subpart Q , §§ 250.1710 and 250.1725.")

Decommissioning an offshore platform generally entails multiple steps:

* permanently plugging all wells supported by the platform (including severing the well casings 15 feet below the *mudline*)[13](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn13 "In DOI regulations, the term m udline refers to the seafloor.") within one year after the lease terminates;
* removing all platforms and other facilities to at least 15 feet below the mudline within one year after the lease terminates, unless the platforms and other facilities are to be used for other approved activities;
* decommissioning all pipelines and removing any pipelines the BSEE regional supervisor determines are an obstruction or pose adverse environmental effects;[14](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn14 "Under the regulations (30 C.F.R. §§250.1750-250.1751), if the BSEE r egional s upervisor determines that a pipeline does not constitute a hazard to navigation or commercial fishing, does not interfere with other OCS uses, and does not have adverse environmental effects, it can be decommissioned in place. Decommissioning in place involves flushing the pipeline, filling it with seawater, and cutting and plugging the ends, among other potential actions (30 C.F.R. §250.1751). In 2021, GAO est...") and
* clearing the seafloor of all obstructions associated with oil and gas operations within 60 days after well plugging and platform removal.[15](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn15 "30 C.F.R. Part 250, Subpart Q, §§250.17 00-250.1754.")

The removal of the platform and its associated equipment also includes its transportation to an area onshore for recycling or disposal.[16](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn16 "Mark J. Kaiser et al., Assessment of Opportunities for Alternative Uses of Hydrocarbon Infrastructure in the Gulf of Mexico , OCS Study BOEM, Regulation and Enforcement (BOEMRE) 2011-028, July 2011, p. 1 (hereinafter BOEMRE, Assessment of Opportunities for Alternative Uses of Hydrocarbon Infrastructure ) .") According to BSEE, "over the past decade, the offshore energy industry has averaged 127 platform removals per year."[17](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn17 "BSEE , \"Decommissioning,\" https://www.bsee.gov/  decommissioning .") In 2023, a study estimated the average costs to decommission a shallow-water or a deep-water *well* located on the OCS (i.e., federal waters) in the Gulf of America to be $660,000 and $24 million, respectively.[18](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn18 "Mark Agerton et al., \"Implications and Environmental Implications of Unplugged Wells for the Gulf of Mexico and Coastal Waters,\" Nature Energy , vol. 8 (2023), pp. 536-547. The authors of this study \" set the shallow-to-deep water cut-off at 1,000 feet .\"") The estimated cost does not include the cost for decommissioning the *platform* or associated infrastructure. In its 2024 report, GAO stated that decommissioning all infrastructure on a lease could cost tens of millions of dollars per lease in shallow water and hundreds of millions per lease in deep water.[19](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn19 "GAO, Interior Needs to Improve Decommissioning Enforcement , GAO-24-106229, p. 2. GAO defined deep water as any depth greater than 1,000 feet.")

Although DOI's regulations generally require lessees to "remove all platforms and other facilities" and to "clear the seafloor of all obstructions" created by the facilities,[20](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn20 "30 C.F.R. §250.1703.") the regulations provide for certain alternatives to full removal of the platform from the marine environment. One option is to allow all or part of a platform to remain on the OCS as part of a Rigs-to-Reefs program (i.e., as an artificial reef site).[21](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn21 "30 C.F.R. §250.1730. Also see BSEE, \"Rigs-to-Reefs , \" https://www.bsee.gov/what-we-do/environmental-compliance/environmental-programs/rigs-to-reefs (hereinafter BSEE, \"Rigs to Reefs\").") This generally involves one of three decommissioning methods that allow the structure to serve as an artificial reef for marine life:[22](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn22 "BSEE, \"Rigs-to-Reefs Program: Fact Sheet,\" March 2022, https://www.bsee.gov/sites/bsee.gov/files/rigs-to-reefs-program-fact-sheet.pdf .")

* *partial removal*—severing the platform at a permitted depth (typically at least 85 feet below the surface of the water) that allows a vessel to navigate in the area unobstructed, and placing the severed portion on the seafloor beside the base of the structure;
* *toppl**ing**-in-place*—severing the entire structure from the seafloor and toppling it onto its side; or
* *tow-and-place*—severing the structure from the seafloor and towing it to a selected reef.

Another option is to reuse or repurpose the offshore oil and gas facility for uses other than an artificial reef. For example, an offshore operator may apply to reuse a facility for activities that "support OCS oil and gas production and transportation" or for other energy-related and marine-related uses (e.g., offshore wind; subseafloor storage of natural gas or other substances).[23](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn23 "30 C.F.R. §250.1725.") An operator would need DOI approval to reuse or relocate an offshore oil and gas facility. Options for repurposing offshore oil and gas structures are discussed further below (section on "[Decommissioning in Place](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#_Ref223016968)").

Decommissioning Needs by Region

DOI divides the OCS into four regions: the Gulf of America, Pacific, Atlantic, and Alaska regions.[24](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn24 "For regional maps, see BOEM, \"Maps and GIS Data,\" https://www.boem.gov/oil-gas-energy/mapping-and-data .") Almost all the OCS oil and gas infrastructure is located in the Gulf of America region, which accounts for 99% of U.S. offshore oil and gas production.[25](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn25 "BOEM, 11th National Outer Continental Shelf Oil and Gas Leasing Draft Proposed Program: 1st Analysis and Proposal , November 2025, p. 3-18, https://www.boem.gov/sites/default/files/documents/oil-gas-energy/national-program/11th_National_OCS_Program_1stAnalysis_and_Proposal.pdf .") The Pacific OCS region hosts 23 oil and gas platforms, all lying off the coast of Southern California. These platforms were installed in the 1960s to 1980s, and some have ceased to produce oil and gas in paying quantities. As of January 2026, BSEE listed 8 of the 23 platforms as being "in various stages of decommissioning."[30](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn30 "BSEE, \"Pacific Region Federal OCS Decommissioning,\" https://www.bsee.gov/stats-facts/ocs-regions/pacific/  pacific-region-federal-ocs-decommissioning .")

Infrastructure decommissioning issues are less immediately relevant in the Atlantic and Alaska OCS regions than in the Gulf of America and Pacific regions. There are no extant oil and gas platforms in the Atlantic OCS region. The Beaufort Sea planning area of the Alaska region has one project, a joint unit in federal and state waters, that continues to produce oil and gas.[31](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn31 "B SEE, \"Northstar Unit,\" https://www.bsee.gov/stats-facts/offshore-information/northstar .") The other Alaska planning areas where federal leasing has taken place—Cook Inlet and Chukchi Sea—have no existing infrastructure in federal waters. (Alaska's Cook Inlet has oil and gas platforms in state waters.)

For facilities in the Gulf and Pacific regions, some observers have identified risks associated with delays in decommissioning idle infrastructure. In its 2024 report, GAO identified "potentially serious" safety and environmental risks (in addition to financial risks to the federal government from decommissioning liability).[32](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn32 "GAO, Interior Needs to Improve Decommissioning Enforcement , GAO-24-106229, pp. 8-11.") GAO stated that safety concerns on idled platforms could stem from platform deterioration, such as degraded walkways that could endanger personnel accessing platforms. Environmental risks could include increased risk of oil spills due to a loss of tank or pipeline integrity. Some recent offshore incidents, such as an April 2025 oil spill in state waters off Plaquemines Parish in Louisiana, have been associated with aging idle infrastructure.[33](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn33 "For more information on the April 2025 spill, which involved a blowout from a n 8 3 -year-old well that had ended production but had not been permanently plugged , see , for example, Eva Tesfaye , \" An 83- Y ear- O ld O il W ell I s L eaking off the C oast of Plaquemines Parish ,\" WWNO New Orleans Public Radio , May 2, 2025, https://www.wwno.org/  coastal-desk/2025-05-02/an-83-year-old-oil-well-is-leaking-off-the-coast-of-plaquemines-parish .")

Financial Responsibility for Decommissioning

DOI regulations, as well as individual oil and gas leases, generally assign financial and operational responsibility for decommissioning to oil and gas lessees (subject to agency approval of decommissioning applications and plans).[34](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn34 "30 C.F.R. §§250.1700-250.1702. Regarding decommissioning applications and plans, see 30 C.F.R. §250.1704. Other offshore parties that may have decommissioning responsibilities under the regulations include right-of-way grant holders, right-of-use and easement grant holders, and owners of operating rights (see footnote 4 ) .") Under the regulations, some of these responsibilities extend not only to the entity that holds the lease at the time it is terminated, but also to *predecessor* lessees that previously held the lease.[35](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn35 "BSEE regulations define a predecessor as \" a prior lessee or owner of operating rights, or a prior holder of a right-of-use and easement grant or a pipeline right-of-way grant, that is liable for accrued obligations on that lease or grant \" (30 C.F.R. §250.1700(d)). The regulations state (30 C.F.R. §250.1701(a)) that current lessees and their predecessors \"are jointly and severally liable for meeting decommissioning obligations for facilities on leases ... as the obligations accrue and...")

Financial Assurance

To help ensure that lessees fulfill their decommissioning responsibilities, DOI requires lessees to provide *financial assurance*, typically in the form of bonds, as part of their lease obligations. DOI regulations require lessees to provide financial assurance in specified amounts at several stages of the oil and gas leasing and development process, including before receiving a lease, before commencing lease exploration, and before engaging in development and production activities.[36](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn36 "30 C.F.R. §§556.900-556.901. There also are bonding requirements for holders of rights of use and easement (30 C.F.R. §550.166) and pipeline rights-of-way (30 C.F.R. §550.1011(a)) on the OCS .") Beyond the base amounts required at each stage, DOI officials may determine that *supplemental* financial assurance is necessary to ensure compliance with lease obligations, including decommissioning obligations.[37](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn37 "30 C.F.R. §556.901(d).") (If the lessee satisfactorily performs the lease obligations, including decommissioning, then DOI cancels the bond.)[38](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn38 "30 C.F.R. §556.906.")

The base and supplemental financial assurance that DOI requires from lessees has sometimes proven insufficient to cover all decommissioning costs when leases terminate. This has left the federal government with responsibility for decommissioning orphaned infrastructure when operators have suffered bankruptcies and predecessor lessees are not available to decommission. As of October 2026, DOI reported that the government was carrying $129.9 million in uncovered liability for existing orphaned facilities, after applying the base and supplemental financial assurance provided by lessees for these facilities.[39](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn39 "BOEM, \"Orphaned Liability,\" https://www.boem.gov/oil-gas-energy/risk-management/property-list-and-orphaned-liability .") In recent years, BSEE has received about $3.0 million in annual appropriations for an offshore decommissioning fund to cover some of its costs for decommissioning such orphaned infrastructure (see the section on "[Federal Appropriations for Decommissioning](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#_Ref235626198)" for further discussion). In terms of potential future exposure, the 2024 GAO report found that, as of June 2023, DOI held about $3.5 billion in total supplemental bonds to cover $40 billion-$70 billion in total estimated decommissioning costs for all wells and platforms on the OCS, meaning that the government would face at least $36.5 billion in total uncovered liabilities if all operators failed to meet their decommissioning responsibilities.[40](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn40 "GAO, Interior Needs to Improve Decommissioning Enforcement , GAO-24-106229, p. 26 .") (Not all of this potential future exposure would be realized, as the liability would only fall to the federal government if the current and predecessor lessees did not perform their decommissioning responsibilities.)

Other Potential Decommissioning Enforcement Mechanisms

DOI's regulations include other steps, beyond using lessees' financial assurance, that DOI could take to enforce decommissioning obligations against lessees and their predecessors. For instance, BSEE officials could issue a *notice of incident of noncompliance* to offshore lessees and/or predecessor lessees who fail to decommission, could apply civil penalties to lessees and/or their predecessors, or could disqualify them as OCS operators.[54](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn54 "30 C.F.R. Part 250, Subpart N; §§250.135; and §250.1708.") In its 2024 report, GAO found that such enforcement tools "have not effectively compelled operator compliance."[55](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn55 "GAO, Interior Needs to Improve Decommissioning Enforcement , GAO-24-106229, p. 12.") GAO stated that DOI's process for issuing citations can extend for years and that the citations and notices were "unlikely to motivate operators."[56](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn56 "GAO, Interior Needs to Improve Decommissioning Enforcement , GAO-24-106229, p. 16.") GAO also found that DOI rarely takes further punitive action, such as assessing penalties or disqualifying operators.[57](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn57 "GAO, Interior Needs to Improve Decommissioning Enforcement , GAO-24-106229, pp. 17-18.") Additionally, BSEE's data systems do not "readily track" information about predecessor lessees, which can complicate the agency's ability to enforce obligations against these parties.[58](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn58 "GAO, Interior Needs to Improve Decommissioning Enforcement , GAO-24-106229, p. 16.")

Decommissioning in Place

Separately from issues related to financial assurance, Congress and DOI have considered the type and extent of decommissioning that lessees must perform. In particular, some DOI authorities allow lessees to leave certain end-of-life oil and gas infrastructure on the seafloor to be used for other purposes, sometimes known as *decommissioning in place*. Under its regulations, DOI may approve partial platform removal or toppling in place so that infrastructure can be used in a state artificial reefing program, known as a Rigs-to-Reefs program.[59](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn59 "30 C.F.R. §250.1730.") Offshore oil and gas infrastructure also could potentially be reused in other ways, such as for offshore wind energy development, offshore aquaculture, or military or space launches, among other uses.[60](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn60 "BOEMRE, Assessment of Opportunities for Alternative Uses of Hydrocarbon Infrastructure , p.1.") Such alternative uses may depend on the location, water depth, condition of the structure, local environmental (including meteorological) conditions, and economic considerations. DOI generally would administer such alternative uses under an Alternate Use right-of-use and easement (RUE).[61](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn61 "30 C.F.R. Part 586.") Under DOI regulations, the holder of an Alternate Use RUE is responsible for decommissioning obligations related to the RUE, once that use terminates.[62](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn62 "30 C.F.R. §250.1731.")

Repurposing an offshore oil and gas platform could reduce decommissioning costs, and could potentially reduce the impacts of a platform's complete removal and the installation of new structures for other uses.[63](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn63 "MMS, \"Chapter 6: Alternative Uses of Existing Oil and Natural Gas Platforms on the OCS,\" in Final Programmatic Environmental Impact Statement for Alternative Energy Development and Production and Alternate Use of Facilities on the Outer Continental Shelf , OCS EIS/EA MMS 2007-046, 2007 (h ereinafter MMS, \"Chapter 6: Alternative Uses of Existing Oil and Natural Gas Platforms on the OCS\" ).") Fowler et al., \"Environmental Benefits of Leaving Offshore Infrastructure in the Ocean,\" Frontiers in Ecology and the Environment , vol. 16, no. 10 (2018), pp. 571-578 (hereinafter Fowler et al., \"Environmental Benefits of Leaving Offshore Infrastructure in the Ocean \" ).") The coauthors of the study, including a BOEM marine ecologist,[65](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn65 "BOEM, \" International Experts Recommend Flexibility i n Offshore Platform Removal Policies ,\" https://www.boem.gov/oil-gas-energy/leasing/international-experts-recommend-flexibility-offshore-platform-removal .") recommended allowing for partial removal of platforms based on environmental considerations and broadening those considerations to include the ecosystem services provided by the offshore structures, among other recommendations.[66](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn66 "Fowler et al., \"Environmental Benefits of Leaving Offshore Infrastructure in the Ocean,\" p. 577.") Other Alternatives for Offshore Infrastructure Reuse

Congress provided the Secretary of the Interior authority to regulate the use of offshore oil and gas structures for alternative marine and energy applications (Section 388 of the Energy Policy Act of 2005; [P.L. 109-58](https://www.congress.gov/law/109/pub/58)).[110](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn110 "43 U.S.C. § 1337(p). The Secretary is to exercise this responsibility in consultation with the Secretary of the d epartment in which the Coast Guard is operating and other relevant federal departments and agencies.") Some stakeholders assert that repurposing decommissioned offshore oil and gas platforms has the potential to turn an uneconomic platform into an asset for a new industry rather than a liability to the marine environment and other marine industries.[111](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn111 "BOEMRE, Assessment of Opportunities for Alternative Uses of Hydrocarbon Infrastructure , p. 1.")

Decommissioned offshore oil and gas platforms have been or could be reused for various purposes. For instance, in addition to fishing and diving uses discussed above (section on "Rigs-to-Reefs Program"), decommissioned platforms also could be used for offshore aquaculture for aquatic plants, shellfish, or finfish.[112](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn112 "NOAA, NMFS, \"Marine Aquaculture,\" https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/insight/marine-aquaculture . For more information about offshore aquaculture, see CRS Report R45952, U.S. Offshore Aquaculture Regulation and Development , by Anthony R. Marshak .") Under this scenario, high-value marine species would be raised in net cages or other contained structures close to or directly off a platform. Potential drawbacks could include escape of non-native species; introduction of diseases and parasites to wild populations; and impacts associated with the application of drugs and antibiotics.[113](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn113 "DOI, MMS, \"Chapter 6: Alternative Uses of Existing Oil and Natural Gas Platforms on the OCS,\" p. 6-9.")

Stakeholders also have proposed reusing decommissioned platforms for emerging offshore energy uses, including carbon capture and storage (CCS).[114](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn114 "For example, United Kingdom (UK), Oil & Gas Authority, UKCS Energy Integration: Interim Findings , 2019, p. 18 (h ereinafter UK, Oil & Gas Authority, UKCS Energy Integration: Interim Findings ) .") CCS involves technologies that capture human-made carbon dioxide from point sources, such as power plants, with the aim of permanently storing the carbon underground.[115](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn115 "Department of Energy, \"Carbon Capture, Utilization & Storage,\" https://www.energy.gov/carbon-capture-utilization-storage . For more information about carbon capture and storage, see CRS Report R44902, Carbon Capture and Sequestration (CCS) in the United States , by Angela C. Jones and Ashley J. Lawson.") In 2021, Congress amended the OCSLA to allow DOI to issue leases, easements, and rights-of-way for CCS on the outer continental shelf ([P.L. 117-58](https://www.congress.gov/law/117/pub/58), §§40307 and 40343).[116](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn116 "As of the date of this CRS report, DOI had not issued implementing regulations for CCS leasing. During the Biden Administration, DOI beg a n an assessment of carbon storage capacity on the OCS (BOEM, \"Carbon Storage,\" https://www.boem.gov/oil-gas-energy/resource-evaluation/carbon-storage ). Reportedly, some lessees have sought to convert existing oil and gas leases to carbon storage leases, but DOI has not permitted such a conversion. See, e.g., Carlos Anchondo and Heather Richards...") Stakeholders have noted that some of the infrastructure associated with decommissioned oil and gas sites could be used or adapted to store carbon dioxide underground.[117](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn117 "Scarlett Evans, \"Seven Surprising Ways to Repurpose Offshore Oil and Gas Infrastructure,\" Offshore Technology , August 19, 2025, https://www.offshore-technology.com/features/seven-surprising-ways-to-repurposing-offshore-oil-and-gas-infrastructure (h ereinafter Evans, \"Seven Surprising Ways to Repurpose Offshore Oil and Gas Infrastructure\" ).") In addition, some pipelines previously used to move petroleum products from the seabed to onshore could potentially be reversed to move carbon dioxide from onshore industrial sites to depleted oil and gas reservoirs in the seabed.[118](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn118 "Evans, \"Seven Surprising Ways to Repurpose Offshore Oil and Gas Infrastructure.\"") Some stakeholders assert that adapting CCS to the offshore environment could accelerate and scale a proven method for reducing net greenhouse gas emissions.[119](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn119 "See, e.g., Shell Global, \"Shell Offshore Carbon Storage,\" https://www.shell.com/what-we-do/carbon-capture-and-storage/shell-offshore-carbon-storage-nl/our-process.html .") Others contend that offshore CCS is insufficiently tested and would create risks of leakage, compromise reservoir pressure, and pose other environmental challenges.[120](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn120 "See, e.g., Center for International Environmental Law, Deep Trouble: The Risks of Offshore Carbon Capture and Storage , July 2024, https://www.ciel.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Deep-Trouble-The-Risks-of-Offshore-Carbon-Capture-and-Storage.pdf .")

Some governments and companies have launched space missions from the ocean as a means to avoid congested schedules at major land-based launch sites and to be closer to the equator (the Earth moves faster around its axis the further the distance from the poles, giving rockets a speed boost).[121](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn121 "Jeff Foust, \"Start Developing Sea-Based Launch Pads,\" SpaceNews , February 26, 2023, https://spacenews.com/  startup-developing-sea-based-launch-pads/ ; Becky Ferreira, \"The Dream of Offshore Rocket Launches Is Finally Blasting Off,\" MIT Technology Review , February 11, 2025, https://www.technologyreview.com/  2025/02/11/  1111467/the-dream-of-offshore-rocket-launches-is-finally-blasting-off (hereinafter Ferreira, \"The Dream of Offshore Rocket Launches\").") Launches from decommissioned oil and gas platforms would typically be limited to small rockets.[124](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn124 "Ferreira, \"The Dream of Offshore Rocket Launches.\"") Separate from proposals for space launches, in 2024, a report indicated that the U.S. Navy was supporting research into a concept that would repurpose decommissioned offshore oil and gas platforms into mobile missile defense and resupply bases.[125](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn125 "Aaron-Matthew Lariosa, \" Concept Turns Oil Rigs Into Mobile Missile Defense and Supply Bases ,\" Naval News , April 13, 2024, https://www.navalnews.com/event-news/sea-air-space-2024/2024/04/leidos-concept-turns-oil-rigs-into-mobile-missile-defense-and-supply-bases/ .") Some note that launches from the ocean can reduce the potential for impacts and accidents near human population centers.[126](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn126 "Ferreira, \"The Dream of Offshore Rocket Launches.\"") Others contend that rocket emissions and chemical pollution associated with launches may pose threats to the marine environment.[127](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn127 "Ferreira, \"The Dream of Offshore Rocket Launches.\"")

Issues and Legislation in the 119th Congress

Congress has debated issues and potential actions related to offshore oil and gas infrastructure decommissioning. Topics of interest have included decommissioning enforcement, offshore lessees' financial strength and bonding requirements, federal appropriations for decommissioning activities, and artificial reefing and the Rigs-to-Reefs program. Congress may continue to consider legislation regarding these topics and to conduct oversight of DOI's offshore decommissioning activities and policies.

Decommissioning Enforcement

Both GAO and the DOI Office of Inspector General have identified DOI's enforcement of offshore decommissioning regulations and deadlines as a management challenge for the federal government.[134](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn134 "Among recent documents, see GAO, Interior Needs to Improve Decommissioning Enforcement , GAO-24-106229 ; and DOI Office of Inspector General, Inspector General's Statement Summarizing the Major Management and Performance Challenges Facing the U.S. Department of the Interior, FY 2025 , January 23, 2026, pp. 9-10, https://www.doioig.gov/sites/default/  files/2021-migration/Inspector-Generals-Statement-Summarizing-the-Major-Management-and-Performance-Challenges-FY-2025.pdf . Both entities h...") As noted above (section on "[Other Potential Decommissioning Enforcement Mechanisms](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#_Ref235110936)"), GAO found in 2024 that DOI's tools for compelling operator compliance with decommissioning regulations and deadlines have generally not been effective. GAO recommended that Congress consider enacting oversight mechanisms "such as requiring annual reporting [by DOI] on the status of decommissioning enforcement efforts and associated liabilities."[135](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn135 "\"Recommendations,\" in GAO , Interior Needs to Improve Decommissioning Enforcement , GAO-24-106229, https://www.gao.  gov/  products/gao-24-106229#summary_recommend .") As of February 2026, GAO stated on its website that this recommendation to Congress remains "Open," as do related recommendations to DOI regarding steps the agencies could take to more proactively enforce regulations.[136](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn136 "\"Recommendations,\" in GAO, Interior Needs to Improve Decommissioning Enforcement , GAO-24-106229 , https://www.gao.  gov/  products/gao-24-106229#summary_recommend .") However, GAO noted on its website steps taken by BSEE in 2024 and 2025 to review its regulations and policies with the aim of ensuring more consistent decommissioning enforcement.[137](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn137 "\"Recommendations,\" in GAO, Interior Needs to Improve Decommissioning Enforcement , GAO-24-106229 , https://www.gao.  gov/  products/gao-24-106229#summary_recommend .")

Bills in the 119th Congress ([H.R. 8099](http://www.congress.gov/bill/119/hr/8099), [S. 4239](http://www.congress.gov/bill/119/s/4239)) would require the Secretary of the Interior to submit to Congress, and make public, an annual report on the numbers of offshore oil and gas wells, platforms, and pipelines for which decommissioning is overdue, the numbers of decommissioning applications received and approved by DOI, and the status of enforcement actions, including notices of incidents of noncompliance, civil penalties, and operator disqualifications, among other related data. In the past, legislators also have proposed other actions, such as imposing fees on idled wells as an incentive to operators to engage in timely decommissioning.[138](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn138 "See Section 71414 of the House-passed version of H.R. 5376 , 11 7 th Congress. This provision was not enacted into law.") In considering such proposals, legislators may weigh federal liabilities from delayed decommissioning, potential burdens to industry from proposed enforcement actions, agency staff capacity to administer and report on decommissioning compliance, and other factors.

Lessees' Financial Strength and Bonding Requirements

Several bills in the 119th Congress address lessees' financial strength and DOI's financial assurance requirements. As discussed above (sections on "[Financial Assurance](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#_Ref235544920)" and "[Changes to Financial Assurance Regulations](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#_Ref241064376)"), under the Biden Administration DOI promulgated new criteria for determining the financial assurance offshore operators must provide. The department projected the new criteria would increase the overall amount of financial assurance required from regulated operators. The Trump Administration has proposed revisions to repeal some of these changes and institute other criteria that are anticipated to shrink overall bond amounts required from operators. Bills in the 119th Congress would make additional changes that differ from both the Biden and Trump Administration rules; some of these changes were considered but not implemented by the Biden Administration.

[H.R. 9034](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119/hr/9034) and [S. 4715](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119/s/4715) would amend the OCSLA to establish "fitness-to-operate" standards for offshore lessees and holders of easements and rights-of-way. Prior to issuing, extending, or transferring a lease, easement, or right-of-way, DOI would be required to certify that the party seeking the authorization is fit to operate, based on the party's past compliance record (including with respect to decommissioning), its financial solvency, and other criteria. These bills also would require every offshore leaseholder to make payments to a decommissioning escrow account on a schedule established by DOI, with timing and amounts determined so that within five years the account balance would be sufficient to fully meet the estimated costs of decommissioning the infrastructure on the lease. (After completion of decommissioning, any remaining balances would be returned to the lessee.) Separately, [H.R. 9035](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119/hr/9035) would address the issue of infrastructure orphaned through operator bankruptcies by providing that payment of expenses related to "the complete cleanup of fossil fuel operations and retirement of fossil fuel assets" (for both onshore and offshore leases) would have priority in bankruptcy proceedings under specified terms.

Federal Appropriations for Decommissioning

Starting in FY2022, BSEE requested annual appropriations for a new Offshore Decommissioning budget activity to support its decommissioning work on orphaned infrastructure (i.e., cases where operator bankruptcies have left the federal government responsible for decommissioning infrastructure). As discussed above under "[Financial Assurance](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#_Ref235626382)," as of October 2026, DOI reported that the government was carrying $129.9 million in uncovered liability for existing orphaned facilities, after applying the base and supplemental financial assurance provided by lessees for these facilities.[139](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn139 "BOEM, \"Orphaned Liability,\" https://www.boem.gov/oil-gas-energy/risk-management/property-list-and-orphaned-liability .") BSEE requested $30.0 million for the Offshore Decommissioning budget activity in each of FY2022-FY2024, $12.0 million in FY2025, and no funding in each of FY2026 and FY2027. Congress provided $3.0 million for this activity in each of FY2022 and FY2023 and $2.9 million in each of FY2024-FY2026. During this time, some appropriations committee reports expressed "strong disagreement that the ultimate responsibility for these activities should fall to the American taxpayer," and expressed that the appropriated funding is to give the agency a "foundation" for contracting decommissioning work, but "once current identified decommissioning needs are provided for," this line item would be discontinued.[140](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49495.html#fn140 "H.Rept. 117-400 , p. 50; S.Rept. 119-46 , p. 59.") For FY2027, House committee-reported [H.R. 9171](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119/hr/9171) would again provide $2.9 million for this account. (As of October 2026, no full-year FY2027 Interior and Related Agencies appropriations bill had been introduced in the Senate.)
