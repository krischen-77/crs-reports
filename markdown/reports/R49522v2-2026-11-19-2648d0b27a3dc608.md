Buy America and the Department of Transportation: In Brief


Introduction

Congress over the years has passed several domestic content laws that aim to protect American
manufacturing and manufacturing jobs. Starting in 1978, Congress placed domestic content
restrictions on federally funded transportation projects carried out by state and local
governments.1 These restrictions are commonly referred to as the Buy America Act, or more
simply, Buy America; they differ from requirements under the Buy American Act of 1933, which
governs purchases made directly by the federal government.2 Buy America generally required
that iron, steel, and manufactured products used in transportation projects be produced in the
United States. In the 2010s, Congress passed certain Buy America requirements on federally
supported drinking water and wastewater infrastructure projects.3

In 2021, Congress passed the Build America, Buy America Act (BABA) as Title IX, Subtitle A,
of the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (IIJA; P.L. 117-58). BABA expanded Buy America
requirements to all federally assisted infrastructure projects, beyond those in the transportation
and water sectors. BABA also expanded the domestic preference requirements for iron, steel, and
manufacturing products to include other construction materials.4 Some of these changes affected
Buy America requirements for federally assisted transportation projects.

This report discusses changes to Buy America requirements resulting from BABA, Buy America
as it applies to transportation projects, as well as other domestic content restrictions that apply to
transportation. The report also discusses the effects of Buy America on manufacturing industry
and employment, and transportation infrastructure. It concludes with a discussion of policy
options and proposed Buy America legislation relevant to transportation introduced in the 119th
Congress.

Build America, Buy America Act

Buy America requirements traditionally extended to federally assisted transportation and water-
related infrastructure. BABA expanded Buy America coverage to other federally assisted
infrastructure projects, including electrical transmission facilities and systems; utilities;
broadband infrastructure; buildings and real property; and structures, facilities, and equipment
that generate, transport, and distribute energy, including electric vehicle (EV) charging.5

Prior to BABA, Buy America requirements applied to iron, steel, and manufactured goods.
BABA broadened coverage to include nonferrous metals, such as copper used in electric wiring;
plastic- and polymer-based products; glass, including optical fiber; and certain other construction
materials, such as lumber, engineered wood, and drywall. Congress excluded cement and
aggregates—comprising sand, gravel, and crushed stone—from the BABA’s list of construction
materials subject to Buy America. Industry groups asserted that transporting these extremely
heavy materials from distant U.S. producers rather than closer Canadian or Mexican producers
would be costly and might have other adverse effects, including more damage to roads and
highways.7

Buy America requires iron and steel, manufactured products, and construction materials (with
exceptions) incorporated into a federally assisted infrastructure project to be “produced in the
United States.” For manufacturing products and construction materials, “produced in the United
States” means that all manufacturing processes occurred domestically. For iron and steel, this
means that all manufacturing processes from the initial melting stage through the application of
coatings occurred domestically. Manufacturing process standards for different construction
materials are provided at Title 2, Section 184.6, of the Code of Federal Regulations. For example,
for glass to be considered produced in the United States, “all manufacturing processes, from
initial batching and melting of raw materials through annealing, cooling, and cutting, occurred in
the United States.” For a manufactured good to be “produced in the United States” under BABA,
the item must contain greater than 55% domestic content, by cost of the components, and be
manufactured in the United States.

Buy America Waivers

The Buy America statutes permit a regulating agency to waive the Buy America provisions in
certain situations, including when Buy America requirements would be inconsistent with the
public interest, when products and materials are not domestically available in sufficient quantities
or of satisfactory quality, and when the domestic products/materials raise the cost of the project
by more than 25%.8 Prior to a waiver being granted, the issuing agency must make the proposed
waiver publicly available on a website designated by the Office of Management and Budget
(OMB) and must afford the public a specified amount of time to comment.

Waivers can apply to a specific project or generally. Prior to IIJA, a general applicability waiver
could be provided in perpetuity. Provisions enacted in BABA require that general applicability
waivers be reviewed every five years to determine if a waiver should be continued. Additionally,

Buy America Requirements in Transportation

BABA required federal agencies, including DOT, to identify deficiencies in preexisting Buy
America requirements and general applicability waivers that were not consistent with its
provisions. In some situations, preexisting Buy America laws in transportation were more
stringent than BABA requirements and, thus, were already consistent with BABA. For example,
BABA generally requires a manufactured product to be manufactured in the United States, with
the cost of the components mined, produced, or manufactured in the United States exceeding 55%
of the total cost of all components. However, Federal Transit Administration (FTA) Buy America
requirements for rolling stock (e.g., rail cars and buses) require final assembly of the rolling stock
in the United States and the cost of the components produced in the United States to be greater
than 70% of the total cost (49 U.S.C. §5323(j) and 49 C.F.R. §661).

In some instances, DOT agencies had general applicability waivers that were inconsistent with
BABA. Consequently, these agencies have promulgated rulemaking to update the administration
of Buy America requirements. For example, in 1983, FHWA waived the application of Buy
America requirements to manufactured products other than those manufactured predominantly of
iron and steel.10 Through a new rulemaking process following enactment of BABA, this
“Manufactured Products General Waiver” was terminated on March 17, 2025.11 The new rule
stipulates that any manufactured product permanently incorporated into a federal-aid highway
project must be manufactured in the United States, with the cost of the components mined,
produced, or manufactured in the United States exceeding 55% of the total cost of all
components.

Effects of Buy America on Manufacturing Industry
and Employment

Economic research on domestic preference requirements, although not necessarily on Buy
America, generally concludes that such preferences enhance production and employment in
affected industries, but that these effects may come at the expense of production and employment
in other industries and the overall economy.30 These broader effects stem mainly from the higher
prices and slower technological innovation of the protected sectors. For example, using a
macroeconomic model, researchers funded by the Canadian government concluded that
eliminating Buy America requirements would result in 57,000 fewer U.S. manufacturing jobs,
including about 1,600 fewer jobs in the United States in iron and steel production. The
researchers also estimated that the U.S. economy overall would gain more than 300,000 jobs if
Buy America were terminated.31 This estimated gain is equal to approximately 0.2% of payroll
employment in the United States.

Assessing the specific industrial effects of Buy America requirements on transportation projects is
difficult due to the lack of data.32 For example, it is unclear how much iron and steel are used in
transportation projects that receive federal funding; hence, data are not available to calculate how
much steel is produced and sold domestically as a direct result of Buy America. The available
data suggest that the steel produced for the Buy America market represents a small portion of
total domestic demand for steel and, consequently, a small share of jobs.33

Effects of Buy America on Transportation Infrastructure

Project Benefits and Costs

The industrial effects of Buy America are accompanied by effects on transportation infrastructure
through the projects that must comply with Buy America law. Existing research on this topic
recognizes that there are likely to be higher costs associated with federal requirements, but the
magnitude of these effects is uncertain. As with the industrial effects, many other factors affect
the cost of transportation projects, including broader market conditions for raw materials and
components and other U.S. government policies that affect the price of goods. For example, U.S.
government tariffs on imported steel and aluminum products increase the prices of imported
products and domestically produced products that compete with affected imports or use them as
inputs.

In the regulatory analysis associated with FHWA’s review of its long-standing Buy American
manufactured products waiver, the agency attempted to estimate the costs and benefits of
rescinding the waiver and instituting a 55% domestic content requirement consistent with
BABA.36 FHWA could not quantify the identified benefits: enhancing domestic manufacturing,
increasing supply chain resiliency, and increasing consistency in applying Buy America
requirements among federal agencies. Of the costs it identified—materials, administrative costs,
project delay, and negative secondary impacts—FHWA could quantify only materials costs and
administrative costs (both federal and state). The materials costs estimate, moreover, was
uncertain. FHWA estimated total costs over 10 years (FY2026-FY2035) of eliminating the
waiver, discounted at 2%, to be between $545 million and $8.5 billion in 2021 dollars. This
amount included between $361 million and $8.3 billion in materials costs and $184 million in
administrative costs.

Along with other factors, Buy America may raise the cost of railcar and bus procurements.37 One
contributing factor is the relatively small size of the U.S. market compared with markets in other
countries and regions; consequently, domestic producers do not benefit from economies of scale.
According to one estimate, in 2023, bus sales (of all types and uses) in the United States were
about 27% of those in China.38 Studies have noted that transit buses cost more in the United
States than in other countries. An analysis of transit bus procurement in 2015 noted that buses in
the United States were about twice the price of those in Japan and South Korea. The study also
asserted that U.S. bus purchasers are limited in their choice of buses, and that the domestic
industry is less innovative than that of other countries. The authors concluded that if public transit
agencies could import buses, “they would have access to a greater menu of differentiated
products at lower prices. This would lead to a higher quality of service provision (e.g., better

Project Delivery

Another concern for transportation projects is the effect of Buy America on project delivery and
delay. Project sponsors, such as state departments of transportation and transit agencies, must
ensure, when necessary, that product suppliers are Buy America compliant, which can take time
and effort. In certain situations, such as when a Buy America product is not available, project
sponsors can apply for a Buy America waiver. This too can be a time-consuming endeavor with
an uncertain outcome.

Transportation project sponsors have voiced concerns about the waiver process and project delay.
For example, in a 2025 letter to FHWA on BABA and the waiver process for manufactured
products, the American Association of State Highway and Transportation Officials (AASHTO)
stated that without a consistent and clearly understood waiver framework and guidance, the
“states’ ability to carry out the Federal-aid Highway Program will be impacted by delays to
project benefits such as improved safety while increasing costs attributable to this uncertainty.”42

timeframe for resolving waiver requests has resulted in uncertainty and project delays. TxDOT

Policy Options and Proposed Legislation in the
119th Congress

As Congress discusses reauthorization of surface transportation programs, lawmakers are
considering several legislative proposals related to domestic content requirement for
transportation projects. Many of these relate to the implementation of the current Buy America
regime and not to the Buy America requirements themselves. Changes to the requirements
themselves, such as increasing or reducing the threshold for domestic content to be considered
“produced in the United States,” are an option Congress could consider. For example, AASHTO
recommends that Congress create a new pilot program that requires Buy America certification
when buses are evaluated with federal requirements, sometimes referred to as “Altoona testing”
because of the location of the testing center in Altoona, PA.44 Others have suggested exempting
foreign bus manufacturers from Buy America for selling up to 100 buses before they have to
commit to manufacturing in the United States according to existing Buy America law.45

On May 19, 2026, a surface transportation reauthorization bill—the Building Unrivaled
Infrastructure and Long-term Development for America’s 250th Act (BUILD America 250 Act)—
was introduced in the House (H.R. 8870) by the chair of the House Committee on Transportation
and Infrastructure. The bill was marked up in the committee and ordered to be reported on May
22, 2026.

Section 1328 of the BUILD America 250 Act would require the Secretary of Transportation to
study the feasibility of requiring yellow paint (including pigment used to produce water-based
paint) for road and highway surface markings to be manufactured domestically. S. 3819/H.R.
3933 (119th Congress) would add “all yellow organic pigments used in water-based paints for
road, highway, or airport surface markings shall be considered to be construction materials” to the
list of construction materials and define “produced in the United States” as “all manufacturing
processes from initial combination of materials through the chemical reaction resulting in the
synthesized pigment, where chemical reaction is a process (including a biochemical process) that
results in a molecule with a new structure by breaking intramolecular bonds and by forming new
intramolecular bonds, or by altering the spatial arrangement of atoms in a molecule, occurred in
the United States.”
