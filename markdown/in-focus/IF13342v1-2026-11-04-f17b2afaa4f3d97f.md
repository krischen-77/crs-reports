Types of Coins Produced by the U.S. Mint

November 4, 2026
(IF13342)

American coins are produced by the [U.S. Mint](https://www.usmint.gov/). The U.S. Mint was established by the Coinage Act of 1792 ([1 Stat. 246](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-1/pdf/STATUTE-1-Pg246-3.pdf#page=1)) to produce coins. This In Focus discusses types of coins produced by the U.S. Mint, a bureau of the Department of the Treasury. These include circulating coins, bullion coins, and numismatic coins.

Circulating Coins

The U.S. Mint strikes [*circulating coins*](https://www.usmint.gov/learn/coins-and-medals/circulating-coins)—coins produced for everyday transactions. Most design aspects of circulating coins are required by law. Pursuant to statute ([31 U.S.C. §5112](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/t31/s5112/)), Congress instructs the Secretary of the Treasury, through the U.S. Mint, to issue specific denominations of circulating coins and specifies the coins' size, weight, and metallic content. For example, the U.S. Mint may issue "a quarter dollar that is 0.955 inch in diameter and weights 5.67 grams" and that is made of a copper and nickel alloy (31 U.S.C. §5112(a)(3) and (b)).

In addition to specific denominations, size, and metallic content, Congress also requires that specified design elements be present on certain coinage. For example, this includes the inscriptions "In God We Trust" and "Liberty" on a coin's obverse, and "United States of America" and "E Pluribus Unum" on a coin's reverse ((31 U.S.C. §5112(d)(1)). Additionally, for some coins, the law specifies the obverse's image—for example, Thomas Jefferson on the nickel's obverse.

For other aspects of coin design not specified by Congress, the Secretary of the Treasury is authorized to change the design or die of a coin only once within 25 years of the first adoption of the design features for that coin (31 U.S.C. §5112(d)(2)). Bullion Coins

The U.S. Mint produces [*bullion coins*](https://www.usmint.gov/coin-programs/bullion-coins) as "[investment-grade](https://www.usmint.gov/coin-programs/bullion-coins/#:~:text=What%20Is%20a%20Bullion%20Coin%3F)" products purchased by "investors seeking a simple and tangible means to own and invest in the gold, silver, platinum, and palladium markets." Bullion coins are not sold directly to the public. The U.S. Mint sells bullion coins to a "[network of official distributors](https://www.usmint.gov/coin-programs/bullion-coins/#:~:text=How%20to%20Buy%20Bullion%20Coins) called '[authorized purchasers](https://www.usmint.gov/coins-precious-metal-coins/bullion-authorized-purchasers/),'" who sell the bullion coins to the public.

Bullion coins can be struck in gold, silver, platinum, and palladium. Recent bullion coins all include an eagle on the reverse. On the obverse, the 2026 gold and silver bullion coins feature an image of Liberty. In 2025, the U.S. Mint produced platinum and palladium coins. The 2025 platinum coin featured the First Amendment's "Right to Petition," with an image of an oak tree on the obverse and an eagle on the reverse. The palladium coin featured Liberty on the obverse and an eagle on the reverse. Numismatic Coins

The U.S. Mint also produces *numismatic coins* designed for collectors. Numismatic coins are sometimes minted to commemorate a historical occurrence or event and are sometimes made of [precious metals](https://www.usmint.gov/coins/precious-metal-coins/)*.*

Numismatic Coins Specifically Authorized by Congress

Numismatic coins specifically authorized by Congress generally include two types of coins: commemorative coins and other congressionally authorized coins.

**Commemorative Coins**

Congress authorizes [c*ommemorative coins*](https://www.usmint.gov/learn/coins-and-medals/commemorative-coins) to celebrate and honor American people, places, events, and institutions. Considered legal tender, commemorative coins are not minted for general circulation. Instead, they are designed to be collected and to assist groups designated by statute ("statutorily designated groups") raise money to support the group's activities through authorized surcharges added to the coin's cost.

Commemorative coin authorization legislation generally specifies certain aspects of the coins, which have included requirements for inscriptions, metallic content, and design. For example, the 2026 [commemorative coins for the FIFA World Cup](https://www.congress.gov/118/plaws/publ143/PLAW-118publ143.pdf) were required to list the year (2026) and "inscriptions of the words 'Liberty,' 'In God We Trust,' 'United States of America,' and 'E Pluribus Unum.'" **Other Numismatic Coins**

In addition to commemorative coins, Congress has directly authorized other numismatic coins. These include authorizations for specific coins (the [Buffalo $50 coin](https://www.usmint.gov/american-buffalo-2026-one-ounce-gold-proof-coin-26EL.html) (31 U.S.C. §5112(q)) and the reissuance of the [Morgan Dollar and Peace Dollar](https://www.usmint.gov/coins/coin-programs/morgan-and-peace-silver-dollar-coins/) coins ([P.L. 116-286](http://www.congress.gov/law/116/pub/286))); and the authorization to redesign a coin, but without specific design instructions (the [semiquincentennial $1 coin](https://www.usmint.gov/semiquincentennial-president-donald-j-trump-2026-1-coin-rolls-bags-MASTER_SEMIQDJT.html) for 2026 that featured President Donald J. Trump on the obverse (31 U.S.C. §5112(y)(1)(C)).

Additionally, Congress has also authorized the Mint to issue silver versions of circulating coins (31 U.S.C. §5132(a)(2)).

Numismatic Precious Metal Coins for Which Congress Has Not Directly Specified a Design

The U.S. Mint also produces numismatic precious metal coins for which Congress has not directly specified a design. For example, in 2020, the U.S. Mint issued gold $10 coins to honor the 400thanniversary of the Mayflower (see [**Figure 4**](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IF/HTML/IF13342.html#_Ref233283417)).
