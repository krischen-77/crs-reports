U.S.-Vietnam Relations

Updated November 23, 2026
(IF10209)

Since the establishment of diplomatic relations between the United States and the Socialist Republic of Vietnam in 1995, overlapping strategic and economic interests have led the two countries to expand ties across a wide spectrum of issues. Particularly after 2010, shared concerns about the People's Republic of China's (PRC's, or China's) increased assertiveness have led to deeper ties. In 2023, the United States and Vietnam elevated their relationship to a "comprehensive strategic partnership," and the second Trump Administration has continued bilateral security cooperation programs. In 2025, the United States was Vietnam's second-largest trading partner (after China), and Vietnam was the United States' seventh-largest goods trading partner. The Trump Administration has imposed unilateral tariffs on Vietnam, and, in October 2025, the U.S. and Vietnamese governments announced a framework for a bilateral trade agreement. In a February 2026 visit to Washington, DC, Vietnam's top leader, To Lam, met with President Donald Trump and attended the inaugural meeting of the U.S.-led Board of Peace. Trump reportedly has invited Lam to attend the December 2026 Group of 20 (G-20) summit meeting in Miami. Lam has invited Trump to attend a regional summit Vietnam is hosting in late 2027.

For decades, Congress has played a significant role in charting the course of U.S.-Vietnam relations. In particular, some Members have been at the forefront of efforts to improve relations, highlight human rights conditions, and address unresolved issues from the Vietnam War. If reports of Vietnam's interest in purchasing U.S. military aircraft become reality, Congress would have the opportunity to review the sale. Additionally, Congress may monitor and conduct oversight of U.S. tariffs on Vietnam and the October 2025 framework trade agreement.

The pace and extent of further improvement in U.S.-Vietnam relations is limited by several factors. First, Vietnam and China share interests on many issues, and Hanoi usually does not undertake large-scale diplomatic moves—especially with the United States—without first calculating China's likely reaction. Second, although opinion polls show the Vietnamese public holds positive views of the United States, many Vietnamese officials suspect that the United States' goal is to end the Communist Party of Vietnam's (CPV's) monopoly on power. Third, U.S. concerns about Vietnam's human rights record, which has deteriorated over the past decade, at times has limited the types of cooperation the U.S. government has been willing to undertake, particularly in the security sector. Fourth, the second Trump Administration's unilateral tariffs against Vietnam and cessation of some bilateral aid programs, as well as Vietnamese perceptions that the Administration's foreign policy decisionmaking can be unpredictable, reportedly are increasing Vietnamese leaders' questions about U.S. reliability.

Vietnam's Political Structure and Policy Direction

Vietnam is a one-party, authoritarian state ruled by the CPV, which sets the country's general policy direction, leaving most day-to-day implementation to the government. The top leadership post is the CPV General Secretary, which since 2024 has been held by To Lam (born 1957).

Every five years, the CPV holds a Party Congress to determine personnel positions and set the direction for Vietnam's economic, diplomatic, and social policies. At the January 2026 14th Party Congress, Lam was reelected as General Secretary (after securing an exemption from the mandatory retirement age of 65) and tapped as the country's President, upending Vietnam's four-decade-old practice of separating the two positions. Under Lam, Vietnam has enacted political and administrative reforms he says are necessary to achieve the CPV's goals of Vietnam becoming an upper middle-income economy by 2030 and a high-income developed country by 2045.

For over a decade, Vietnam's leaders have tried to meet these goals by diversifying the country's export markets and sources of foreign direct investment (FDI). On foreign policy, Vietnam's approach has included boosting its defense capabilities and expanding its security relationships with the United States, Australia, India, and Japan as a hedge against China's expanding power. A stated principle of Vietnam's foreign policy is avoiding an over-dependence on any one country or group of countries. This pursuit of balance, combined with a wariness of alarming China, has resulted in Vietnam expanding relations with the United States incrementally and in a nonlinear fashion.

China-Vietnam Relations

China is Vietnam's most important bilateral partner. The two countries have Communist Party-led political systems that provide a party-to-party communication channel and engender similar official worldviews. China is Vietnam's largest trading partner, and since 2024—including during Lam's April 2026 visit to Beijing—the two countries have signed dozens of economic agreements that, among other steps, would upgrade transportation links. Tensions, however, can flare over the two countries' competing claims in the South China Sea and over PRC dams on the Upper Mekong River. The two countries fought a short but intense border war in 1979, which also informs Vietnam's cautious approach to China. Since the mid-2010s, to help Vietnam improve its maritime capabilities, the United States has provided Vietnam with 24 new coast guard patrol vessels, unmanned aircraft systems, coastal radar, and three decommissioned U.S. Coast Guard cutters, Vietnam's largest coast guard ships. In the 119th Congress, [S. 4600](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119/s/4600), the South China Sea Strategy Act of 2026, would require the State Department to develop a South China Sea diplomatic engagement strategy, to include engagement with Vietnam.

Human Rights

For the past three decades, the CPV has permitted some forms of personal and religious expression while selectively repressing individuals and organizations that it deems a threat to the party's monopoly on power. Over the past decade, repression against dissenters has worsened, and the government has increased its legal and technological capacity to monitor Vietnamese citizens' social media activity. In the 119th Congress, the proposed Vietnam Human Rights Act ([H.R. 3122](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119/hr/3122)) would call on the President to impose sanctions on Vietnamese officials found to be involved in various human rights abuses.

Economics and Trade

Over the past decade, Vietnam has emerged as a major manufacturing center and has become one of the United States' top 10 trading partners. In 2025, total bilateral trade in goods was $209.4 billion, up 40% from 2024. U.S. imports from Vietnam were $193.8 billion (+42%) and U.S. exports to Vietnam were $15.6 billion (+19%). Vietnam is a major U.S. source for consumer electronics, furniture, apparel, and footwear imports. Top U.S. goods exports to Vietnam are semiconductors, cotton, telephone sets, nuts, and soybeans. U.S. services exports to Vietnam were $3.5 billion and U.S. services imports were $2.4 billion in 2025.

As bilateral trade flows have increased, U.S. business interest in Vietnam has grown, fueled by Vietnam's relatively low wages, market-oriented economic reforms, and political stability. Rising production costs in China, U.S. tariffs on PRC products, private sector efforts to diversify supply chains, and the conclusion of regional trade agreements between Vietnam and key trading partners in East Asia have increased Vietnam's attractiveness to foreign investors, including PRC companies, especially in the consumer electronics and semiconductor industries. In 2025, U.S. FDI stock in Vietnam was $4.9 billion, up 24% from 2024. The U.S. government has identified several barriers to trade in Vietnam, including relatively high Vietnamese tariffs on agricultural products, lack of regulatory transparency, inadequate protection of intellectual property rights (IPR), and digital trade issues.

Since April 2025, the Trump Administration has imposed tariffs on Vietnamese goods under various tariff authorities in part to address certain bilateral trade issues. Most recently, in July 2026, the Trump Administration imposed a 12.5% tariff on most goods from Vietnam under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 following an investigation conducted by the U.S. Trade Representative (USTR) that determined Vietnam failed to "impose and effectively enforce a prohibition" on imports produced with forced labor. Vietnam is also subject to two other ongoing Section 301 investigations related to structural excess capacity and IPR policies that may result in additional tariffs. In November 2026, USTR extended the timeline for a final determination in the investigation into Vietnam's IPR policies to February 2027. The Trump Administration has also imposed sectoral tariffs under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, including on U.S. imports of timber and lumber products (e.g., furniture), which are a major Vietnamese export.

In October 2025, the United States and Vietnam issued a [joint statement](https://www.whitehouse.gov/briefings-statements/2025/10/joint-statement-on-united-states-vietnam-framework-for-an-agreement-on-reciprocal-fair-and-balanced-trade/) on a framework for an Agreement on Reciprocal, Fair, and Balanced Trade, prompted by the Trump Administration's various tariff actions. The joint statement stated a final agreement would address U.S. exporters' access to Vietnam's market; Vietnam's nontariff barriers; commitments on digital trade, services, and investment; cooperation on supply chain resiliency, including on "duty evasion" and export controls; and more. The joint statement does not address current and potential Section 232 tariffs that affect Vietnamese industries such as timber, semiconductors, and critical minerals. To date, the U.S. and Vietnamese governments have not finalized an agreement under the framework. It is unclear whether a final agreement will address Section 301 and Section 232 tariffs on Vietnamese goods.

U.S. Foreign Assistance to Vietnam

From FY2023 to FY2025, Congress appropriated around $200 million annually for aid to Vietnam. This fell to under $85 million for FY2026, following the Trump Administration's foreign aid cuts, which halted most health, climate, and technology programs in Vietnam. For FY2027, the Administration has not publicly released comprehensive country-specific information about its request for foreign aid to Vietnam. Congress historically has championed the creation of many Vietnam War reconciliation programs, which arguably have enabled the deepening of overall U.S.-Vietnam relations. The Trump Administration generally has continued these programs. Since 2007, Congress has appropriated over $500 million for remediating the damage and related health problems resulting from the U.S. military's use of Agent Orange and its accompanying dioxin defoliant over Vietnam between 1961 and 1971. In 2020, the U.S. and Vietnamese governments developed a 10-year dioxin remediation plan for the Bien Hoa Airbase, near Ho Chi Minh City, with an estimated total cost of up to $450 million. Since FY2021, Congress has authorized the annual transfer of up to $15 million from the Defense (War) Department to the State Department for use in the Bien Hoa cleanup. The Senate Armed Services Committee-approved version of the FY2027 National Defense Authorization Act ([S. 4784](http://www.congress.gov/bill/119/s/4784), §1243), would continue this practice.
