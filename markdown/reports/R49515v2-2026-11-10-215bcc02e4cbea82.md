Members of Congress have expressed concerns relating to national security about investments in
U.S. agricultural land by foreign adversaries, according to 15 C.F.R. §791.4 (e.g., the People’s
Republic of China), particularly investments near sensitive military sites. These concerns would
be addressed by various bills introduced in the 119th Congress, some of which would amend the

USDA reports that as of December 31, 2024, foreign persons held interests in over 47 million acres of U.S. agricultural land,

Current federal law imposes no restrictions on the amount of private U.S. agricultural land that can be foreign-owned.
However, AFIDA requires foreign persons who acquire or transfer interest in agricultural land to submit a report to USDA
within 90 days of the transaction. Persons who fail to report may be subject to a civil penalty.

A 2024 Government Accountability Office report identified gaps among AFIDA data collection and information sharing with
the interagency Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States (CFIUS). The report was initiated by some Members
identifying foreign investments near U.S. military bases and purchases that could lead to foreign control of supply chains. In
June 2026, USDA released a proposed rule to amend the AFIDA regulations. The rule proposes changes to define
agricultural land to include land used for animal slaughtering and processing and solar and wind electric power generation; a
requirement to include digital, open-source geospatial map and property boundaries and a legal description of the land in
reporting; and amendments to civil penalties based on transaction and whether the foreign person is associated with a foreign
adversary.

In the 119th Congress, some Members have introduced legislation that would amend AFIDA, increase and improve data
collection on foreign ownership, or restrict foreign ownership associated with foreign adversaries. Two of these proposals are
the House-passed farm bill, the Farm, Food and National Security Act of 2026 (H.R. 7567), and the Senate committee-
reported Agricultural Act of 2026 (S. 5526). The House-passed bill would amend AFIDA disclosure requirements, amend
civil penalties for AFIDA violations, restrict certain foreign ownership of agricultural land, and alter the relationship between
USDA and CFIUS. S. 5526 proposes amendments to AFIDA reporting requirements and the civil penalty structure.

Many states and localities have introduced proposals or enacted laws to restrict foreign ownership of agricultural land or
require reporting similar to what is required under AFIDA, but there is no uniform approach across such legislation. For
example, some local laws restrict the amount of land that can be owned by foreign persons or the duration of ownership, and
some differ in their definition of agricultural land.

Congress, the executive branch, and state and local governments continue to deliberate foreign ownership of U.S. agricultural
land and whether to amend related laws. Congress may consider whether issues or concerns relating to foreign ownership
may require amendments to AFIDA or changes in interagency collaboration between USDA and CFIUS.

Introduction

Foreign investment and ownership of U.S. agricultural land have increased over time, according
to data collected by the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA). USDA reports that since 2017,
foreign holdings have increased an average of “over 2.4 million acres annually, ranging from 1.3
million acres to nearly 3.4 million acres per year.”1 Some Members of Congress have identified
the potential for investment in U.S. agricultural land by foreign adversaries, such as the People’s
Republic of China (China).2 These potential investments include Chinese nationals’ investments
in agricultural land nearby sensitive U.S. government sites, such as the 2023 proposed acquisition
of a corn milling facility near Grand Forks Air Force Base in North Dakota.3 Other issues
identified by some Members include the impact of foreign investment in agricultural land on U.S.
farms and rural communities and the domestic food supply.4

Some Members have introduced legislative proposals to amend the reporting requirements for
foreign investment transactions involving U.S. agricultural land under the Agricultural Foreign
Investment Disclosure Act of 1978 (AFIDA; P.L. 95-460; 7 U.S.C. §§3501 et seq.) and to restrict
ownership of U.S. agricultural land by foreign adversaries. No federal laws prohibit foreign
persons from acquiring U.S. agricultural land, but AFIDA requires foreign persons report to
USDA their involvement in most agricultural land transactions within 90 days of the transaction.
Legislative proposals such as the House-passed farm bill, the Food, Farm, and National Security
Act of 2026 (H.R. 7567), and the Senate committee-reported Agricultural Act of 2026 (S. 5526)
include amendments to AFIDA and restrictions on foreign ownership of U.S. agricultural land.5

USDA Data on Foreign Ownership

USDA reports that foreign persons held interests in over 47 million acres of U.S. agricultural land
as of December 31, 2024, which accounted for 3.6% of all privately owned agricultural land and
2% of all land in the United States (Table 1).7 USDA data on foreign ownership are collected
from AFIDA disclosures. Data cover both foreign-owned (35.7 million acres) and U.S.
subsidiary-owned land (11.5 million acres).8 USDA reports that foreign agricultural land interests
have increased by an average of over 2.4 million acres per year since 2017. In 2024, forestland
accounted for 47% of all foreign-owned land, cropland accounted for 29%, and pasture and other
agricultural land accounted for 22%. Land considered agricultural under AFIDA but used for
nonagricultural purposes (such as homesteads and roads) accounted for 2%.9

Foreign persons from five countries accounted for approximately 61% of all foreign-owned U.S.
agricultural land in 2024: Canada (34%), the Netherlands (10%), Germany (6%), Italy (6%), and
the United Kingdom (6%).10 Foreign persons from other countries with holdings of more than
500,000 acres include persons from Portugal, France, Denmark, Luxembourg, Mexico,
Switzerland, the Cayman Islands, Singapore, Sweden, and Japan.11 Approximately 6% of the
AFIDA-reported foreign-held acres were by a “country not listed” (e.g., there was no foreign
investor listed or no predominant country code).12

Existing Federal Reporting Requirements

Federal law imposes no restrictions on the amount of private U.S. agricultural land that can be
foreign-owned but requires foreign persons to disclose transactions, investments, and ownership
of agricultural land to USDA. Federal reporting requirements are authorized under AFIDA, and
USDA regulations specify how AFIDA is to be implemented.16 The Farm Service Agency (FSA)
also maintains an AFIDA handbook that provides guidance to state and county offices for
administering AFIDA.17 For example, the handbook includes information on how FSA offices are
to handle incomplete AFIDA forms and check for errors in forms.

AFIDA establishes a nationwide set of requirements for the collection of information on foreign
purchasers of U.S. agricultural land. AFIDA requires any foreign person who acquires or transfers
interest, other than a security interest (e.g., a mortgage- or debt-securing interest), in U.S.
agricultural land to submit a report to USDA within 90 days of the acquisition or transfer.18

AFIDA defines agricultural land as land that is used for agricultural, forestry, or timber
production.19 The AFIDA regulations further define agricultural land to include land that was
used for farming, ranching, or timber production within the last five years.20 An exception is
made for land that does not exceed 10 acres if the gross sale receipts from the land are less than

USDA AFIDA Proposed Rule

On June 25, 2026, USDA released a proposed rule to update AFIDA regulations, with a public
comment period through August 10, 2026. The rule proposes to transfer the administration of
AFIDA from FSA to the USDA Office of Homeland Security (OHS).41 It would also amend the
definitions of terms such as agricultural land, the reporting requirements under AFIDA, the
assessment of civil penalties, and the review process for civil penalties. The rule would also
create a process to appeal civil penalties through OHS.42

The proposed rule would expand the scope of agricultural land defined and covered under
AFIDA. The proposed rule would base its classification of farming, ranching, forestry, or timber
production activities on selected codes from the 2022 version of the North American Industry
Classification System (NAICS), which is different from the current regulations that use the SIC
manual.43 Under the proposed rule, USDA would require reporting on the basis of selected
NAICS codes that include a broader list of agricultural supply chain activities and different uses
of agricultural land than are currently included under AFIDA regulations. For example, these new
categories would include the following uses of land: animal slaughtering and processing; farm
product warehousing and storage; solar and wind electric power generation; pipeline

Stakeholder Response
Stakeholder comments on the proposed rule have varied.60 Some stakeholders have expressed
broad support for AFIDA reform, online data collection, amendments to the civil penalty structure
for AFIDA, increased reporting from or data collection on foreign adversaries, and more
collaboration between USDA and CFIUS.61 Some stakeholders have commented on the rule.62

proposal to lower the threshold of foreign ownership for reporting.64

Issues for Congress

Selected Legislation in the 119th Congress
In the 119th Congress, some Members have introduced legislation that would affect the way
foreign investors report agricultural land transactions or restrict their ability to enter the market.
Some bills would amend the reporting requirements under AFIDA. For example, S. 4192 would
require geospatial data on the boundaries of the reported property. S. 4192 would also require
USDA to lower the threshold for what qualifies as significant interest or substantial control for
foreign persons associated with a foreign adversary, which would likely lead to additional
reporting burdens.77 Other bills (e.g., H.R. 4362/S. 1969) would set in statute a minimum
ownership share for a foreign person to be required to submit an AFIDA report, whereas current
standards are set by USDA.

Some proposed legislation includes provisions relating to USDA’s data management and
reporting guidance. For example, some bills would direct USDA to develop a database, using
AFIDA data, of agricultural land purchased by foreign persons (e.g., H.R. 1920/S. 886). Other
bills would require USDA to make AFIDA data public (e.g., H.R. 1438/S. 618). Some proposals
would require USDA to update the AFIDA handbook regularly (e.g., H.R. 4362/S. 1969).

Some proposed legislation would amend the civil penalty structure for AFIDA and expand
USDA’s investigative capabilities. Some proposals would amend the USDA civil penalty amounts
(e.g., H.R. 1438/S. 618). Other bills would set certain penalty amounts for AFIDA violations from
shell corporations (e.g., H.R. 1629 and S. 845). Some bills would amend AFIDA provisions
concerning investigative actions, such as by establishing a role for the Farm Production and
Conservation Business Center in investigations (e.g., H.R. 4362/S. 1969). Other bills would
prioritize enforcement actions pertaining to foreign adversaries (e.g., S. 4192). Some proposals
would amend USDA’s authority relating to investigative actions by creating a position within
USDA focused on AFIDA investigative actions (e.g., H.R. 1920 and S. 886). Some other bills
would require annual USDA audits of reports as well as training for state and county personnel on
AFIDA (e.g., H.R. 1629 and S. 845).

Some bills would require USDA to submit reports to Congress on the agricultural land-
purchasing activities of foreign adversaries (e.g., H.R. 1920 and S. 886); the activities of foreign
owners and economic impacts of foreign ownership on U.S. family farms and rural communities
and the domestic food supply (e.g., H.R. 1629 and S. 845); or potential national security issues
associated with foreign ownership (e.g., H.R. 1438/S. 618). Additionally, some bills would
prohibit some agricultural investments by foreign adversaries (e.g., H.R. 1438/S. 618; H.R. 5760
and S. 903) or place limitations on foreign persons from participating in certain USDA programs
(e.g., H.R. 1184; H.R. 1438/S. 618; H.R. 1920 and S. 886).78

Discussion

The extent and implications of foreign ownership of agricultural land remain of interest to
Congress, the Administration, and state and local governments. In the 119th Congress, both
chambers of Congress have seen legislation that would amend foreign ownership disclosure
requirements in AFIDA, including the House-passed farm bill (H.R. 7567) and the Senate
committee-reported farm bill (S. 5526). Some of these bills would also place restrictions on
foreign ownership. In addition, the Trump Administration has proposed a new rule to amend the
AFIDA regulations, and states continue to pass laws relating to foreign ownership.
Some approaches differ between legislative proposals in the 119th Congress and USDA’s
proposed rule. For example, USDA’s proposed rule would require holders of leases longer than
one year to submit an AFIDA report; the Senate committee-reported farm bill (S. 5526) would set

land, and more types of transactions than the farm bill proposals. This is due in part to the

substantial control, and the lease length requirements. Both the proposed rule and the farm bill
proposals include efforts to collect AFIDA reports in a digital database and amend the civil
penalty amounts. These provisions may streamline the data collection process and provide an
incentive for compliance with AFIDA. Other proposals may lead to more data on foreign
ownership of U.S. agricultural land, such as by requiring geospatial data to be collected in AFIDA
reporting. An increase in data could provide USDA with more information on the potential
impacts of foreign investment on the agricultural economy. Additional reporting under AFIDA
may also create administrative burdens for filers of AFIDA reports and potentially disincentivize
foreign investment in agricultural land. Additionally, some legislation and provisions in the
proposed rule differ in approach as to whether they would create new or additional reporting
requirements for foreign ownership broadly or limit their scope to foreign adversaries.

These policy changes would have different effects. If a primary goal is to obtain additional
reporting and data specifically regarding foreign adversaries, changes addressing foreign
ownership broadly may create additional burdens on foreign investors from countries that are not
considered foreign adversaries. Conversely, policy changes directed specifically at foreign
adversaries may provide less data on foreign ownership in total, as other foreign persons would
not be subject to the new provisions.

Federal law differs from some state laws regarding restrictions on foreign ownership of
agricultural land. While amendments to AFIDA may provide more data on foreign ownership or
more oversight from USDA on controversial agricultural land transactions, they may not quell all
national security or food security concerns pertaining to certain foreign ownership issues, such as
investment in agricultural land by persons associated with foreign adversaries.80 Resolving the
national and food security issues associated with foreign agricultural land ownership may involve
multiple agencies and government bodies, including CFIUS.81 For example, as noted by GAO,
AFIDA data are not regularly used in CFIUS reviews, in part because of a lack of information
sharing between CFIUS and USDA.82 Congress may consider proposals that include requirements
for information sharing between USDA and CFIUS or codification of a specific role for USDA on
CFIUS. Additionally, some stakeholders may favor placing prohibitions on foreign persons from
certain foreign countries from purchasing agricultural land to address security concerns in a
manner that goes beyond data reporting. Others may see no need for these restrictions, may
identify or oppose the economic impacts of such policies on foreign persons or may assess the
potential for negative effects on agricultural land prices. Some stakeholders may favor reducing
the existing reporting requirements to lower administrative burdens or leaving the issue primarily
to state governments.83
