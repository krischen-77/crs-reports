Summary

Short-term, small-dollar loans (SDLs) are consumer loans with relatively low initial principal amounts (often less than $1,000) and relatively short repayment periods (typically ranging from two weeks to six months). Consumers frequently use SDL products to cover cash flow shortages that may occur because of unexpected expenses or periods of insufficient income. SDLs can be offered in various forms and by various types of lenders. Depositories (i.e., banks and credit unions) can make SDLs through financial products such as credit cards, credit card cash advances, and checking account overdraft protection programs. SDLs can also be provided by nonbank lenders, also referred to as alternative financial service (AFS) providers, which include payday or automobile-title lenders.

Whether borrower financial situations would be made worse from the use of expensive credit or from limited credit access is widely debated. Consumer groups often raise concerns regarding the affordability of SDLs. The rates and fees borrowers pay for SDLs are considered expensive. Borrowers may also fall into debt traps, situations where borrowers repeatedly roll over existing loans into new loans and subsequently incur more charges rather than completely pay off the loans. Although debt traps are more frequently discussed in the context of nonbank products such as payday loans, borrowers may still find it difficult to repay outstanding balances and face additional charges on loans, such as credit cards, that are provided by depositories. Conversely, the lending industry raises concerns about the reduced availability of SDLs. Regulations designed to reduce borrowers' costs may result in higher costs for lenders, possibly limiting or reducing the availability of SDLs.

SDLs are considered an expensive form of borrowing because they often carry triple-digit annual percentage rates (APRs). However, the standard APR calculation arguably overstates the actual cost of loans if their repayment terms are less than a full year. For borrowers seeking less expensive lending alternatives, performing reliable cost comparisons is difficult because SDLs lack standardized features. Regulatory barriers also limit the ability of banks and credit unions to compete directly with AFS providers in the SDL market, which could drive down costs. For these reasons, evaluating the relative costs of SDLs is problematic for borrowers.

Similarly, the non-standardization of SDLs and related market pricing issues are also problematic for policymakers. For example, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) implemented federal regulations in 2017 for payday, vehicle title, and certain high-cost loans—an affordability framework designed to protect borrowers by establishing baseline requirements to serve as a floor for state regulations. However, the CFPB rescinded two provisions in 2020 that would have required SDL lenders to underwrite borrowers for affordability. Had these requirements proven too costly for lenders, any resulting market contraction could have driven SDL borrowing costs even higher. As of the publication of this report, the CFPB has not introduced an alternative regulatory framework to replace the rescinded provisions. Meanwhile, on February 5, 2026, the Predatory Lending Elimination Act (S. 3793) was introduced in the 119th Congress to establish a 36% APR limit on SDLs. If S. 3793 is enacted while current market pricing issues persist, the CFPB's retrospective findings suggest that an SDL market contraction may occur.

Introduction

Short-term, small-dollar loans (SDLs) for consumers are typically for low initial principal amounts (often less than $1,000) and designed to be repaid within short periods typically ranging from two weeks to six months.1 SDLs may be used to cover cash flow shortages that occur because of unexpected expenses or periods of income shortfalls, particularly for individuals lacking adequate emergency savings or those with impaired credit.2 SDLs can be offered in various forms and by various types of lenders. Federally insured depository institutions (i.e., banks and credit unions) offer SDLs via financial products such as credit cards, credit card cash advances, and checking account overdraft protection programs. Nonbank lenders—including alternative financial service (AFS) providers and financial technology (fintech) firms—offer SDLs such as payday loans, personal debt consolidation loans, and automobile-title loans.3

The borrowing costs associated with SDLs appear substantially higher when compared to longer-term, larger-dollar loans. However, some borrowers may not fully understand the annual percentage rate (APR)—the price of SDLs and most longer-term consumer loans—especially when the loan maturities are under 12 months. Furthermore, the lack of uniform loan features makes APR price comparisons difficult across different SDL products, which is a key problem for both borrowers and regulators.

If borrowers do not fully understand or cannot do direct SDL cost comparisons, then they risk falling into debt traps. Debt traps occur when borrowers reborrow (i.e., roll over existing loans into new loans), incurring additional charges but making little progress toward paying off their initial loans.4 Repeated borrowing of similar loan amounts while steadily accumulating fees causes rising indebtedness, entrapping individuals into worse financial situations. Although debt traps are frequently linked to nonbank products such as payday loans, they may arise for borrowers who make only minimum payments (rather than pay off the entire balances at the end of statement periods) on credit card loans issued by depositories.

If regulators cannot do direct SDL cost comparisons, then inferring lenders' profit margins is problematic. In other words, regulators face challenges estimating the average size of loan markups when industry SDLs lack standardized terms, features, and consistent lender reporting requirements. Consequently, designing consumer protection policies in the absence of uniform pricing data risks subjecting SDL lenders to considerable compliance costs and or ineffective policy responses.

Below are examples of measures taken by lawmakers and regulators to address the costs of SDLs.

  • Congress passed the Credit Card Accountability Responsibility and Disclosure Act of 2009 (CARD Act; P.L. 111-24) to discourage pricing practices that could result in hidden credit card rates and penalty fees.
  • Congress passed the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010 (Dodd-Frank Act; P.L. 111-203), creating the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) and endowing it with authorities over bank and nonbank firms that offer consumer financial products, including SDL lenders.5
  • In 2013, prudential bank regulators issued warnings to banks regarding deposit advances with similarities to payday loans. Consequently, banks withdrew these and similar product types unless they obtained explicit exemptions.
  • The CFPB finalized a rule in 2017 to serve as a regulatory floor for state SDL requirements. The rule initially required lenders to either underwrite SDLs to ensure borrower affordability or provide loans with certain features—but the CFPB rescinded this requirement and subsequently reissued a final rule in 2020.
  • On February 5, 2026, the Predatory Lending Elimination Act (S. 3793) was introduced in the 119th Congress to extend the protections of the Military Lending Act of 2006 (MLA; P.L. 109-364) to all consumers. Specifically, S. 3793 would amend the Truth in Lending Act (TILA; P.L. 90-321) to establish a 36% APR limit on SDLs. S. 3793 would not apply to consumer credit products such as residential mortgages, automobile loans, or loans by federal credit unions.
  • Many states have laws governing SDLs that vary in permissibility. For example, some states may have conditional access to SDLs, while others may ban certain types altogether.6 Differences in state laws may influence loan product offerings, thereby contributing to the lack of product uniformity across SDL markets.

This report analyzes SDL market pricing issues.7 Following an overview of various loan product descriptions, the SDL pricing mechanics and related challenges are presented. Specifically, the general calculation and interpretation of the APR is illustrated. Next, the report highlights how the lack of SDL product uniformity complicates cost comparisons, thereby compromising the effectiveness of the APR as a disclosure tool. After reviewing various regulatory approaches to borrower protections, the report outlines the CFPB's initial affordability framework and subsequent modifications. The CFPB's retrospective findings may be useful for anticipating the implications of S. 3793 if enacted while current SDL market pricing issues persist. The report concludes by noting regulatory constraints that limit depositories' competitiveness with AFS lenders in certain markets, thus lessening the downward cost pressures on SDL market pricing.

SDL Prices and Pricing Issues

SDLs are considered an expensive form of borrowing, particularly because of their triple-digit APRs.9 Apart from credit cards, SDLs typically require full payment on maturity. Making partial or missing payments does little to reduce the original principal, while reborrowing adds more fees and interest charges that increase total indebtedness.10 However, the APR metric arguably overstates a loan's cost when a repayment term of less than a full year is used in the denominator. Furthermore, reliable cost comparisons depend on evaluating a borrower's next-best borrowing alternative, but finding SDLs with similar features is difficult. For these reasons, the perception that SDLs are overpriced (e.g., costing more than alternative burdens, possibly resulting in a financial burden) may not be entirely accurate.11

Loan Pricing Fundamentals: Demand and Supply Factors

The annual percentage rate (APR) is the total loan price, encompassing both interest and fees while also reflecting the demand and supply for credit. Credit demand captures what borrowers are willing to pay today instead of saving or deferring consumption until the future. The willingness to pay a premium for credit can be driven by a preference for immediate consumption or by sudden, unforeseen occurrences that necessitate urgent spending.

The supply of credit reflects the costs lenders incur to acquire the funds subsequently used to offer SDLs. Different lender types, however, rely on different funding sources. Depositories (i.e., banks and credit unions) typically finance their loan portfolios with federally insured deposits that pay rates comparable to those charged for overnight interbank borrowing. In contrast, nonbanks obtain funding by borrowing from depository institutions or private equity investors. (Some payday lending firms are wholly owned by hedge funds or publicly traded and owned by shareholders.)12 Consequently, nonbanks generally pay more relative to depositories to acquire the funds to make SDLs.

Various financial risks are also priced into consumer loans. For example, loans secured by collateral—which lenders can seize to recover losses in case of default—carry lower rates relative to those secured without collateral. For this reason, unsecured SDLs are more expensive than longer-term loans that are typically secured by the purchase being financed. The cost of credit also reflects risk-based pricing, the practice of charging riskier borrowers higher rates to reflect their additional credit or default risk.13 While risk-based pricing strategies may decrease loan rejections and expand credit access for higher-risk borrowers, risk premiums will increase their borrowing costs.

Borrowers may also pay fees for expenses that are unrelated to their financial risks. For example, lenders incur costs during loan underwriting—the process of evaluating an applicant's financial risks (e.g., purchasing credit report data; examining past banking relationships; verifying identity, income, and employment).14 Other expenses may require further investigation to uncover.15

Understanding the Annual Percentage Rate (APR) Calculation

The APR represents the total annual borrowing costs of a loan expressed as a percentage. The APR is calculated using both interest rates and origination fees.16 The APR may be calculated using the following general formula:

APR= [(INTFEES)/(LNAMT)] * (365/DAYSOUT) * 100, where

INTFEES=total interest and fees paid by the borrower;

LNAMT=loan amount or total borrowings; and

DAYSOUT= number of days that the loan is outstanding (term length).

The formula shows that the APR is inversely related to both the LNAMT and DAYSOUT. Assuming two loans have identical LNAMTs and INTFEES, a 30-day SDL will mathematically generate a higher APR than a loan with a longer maturity, such as a year or more. For this reason, how best to interpret the APR for loans with maturities under 365 days has been debated.17 An APR based on a term length of one year or greater accurately reflects the annual cost of credit. By contrast, an APR for a loan expected to be repaid in less than 365 days is arguably overstated.

APR comparisons are most reliable for loans with identical maturities.18 For example, comparing two payday loans with identical two-week maturities is most meaningful, even though both carry triple-digit APRs. In contrast, comparing APRs of loans with different maturities (e.g., a loan that matures in two weeks versus one maturing in 365 days) can be misleading. Although the APR of the longer-term loan will mathematically be lower, the interest and fees paid by borrowers can be higher for larger loan amounts or longer maturity lengths. Stated differently, loans with triple-digit APRs may not necessarily be more expensive than those with double-digit APRs, particularly when loans with different amounts and maturity lengths are compared.

Some Challenges Achieving SDL Market Pricing Efficiency

Although standard economic theory assumes that competition among buyers and sellers should drive market prices toward a product's true value (i.e., cost), this may not occur in SDL markets. Cost comparisons across SDL markets are more complex, and the price may not even be the primary consideration for borrowers who acquire SDLs. These issues complicate the ability to evaluate the level of market competitiveness, all of which are outlined in this section.

Failure of Market Price Convergence

A market is competitive when suppliers are likely to lose market share by pricing their products too aggressively, meaning that they lack the ability to set prices higher than their competitors. Stated differently, an economic principle—the law of one price (LOOP)—asserts that competition will cause the prices of identical products to converge.20 When the LOOP holds, SDL pricing is likely to be efficient, meaning that competition has driven prices down to more closely reflect lenders' supply costs (rather than excessive markups) even if the products remain expensive.21

Ideally, SDL comparisons by prospective borrowers or regulators would consist of either two identical loans (e.g., two payday loans) or an SDL product and a close alternative (e.g., a bank overdraft product and a payday loan). However, making ideal comparisons is not always possible. Small differences in products can still increase the difficulty of comparing costs. For example, when setting their own overdraft pricing policies, some banks may charge a fixed fee for each overdraft regardless of the amount borrowed, whereas others have a tiered pricing structure as the amount of the loan increases.22 Some banks require repayment sooner than the two-week period typically associated with a payday loan; other banks may allow slightly longer repayment periods, such as 30 days. Thus, without uniformity among products, APR comparisons are difficult among similar depositories and especially across depositories and nonbank lenders.23

Furthermore, variations in pricing policies create data collection challenges that obscure whether SDL markets can converge as predicted by the LOOP to achieve efficient pricing. Not all lenders must report data on their SDLs, and differing pricing policies cause reported metrics to lack standardized definitions. For example, interest rates and fees can be reported separately or combined into a single metric. Maturity lengths differ among SDL products, raising comparability questions. Because of their unique repayment terms, buy now, pay later plans technically have 0% APRs.24 Moreover, data regarding borrowers' financial literacy and shopping for better credit pricing is limited.25 Evidence on SDL market competitiveness, therefore, is fragmented, and the APR may not be the primary factor borrowers consider when choosing these loans.

Borrowers' Non-Price Considerations

Instead of making loan comparisons based solely on product prices, borrowers have strong preferences for certain product features, placing value on the conveniences associated with certain SDL products. Under such circumstances, prices may reflect more specialized market segmentations. The situations below illustrate when borrowers may place greater weight on nonprice factors relative to the total loan price.

  • Some borrowers may not find the APR to be a useful disclosure—either because the product terms are not uniform or because the interpretation of the metric is not understood. Notably, some SDL users report that the flat-fee pricing structure of AFS providers is easier to understand in comparison to banks' fee structure disclosures.26 Furthermore, some borrowers facing cash flow shortages may value the actual dollar cost of credit rather than the APR.
  • Borrowers may consider factors related to product delivery, such as the convenience of locations, business hours, or the length of the application process.27 Consequently, these borrowers may be willing to pay higher fees for the speed and accessibility of AFS products.28
  • Rather than compare APRs, some borrowers choose loan products based on their perceptions of how best to access credit. For example, some borrowers may use credit cards for routine payments and payday loans mostly for emergencies. Others may use credit cards sparingly to preserve credit access during emergencies.29 Utilizing depositories' loan products can increase borrowers' credit utilization ratios (the amount of outstanding debt relative to a credit limit), which can adversely affect their credit scores and credit access in case of future emergencies.30 Conversely, use of AFS products may not appear on credit reports.

Pricing, therefore, may not be the sole consideration for SDL borrowers. When choosing between bank and AFS products, borrowers frequently prioritize speed and delivery convenience, giving AFS providers a competitive advantage. Consequently, evaluating whether SDLs are overpriced is challenging.

Evidence of Competitive and Noncompetitive Pricing in Payday Markets

Pricing efficiency may be evaluated by examining the extent of market competitiveness. Some research has found evidence of market competitiveness in the payday lending industry, meaning that pricing structures appear commensurate with operating costs.31 For alternative financial service (AFS) lenders, profitability was found to depend highly on volume and loan loss rates.32 Although rollovers expand a lender's loan volume, they increase the risk of an elevated loan loss rate—a primary expense for this industry (alongside wage and occupancy costs such as rent).33 Additional research found that the risk-adjusted returns at publicly traded payday firms were comparable to those of other financial firms.34 Thus, research has yet to confirm whether payday firms' pricing practices generate revenues significantly in excess of costs.

Alternatively, the evidence of price collusion, referring to either explicit or implicit collaboration by industry firms to charge similar prices, is consistent with noncompetitive pricing practices. Price caps, such as state usury ceilings, may facilitate implicit price collusion by incentivizing lenders to charge the maximum legal rate even when their costs are lower. This behavioral response, predicted by economic theory, conceals evidence of unlawful price fixing.35 Research has found that many payday lenders charge the maximum usury rates allowed in their states, but whether their cost structures would permit lower rates was not confirmed.36

SDL Regulatory Issues: Current Approaches and Challenges Absent Market Price Convergence

Consumer lending is largely regulated to protect consumers and ensure lenders' compliance with fair-lending laws. Bank and nonbank lenders are subject to federal consumer protection and fair-lending laws when offering consumer loan products, and multiple regulators oversee small-dollar lending. Depositories with assets below $10 billion are supervised for consumer protection compliance by their prudential regulators. Banks are supervised by the federal prudential regulators, namely the Federal Reserve System, the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC), and the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC). The National Credit Union Administration (NCUA) is the federal prudential regulator for credit unions. State-chartered depositories may face additional state-level regulations, but they must still comply with federal regulations if their deposits are federally insured. Depositories with national charters are generally exempt from additional state consumer regulations, but exceptions may apply for certain circumstances.

The CFPB is the primary federal regulator with the authority to issue regulations pursuant to most federal consumer financial protection laws that cover an array of consumer financial products and services.37 Depository institutions with assets greater than $10 billion are supervised for consumer protection compliance by the CFPB. Nonbank lenders—financial firms without national or state depository charters, which include many AFS providers (e.g., payday and auto-title lenders)—may be supervised by the CFPB or state financial regulators.

Regulatory Approaches for Consumer Protections

Federal and state authorities (independently or jointly) may enforce consumer protections for SDLs through a combination of the following approaches: disclosure requirements; product-feature requirements; underwriting requirements; unfair, deceptive, or abusive acts or practices (UDAAP) requirements; and usury laws.

  • Disclosure laws are designed to ensure that borrowers are informed of their loan costs. TILA passed in 1968, is currently implemented by Regulation Z. TILA applies to nearly all forms of consumer credit, requiring covered lenders to disclose total credit costs—both the loan rate and fees—in the form of the APR.38 TILA does not regulate what lenders may charge for consumer loans. TILA does not apply to business loans.39 Financial institutions operating in certain states may face additional state disclosure requirements.
  • Product feature requirements or restrictions can be regulated. Payday lending, for example, is permitted in 38 states, but some states place restrictions or outright prohibitions on the rollover product feature.40 In 2009, Congress passed the CARD Act to ban a product feature that automatically recalculates and applies additional finance charges based on formulas that credit card and overdraft borrowers may not fully understand.41
  • Underwriting regulations can require lenders to verify specific documents or consider other factors when underwriting applicant credit requests. For example, the Dodd-Frank Act requires a lender to make a good-faith determination of a consumer's ability to repay a mortgage and to show evidence of compliance by offering a qualified mortgage.42
  • UDAAP is broad authority to promulgate rules declaring that specific acts or practices are unlawful because they are "unfair, deceptive, or abusive."43 The CFPB initially used its UDAAP authority to issue a bulletin related to the marketing and sale of "add-on" products with credit cards (e.g., debt protection, identity theft protection, credit score tracking) that are supplementary to the credit provided by the card itself—but that bulletin was rescinded.44 The CFPB provided guidance for lenders to avoid harming consumers when offering products with add-on features.45
  • Usury laws are consumer protection laws broadly designed to limit the amount of interest charged on loans. Usury laws are frequently promulgated at the state level, meaning that loan originations are subject to the caps in the applicable state; some states have multiple usury rates that apply to different forms of credit.46 The National Consumer Law Center reports that the Uniform Small Loan Laws, which place caps of 36% to 42% per year on loans of $300 or less, were adopted in 34 states over 1914 to 1943 to encourage lenders to make SDLs and ultimately reduce widespread loan-sharking.47

Currently, no general federal usury limit exists for SDLs, and federal law prohibits the CFPB from establishing usury limits.48 Statutory and regulatory interventions, however, have resulted in rate and fee restrictions under specific scenarios, as outlined below, and these restrictions may expand in the future.

  • The MLA (P.L. 109-364) was passed in 2006 to protect active-duty military personnel and their eligible family members from predatory lending.49 The final rule implementing the MLA in 2007 requires that payday loans have terms of 91 days or less; vehicle-title loans have terms of 181 days or less; and SDLs to military personnel be limited to a 36% annual interest rate, which is known as the military APR (MAPR).50 On July 22, 2015, the Department of Defense announced final rules to extend the MAPR to a wider array of credit products, including credit cards.51 As of October 3, 2016, lenders receive safe harbor protection if they verify whether a consumer is eligible for the MLA protections by using information provided by the MLA database maintained by the Department of Defense.52 As of October 3, 2017, the MAPR applies to credit card products offered to military servicemembers.
  • The credit union system's ability to serve and compete in the SDL market has limitations, namely its membership restrictions and interest rate ceilings.53 However, the credit union system is permitted to make payday alternative loans (PALs) to its membership, which differ from traditional payday loans. PALs typically have longer maturities than AFS products and, therefore, lower APRs. The CFPB argues that PALs protect consumers because the interest rate is no greater than 28% and the application fee is no greater than $20.54 NCUA also requested a Department of Defense exemption from the 36% MAPR for PALs to prevent credit unions from reducing their lending to military servicemembers.55 (Certain credit card fees were also exempted from the calculation when the MAPR was extended to credit cards.)56 Consequently, specific exemptions are necessary for depositories to compete effectively with AFS providers.
  • On February 5, 2026, the Predatory Lending Elimination Act (S. 3793) was introduced to extend MLA protections to all consumers. The bill would amend TILA to establish a 36% APR limit on SDLs while exempting consumer credit products—such as residential mortgages, automobile purchase loans, and loans issued by federal credit unions—from the APR cap. As previously discussed, if total funding costs are not sufficiently covered by the 36% cap to yield profits commensurate with elevated default risks, lenders may offer fewer SDLs and seek more profitable opportunities. Congress may consider delegating rate-cap authority to the CFPB rather than codifying a fixed cap in statute. Because the APR non-convergence issue remains problematic, this delegation would allow the CFPB to adjust or dismantle caps if previous regulatory challenges, discussed in the following section, resurface.

Regulatory Challenges Absent Market Price Convergence

Market price convergence, conceptually analogous to an efficient LOOP outcome, allows regulators to rely on benchmark comparisons rather than inflexible APR caps to implement consumer protection regulations. For example, although achieving price convergence in the conventional mortgage market still has challenges, various standardization initiatives in underwriting, data collection, and credit reporting enable mortgage APRs—particularly for creditworthy borrowers—to converge within a certain range.57 For this reason, the consumer protection framework adopted by regulators under the Home Mortgage Disclosure Act (P.L. 94-200) is based on APR comparisons. Specifically, the APRs offered by mortgage lenders must be reported to the CFPB and compared to an average (weekly) benchmark APR to see whether they stay within an established range.58

By contrast, underwriting is costly for many SDLs given their low principal amounts, and lenders are also unlikely to review credit reports. Combined with the lack of standardized products and price convergence, regulating the SDL markets to protect (subprime) borrowers may compromise credit availability. This section summarizes examples of challenges.

The CFPB's Payday, Vehicle Title, and Certain High-Cost Installment Loans Rule: Evolution from 2017 to 2025

Given the difficulty of relying on SDL cost comparisons, the CFPB prioritized a borrower's ability to repay rather than APR benchmark metrics. On October 5, 2017, the CFPB issued a final rule establishing minimum requirements for SDLs, which included underwriting requirements for lenders.59 The CFPB stated that one primary purpose of the rule was to end payday debt traps, which would be used as an indicator of loan affordability.60 The CFPB deemed loan payments unaffordable if the only three options for borrowers when unable to repay the full amount due are to (1) default on the loan, (2) take out an additional loan, or (3) make the loan payment while failing to meet other major financial obligations or basic living expenses.61 The proposed framework was designed to mitigate these adverse outcomes.

The rule established—at the federal level—a consumer protection floor, covering loans lasting 45 days or less, such as payday loans, auto-title loans, and direct deposit advances. Loans for more than 45 days in which the cost of credit exceeds 36% and the lender has a security interest (e.g., the ability to repossess a borrower's vehicle if the loan is not repaid or access to a paycheck or a checking account) are covered. The rule excludes various types of loans, such as consumer loans secured by the good purchased (e.g., a durable good that can be used to secure the loan, such as a car), mortgage loans, credit card loans, student loans, overdrafts, and PALs (issued by prudentially regulated credit unions). The rule applies to all lenders of covered products.

The key provisions below were promulgated in the initial final rule, but only the last provision—payment withdrawal requirements when collecting funds owed—remains active, as the preceding requirements were formally rescinded.

  • The Full-Payment (Ability-to-Repay) Test for Covered Short-Term Loans. Lenders were initially required to consider and verify the borrower's income, major financial obligations, and borrowing history, as well as the timing of the borrower's income, using information from at least one credit bureau. Lenders were required to ensure that borrowers could pay off their loans and all finance charges without having to reborrow for approximately 30 days. Borrowers would be unable to reborrow or refinance the same amount of debt, and the total number of successive short-term loans would be capped. A lender would not be able to make a loan to a consumer who already has taken three loans within 30 days of each other.
  • The Principal Step-Down Option for Covered Short-Term Loans. For lenders choosing not to adopt systems for SDL underwriting, the final rule allowed for an alternative compliance option that stipulates required loan features. SDLs up to $500 could be originated without underwriting for borrowers having no other outstanding SDLs or loans with balloon payments. Borrowers may obtain up to two extensions if they pay down at least one-third of the remaining principal balance for each extension. Lenders must still confirm that a customer does not have outstanding loans with other lenders, and the customer would not be permitted to have any more than six loans over a consecutive 12-month period.
  • Alternative Compliance Option 2 for Longer-Term Balloon-Payment (e.g., Payday and Vehicle Title) Loans. A longer-term balloon payment loan was defined in the final rule as one that requires a borrower to repay most of the entire loan amount in a single payment. Lenders could make loans (without meeting the ability-to-repay requirement) meeting the following criteria: The maturity of the loan must be a minimum of 46 days and a maximum of 24 months.
  • Payment Withdrawals Requirements When Collecting Funds Owed. When collecting funds owed, lenders must give advance notice to borrowers before attempting to debit payment from their depository checking, savings, or prepaid accounts. After two consecutive unsuccessful payment collection attempts, lenders must obtain new authorizations from borrowers before making further collection attempts. This rule protects borrowers from amassing insufficient funds and overdraft fees (levied by third-party depositories) when repeated attempts are unlikely to result in successful recoveries for SDL lenders.

Although the final rule set August 19, 2019, as the date for lenders to comply with the mandatory underwriting provisions, a proposed rule to rescind the mandatory underwriting provisions of the finalized 2017 rule was issued on February 6, 2019, delaying the compliance deadline to November 19, 2020.62 The CFPB subsequently issued a final rule on July 7, 2020, revoking the 2017 mandatory underwriting provisions for covered SDLs and longer-term balloon-payment loans before they ever went into effect.63 The provision limiting lenders' collection attempts, however, was retained by the 2020 final rule. Although the technical rules remain in effect, the CFPB announced in 2025 that it will not prioritize active enforcement or penalties regarding these payment and disclosure provisions, choosing instead to redirect resources and pursue actions to narrow or reconsider the rule.64

The CFPB and industry stakeholders had projected a large SDL market contraction prior to finalizing the 2017 rule.65 Because SDLs lack standardization, APR comparisons could not provide reliable estimates of average lender costs to inform the CFPB's affordability standard. Thus, the CFPB's proposed rule initially estimated that its affordability standard would result in a 55% to 62% decrease in the number of SDLs offered by AFS providers, alongside a 71% to 76% drop in their revenue.66 The CFPB expected that many AFS lenders would choose the step-down compliance option rather than incur the underwriting costs necessary to comply with the full payment test. Meanwhile, a study commissioned by the AFS industry also predicted a large contraction, closer to an 82.5% decrease in SDL offerings.67

Assuming more AFS lenders would adopt the step-down compliance option, the CFPB attributed the projected SDL market contraction to fewer loan rollovers, thus benefiting consumers by reducing both their default chances and likelihood of falling into debt traps.68 In contrast, the industry attributed its projected contraction to the inability of lenders to recover compliance costs. The industry argued that payday loans of $500 or less would not generate sufficient yields to cover the added underwriting expenses.69 Furthermore, some individual AFS lenders (e.g., storefront lenders) had already lacked sufficient loan volume to cover added underwriting, documentation, and verification costs.70 Regardless of the compliance option chosen, the industry had predicted that borrowers would face unmet credit needs or be forced to use less preferable loan products.71 As noted, the 2017 underwriting provisions were rescinded before either predicted outcome could be observed.

In its 2020 final rule rescinding the underwriting requirements, the CFPB reconsidered certain 2017 positions. First, the CFPB reversed its previous stance that consumers lacked a requisite understanding of the their ability to repay SDLs to avoid the risks related to unaffordable payments, reborrowing, or default.72 Second, the CFPB determined that the evidence used to conclude that consumers lacked accessibility to alternative mainstream loan products (from mainstream financial institutions) was insufficient.73 Moreover, if the 2017 requirements had led to significant market shrinkage, less competition could further drive up SDL product prices, arguably causing greater financial harm to consumers.74

Permissible Activities of Depositories

Regulatory differences between depositories and AFS lenders may restrict competition that could lower SDL market pricing. As of fourth quarter 2023, the Federal Reserve estimated that depositories held 43% of SDLs (defined as personal loans with a credit limit of $1,000), and nearly 70% of those borrowers would be considered subprime (having credit scores below 620).75

A regulatory constraint facing depository institutions is the inability to offer SDLs comparable to those offered by AFS providers. For example, a subprime (fee harvester) credit card mirrors a payday loan because its total fees amount to a large proportion of the credit limit. Depositories could offer subprime credit cards to customers with impaired credit, which means those posing a higher probability of default risk relative to more creditworthy customers. The CARD Act, however, mandates that the total fees of subprime credit cards cannot exceed 25% of the initial credit limit. This cap restricts a loan product option for subprime borrowers, thereby undermining depositories' ability to effectively compete with AFS providers in an SDL (i.e., payday) market.76

On April 25, 2013, the OCC, FDIC, and Federal Reserve issued guidance that expressed concerns regarding deposit advance products given their similarities to payday loans.77 The prudential regulators noted that the high costs and repeated extensions of credit could increase borrower default risks.78 They recommended that customers with impaired credit should not be eligible for this product, lenders should not extend subsequent advance loans until a customer repays each deposit advance in full, and lenders may offer no more than one loan per monthly statement cycle.79 Afterward, most banks discontinued offering deposit advances.80 In other words, prudential safety and soundness compliance necessitates formal underwriting, prompting depositories to offer longer-term loans that not only allow recovery of underwriting costs but also are easier for borrowers to repay.81

The OCC rescinded its deposit advance guidance in October 2017, followed by the FDIC in July 2020.82 The prudential depository regulators subsequently issued joint interagency guidance to provide uniform principles for all financial institutions.83 The 2020 principles still retained emphasis on affordability (measured by a high percentage of customers repaying their SDLs), mitigation of debt cycles, and products capable of helping borrowers improve their credit scores and transition into more traditional credit products.84 Banks have largely chosen not to invest in the development of deposit advance products.85

Although the federal banking regulators encourage small-dollar lending, banks can primarily serve market segments that favor more traditional loan products, as discussed in the text box below. Rather than competing directly, banks can partner with or extend credit to AFS providers that will subsequently offer SDLs.86 Banks, however, are still unlikely to be awarded credit under the Community Reinvestment Act (P.L. 95-128, Title VIII) for either offering SDLs that resemble those originated by AFS providers or extending indirect support for SDL markets.87

Can Small-Dollar Lending Be Profitable for Banks?

The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) conducted a small-dollar loan pilot program between December 2007 and December 2009, beginning with 31 participating banks, to observe the feasibility of offering lower-credit-cost alternatives to payday loans and fee-based overdraft programs.88 Customers in the pilot program were offered loans that, unlike typical short-term, small-dollar loan (SDL) products, were for $2,500 or less for approximately 90 days or more with annual percentage rates (APRs) of 36% or less; the streamlined underwriting process included proof of identity, proof of income, and a credit report.89 The FDIC reported that banks found small-dollar lending to be a useful business strategy, facilitating the ability to build or retain profitable, long-term relationships with customers as well as the opportunity to get favorable consideration under the Community Reinvestment Act. However, banks found small-dollar lending programs to be more successful or cost-effective when targeted to existing customers who use financial products over longer time periods (as opposed to new customers with financial behaviors and histories that have not been previously observed).90 The fixed costs associated with evaluating financial risks (e.g., purchase of credit reporting data; past banking relationships; verification of identity, income, and employment) are similar, regardless of whether a financial product is offered for two weeks or a credit card loan is made for a year. Stated differently, recovering the underwriting costs for new SDL customers or those who infrequently make transactions is difficult.
