Introduction

The Office of Personnel Management (OPM) is the federal government’s central human resources
agency and is responsible for providing leadership and oversight of the federal civilian workforce.
The antecedent of OPM may be traced to the enactment of the Civil Service Act of 1883, known
as the Pendleton Act, which statutorily established some underlying aspects of today’s federal
personnel system.1 Prior to the Pendleton Act, according to OPM, “[f]ederal employment was
largely based on political affiliation or personal connections, a system known as the ‘spoils
system,’ rather than applicants’ knowledge, skills, and abilities.”2 The Pendleton Act and the
resulting merit system, OPM says, “ushered in a new era and created a competitive civil service,
which emphasized an applicant’s relative level of qualifications for the position being sought,
after fair and open competition.”3 The act established the Civil Service Commission, which was
intended to insulate the hiring and management of federal employees from partisan political
influence.4

The Civil Service Commission continued with much the same role until the enactment of the
Civil Service Reform Act of 1978 (CSRA; P.L. 95-454), which created OPM.5 The CSRA
authorized significant leadership roles in federal human resources management and personnel
policy for OPM.6 By consolidating human resources leadership within OPM, Congress intended
for the agency “to concentrate on planning and administering an effective government-wide
program of personnel management.”7

Statutory Authority

Generally, the statutory provisions governing OPM are codified in Title 5 of the U.S. Code and,
specifically, in Chapter 11. Section 1103(a) of Title 5 vests the OPM director with fulfilling
various functions, including “executing, administering, and enforcing (A) the civil service rules
and regulations … and the laws governing the civil service; and (B) the other activities of [OPM]
including retirement and classification activities; except with respect to functions for which the
Merit Systems Protection Board or the Special Counsel is primarily responsible.”8

In addition, the OPM director is responsible for “aiding the President … in preparing such civil
service rules as the President prescribes, and otherwise advising the President on actions which
may be taken to promote an efficient civil service and a systematic application of the merit

Mission, Key Functions, and Organization

As the federal government’s central personnel agency, OPM has responsibilities related to human
capital management, administration of federal employee benefit programs, and vetting of job
candidates. In recent years, OPM has included a mission statement and a list of core values in its
strategic plans. For example, the agency’s Strategic Plan: Fiscal Years 2026-2030 mission
statement is “OPM leads federal agencies in building merit-based, accountable, and modern
workforce systems that attract and retain top talent to meet America’s toughest challenges.”14

The Role of OPM in Federal Workforce
Management

Federal workforce management functions are distributed across a spectrum of centralized and
decentralized activities. Some functions are administered more centrally by OPM, while others
are delegated to, or administered directly by, individual executive branch agencies. The degree of
OPM involvement varies from establishing government-wide requirements and exercising direct
oversight to providing guidance and allowing agencies substantial discretion in implementation.
These different approaches may involve trade-offs between promoting consistency and
uniformity on the one hand and providing agencies with greater flexibility to tailor workforce
policies and practice to their needs on the other.

Competitive Service Hiring

Competitive service hiring provides an example of a workforce management function in which
OPM establishes a government-wide framework and affords agencies substantial discretion in
implementation.37 OPM establishes the standards and procedures governing the competitive
hiring process, while agencies generally determine when to use these standards and procedures to
fill positions, which positions to fill, and which permissible assessment tools to use to evaluate
candidates in practice. This gives agencies flexibility to use the process established by OPM in
ways that reflect their individual missions, organizational structures, budgets, and personnel
needs. This discretion may enable agencies to respond to changing staffing needs while
maintaining their commitment to merit-based hiring principles.

between OPM and individual agencies. OPM establishes regulations and standards for
performance management, including the basic framework for how federal employes are to be
assessed.38 Agencies are required to assess the performance of their personnel within this
framework, but they have substantial responsibility for designing and administering their own

Reductions in Force

Reduction in force (RIF) procedures provide an example of a workforce management function in
which OPM exercises a comparatively high degree of centralized authority and agencies have less
discretion in implementation.39 Agencies retain the authority to determine whether a RIF is
necessary and when it will occur; however, once an agency decides to conduct a RIF, it must
follow the detailed requirements established in statute and implemented through OPM regulation,
including rules governing competitive areas, retention standing, employee notification, and
appeals. This relatively prescriptive framework may reflect the importance of fairness and
impartiality in administering RIFs. The rigidity of this process may help prioritize uniformity and
consistency while still allowing agencies limited flexibility to decide when to use the process in
response to the conditions that may give rise to a RIF.

Issues for Congressional Oversight

For congressional oversight purposes, several issues related to OPM’s varied responsibilities and
capacity as an agency may warrant consideration. Recent federal workforce reforms, including
changes to hiring and workforce downsizing, may increase demands on OPM as it develops
government-wide policies, provides guidance, and oversees implementation of those policies and
guidance. At the same time, reductions in OPM’s staffing levels could raise questions about the
agency’s capacity to perform its responsibilities effectively. More broadly, Congress may wish to
consider how the appropriate balance between centralized OPM oversight and agency-level
discretion could change as federal workforce management priorities and needs evolve.

In addition to developing the applicable rules and procedures for employee appeals, OPM must
now receive, review, and adjudicate individual appeals, potentially creating a new and ongoing
workload for staff that could be burdensome. OPM, however, argues that this change will
streamline the process.

Given these recent developments in the federal workforce management policy arena, Congress
may wish to consider whether OPM has sufficient capacity to effectively carry out its
responsibilities.

OPM Staffing and Budget Changes

OPM’s salaries and expenses budget declined from $219,076,000 in FY2025 to $167,535,000 in
FY2026, and the President’s budget requested $145,973,000 for FY2027 (see “OPM’s Operating
Budget”). In addition, OPM’s staff has decreased by 1,059 employees since January 20, 2025.49
These changes may have implications for OPM’s ability to carry out its responsibilities at a time
when the agency’s workload may also be expanding (see “OPM’s Role in Recent Federal
Workforce Policy Reforms”). Reductions in budgetary resources in OPM’s Salaries and Expenses
account and recent workforce reforms that have resulted in new roles for OPM may raise
questions for Congress about OPM’s capacity to provide effective government-wide oversight,
process increased amounts of agency information in the form of new reports, and implement new
workforce policies while continuing to perform its existing statutory functions.

Centralizing Versus Delegating Federal Workforce Management
Responsibilities

Workforce management responsibilities are allocated among OPM and individual agencies to
varying degrees (see “The Role of OPM in Federal Workforce Management”). The way in which
these responsibilities are allocated may raise questions about the appropriate balance between
centralized oversight within OPM and agency discretion and flexibility. Increased centralization
within OPM may promote consistency, uniformity, and government-wide accountability, while
delegation may allow agencies to respond more readily to differences in mission, workforce
composition, and operational needs. At the same time, greater centralization may increase
demands on OPM’s resources and capacity, while greater delegation may result in less uniformity
across agencies. In considering the future approach to federal workforce management, Congress
may wish to assess which functions are best suited to centralized administration by OPM, which
may be more effectively delegated to agencies, and how the appropriate balance may vary as
circumstances change.
