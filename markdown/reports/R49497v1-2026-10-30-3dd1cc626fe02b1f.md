Summary

The District of Columbia (DC) government's local operating budget and much of its local legislation is subject to congressional approval, pursuant to the plenary legislative authority of Congress over the federal capital granted by the U.S. Constitution. In addition to congressional authority over the DC budget process, annual federal appropriations legislation has typically included a series of federal payments for a variety of services and initiatives in DC. Such legislation also often includes general provisions, specific to DC, that establish fiscal, budgetary, and policy controls on federal (and in some cases, local) DC funds.

Each year, the DC government produces a budget through a process coordinated between the Executive Office of the Mayor and the Council of the District of Columbia (DC council). Under the current process, the budget consists of a federal portion and a local portion, which are adopted by the DC council in two separate bills. Once approved, the federal portion is transmitted by the mayor to the President of the United States, who forwards it to Congress for review, possible modification, and approval through the annual appropriations process. The local portion is submitted by the chair of the DC council to the Speaker of the House of Representatives and the President of the Senate, for review by Congress. In 2013, DC enacted the Local Budget Autonomy Amendment Act of 2012 (D.C. Law 19-321), which amended DC's home rule charter to allow for enactment of DC's local budget after a 30-day congressional review period (also known as the layover period), similar to most other DC laws, as opposed to passing the local budget through the federal appropriations process. After a series of legal and legislative challenges, the act was upheld by a 2016 DC Superior Court ruling. The DC government has observed the act in its budget process since 2016. Congress has continued to approve the DC budget in the regular appropriations process.

On May 2, 2025, the Trump Administration submitted to Congress its FY2026 discretionary budget request, which included a proposed $728.5 million in federal payments to the District of Columbia. The DC government passed a budget of $22 billion in gross funds on September 4, 2025. In the House, a Financial Services and General Government Appropriations Act (FSGG), 2026 ([H.R. 5166](http://www.congress.gov/bill/119/hr/5166)), which would have provided $823.9 million in federal payments for DC, was marked up in subcommittee on July 21, 2025; marked up in the full Appropriations Committee on September 3, 2025; and reported ([H.Rept. 119-236](http://www.congress.gov/congressional-report/119/hrpt/236) ) on September 5, 2025. In the Senate, [S. 3290](http://www.congress.gov/bill/119/s/3290) was introduced on December 1, 2025; no further committee action occurred on the bill. The bill would have provided $794.8 million in federal payments for DC. No full-year FSGG appropriations were enacted prior to the end of September 2025, and appropriations lapsed. A continuing resolution (CR; Division A of [P.L. 119-37](http://www.congress.gov/law/119/pub/37)) was enacted on November 12, 2025, providing funding through January 30, 2026. Section 136 of the CR granted congressional approval of the DC general fund and capital budgets for FY2026, during the covered period. The President signed the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026 ([P.L. 119-75](http://www.congress.gov/law/119/pub/75)), on February 3, 2026. Division E, Title IV of the act provided $879 million in federal payments for DC.

Introduction

The U.S. Constitution provides Congress with plenary legislative authority over the District of Columbia (DC) as the federal capital.[1](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn1 "Article I, Section 8, clause 17 of the U.S. Constitution, https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/article-1/section-8/clause-17/    .") With the passage of the 1973 District of Columbia Self-Government and Governmental Reorganization Act (Home Rule Act; [P.L. 93-198](https://www.congress.gov/law/93/pub/198)), Congress granted DC limited home rule authority and empowered DC residents to elect a mayor and city council.[2](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn2 "For an overview of the current and previous local governing structures in DC, see CRS In Focus IF12577, Governing the District of Columbia: Overview and Timeline , by Joseph V. Jaroscak and Ben Leubsdorf .") The Home Rule Act also outlines the process by which the DC government establishes its budget and manages local finances, subject to congressional approval and oversight.[3](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn3 "87 Stat. 798-812; D.C. Official Code , Subpart 1.")

Congress retains ultimate legislative authority over DC.[4](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn4 "87 Stat. 813.") As it pertains to the DC budget process, Congress has historically exercised this authority through approval of the annual DC budget (including planned expenditures of local revenues),[5](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn5 "For a recent example of a provision granting budgetary approval to DC in an appropriations act, see P.L. 118-47 , Division B, Title IV, District of Columbia Funds .") provision of federal payments for certain activities in DC,[6](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn6 "For recent examples of federal payments for activities in DC, see P.L. 118-47 , Division B, Title IV.") and the inclusion of general provisions (often referred to as "riders") that direct or limit how the local government expends funds.[7](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn7 "For recent examples of general provisions pertaining to the DC, see P.L. 118-47 , Division B, Title VIII.") In some cases, in the absence of full-year appropriations, Congress has included provisions in continuing resolutions allowing DC to expend local revenues pursuant to its current fiscal year budget, prior to formally approving the DC budget in an appropriations act.[8](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn8 "For example, see P.L. 118-83 , Division A, Section 128.")

This report provides an overview of the DC budget process; details the types and amounts of federal payments appropriated in the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026 ([P.L. 119-75](http://www.congress.gov/law/119/pub/75)); and discusses selected general provisions related to local DC policy in the FY2026 appropriations act.

District of Columbia Budget Process

The DC Home Rule Act codifies the process by which the Executive Office of the Mayor and Council of the District of Columbia (DC council) develop DC's annual budget.[9](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn9 "87 Stat. 798; D.C. Official Code § 1–204.42.") Under this process, the mayor establishes a proposed budget, consistent with guidance on funding levels from the DC Chief Financial Officer (CFO). The mayor's budget also considers agency requests and other analysis by the Executive Office of the Mayor.[10](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn10 "Council of the District of Columbia, Office of the Budget Director, Budget Process (Step-by-Step) , https://www.dccouncilbudget.com/budget-process-step-by-step .")

The DC council serves both an oversight and deliberative legislative function during the budget process. Each DC council committee holds performance review hearings and budget hearings for each agency under its jurisdiction. Committees compile information and recommendations from this oversight process into committee reports, which provide the basis for the development of a unified balanced budget at the council level.

As required in the Home Rule Act, the DC council must approve a budget within 70 days after receiving a budget proposal from the mayor.[11](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn11 "87 Stat. 801; D.C. Official Code § 1–204. 46.") The council considers and approves the annual DC budget through three separate but related local bills:

* 1. the Local Budget Act,[12](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn12 "For an example, see DC Government, A26-0147 —Fiscal Year 2026 Local Budget Act of 2025 , October 23, 2025, https://lims.dccouncil.gov/Legislation/B26-0260 .")
* 2. the Federal Portion Request Act,[13](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn13 "For an example, see DC Government, A26-0143 —Fiscal Year 2026 Federal Portion Budget Request Act of 2025 , September 12, 2025, https://lims.dccouncil.gov/Legislation/B26-0261 .") and
* 3. the Budget Support Act.[14](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn14 "DC budget support acts typically include amendments to local laws that are deemed necessary to carry out the fiscal year budget. For an example, see DC Government, A26-0148—Fiscal Year 2026 Budget Support Act of 2025 , December 6, 2025, https://lims.dccouncil.gov/Legislation/B26-0265 .")

The Home Rule Act directs the mayor to transmit the federal portion of the budget to the President, who forwards it to Congress for review, possible modification, and approval through the annual appropriations process. Under the act, the chair of the DC council is required to submit the local portion of the budget to the Speaker of the House of Representatives and the President of the Senate, for review by Congress.[15](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn15 "87 Stat. 801; D.C. Official Code § 1–204. 46.") In practice, the DC government transmits the full budget and financial plan as one package to Congress and the President, concurrently.[16](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn16 "For example, see DC Government, Fiscal Year 2026 Approved Budget and Financial Plan , October 3, 2025 , https://cfo.dc.gov/page/annual-operating-budget-and-capital-plan .")

Since enactment of [P.L. 109-115](https://www.congress.gov/law/109/pub/115) for FY2006 appropriations, Congress has typically included budgetary approvals, appropriations of federal payments, and general provisions for DC in a multi-agency appropriations bill. In FY2006 and FY2007, DC appropriations were included in Transportation, Treasury, Housing and Urban Development, the Judiciary, the District of Columbia, and Independent Agencies appropriations bills ([P.L. 109-115](https://www.congress.gov/law/109/pub/115) and [P.L. 110-5](https://www.congress.gov/law/110/pub/5), respectively). Since FY2008, DC appropriations have been included in the Financial Services and General Government appropriations bill (FSGG). Before FY2006, DC appropriations were provided by the House and the Senate in a stand-alone bill.

Local Budget Autonomy

In 2013, DC enacted the Local Budget Autonomy Amendment Act of 2012 (D.C. Law 19-321).[17](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn17 "D.C. Law 19-321, Local Budget Autonomy Amendment Act of 2012 , https://lims.dccouncil.gov/Legislation/B19-0993 .") Prior to 2013, some DC political leaders had expressed concern about the effect of delays in the annual federal appropriations process on the ability of the DC government to manage its affairs and deliver public services.[18](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn18 "DC Council, Report on Bill 19-993, \"Local Budget Autonomy Act of 2012 , December 4, 2012, https://lims.dccouncil.gov/downloads/LIMS/26749/Committee_Report/B19-0993-CommitteeReport1.pdf?Id=60330 .") The local act amended DC's home rule charter to allow for enactment of DC's local budget after a 30-day congressional review period (also known as a layover period), similar to most other DC laws.[19](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn19 "For an overview of the regular DC lawmaking process and the c ongressional role, see CRS Report R47927, District of Columbia Local Lawmaking and Congressional Authority , by Joseph V. Jaroscak, Christopher M. Davis, and Ben Leubsdorf .") The DC Board of Elections placed the proposed charter amendment on an April 23, 2013, ballot. DC voters approved the local budget autonomy charter amendment with 83% of the vote in support of the amendment.[20](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn20 "DC Council, Council's Battle for Budget Autonomy Wins Substantial Victory for DC Taxpayers , March 23, 2016, https://dccouncil.gov/councils-battle-budget-autonomy-wins-substantial-victory-dc-taxpayers/ .")

The act faced legal and federal legislative challenges.[21](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn21 "Council of the District of Columbia v. Jeffrey S. Dewitt, 144 Daily Wash. L. Rptr. 893 (D.C. Super. Ct. March 18, 2016).") A 2016 DC Superior Court ruling upheld the act.[22](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn22 "\"Superior Court Judge Sides with D.C. Lawmakers on Control of Locally-Raised Dollars,\" Washington Post , March 18, 2016, https://www.washingtonpost.com/local/public-safety/superior-court-judge-sides-with-dc-lawmakers-on-control-of-locally-raised-dollars/2016/03/18/1059c6e6-ed55-11e5-a6f3-21ccdbc5f74e_story.html .") The DC government has observed the act in its budget process since 2016.[23](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn23 "Information provided by the District of Columbia Office of the Chief Financial Officer, 2020.") On the other hand, the Government Accountability Office, in a 2016 analysis, contended that the act had "no legal standing" and suggested that determining the level of DC's local budget autonomy "is a matter within Congress's discretion under its constitutional powers."[24](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn24 "U.S. Government Accountability Office, Testimony Before the Subcommittee on Government Operations, Committee on Oversight and Government Reform, House of Representatives: District of Columbia Local Budget Autonomy Amendment Act of 2012, GAO-16-663T, May 12, 2016, https://www.gao.gov/assets/680/677232.pdf .") Congress has continued to exercise its authority to approve, modify, or disapprove the DC budget, including planned expenditures of locally generated revenues, through the regular federal appropriations process.[25](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn25 "For example, see P.L. 119-75 , Division E, Title IV.")

General Provisions

FSGG appropriations acts typically include a series of general provisions pertaining to federal payments and other sources of DC budgetary funding. These provisions can be grouped into several distinct but overlapping categories, with the most predominant being provisions related to fiscal and budgetary directives and controls. Other provisions include administrative directives and controls, limitations on lobbying for statehood or congressional voting representation, congressional oversight, and congressionally established restrictions or prohibitions on expenditures related to social policy. [39](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn39 "Such provisions are limitations (also known as limitation provisions or, more colloquially, riders ) that restrict/prohibit the use of funds for certain purposes. For information on limitations, see CRS Report R41634, Limitations in Appropriations Measures: An Overview of Procedural Issues , by James V. Saturno .")

Division E, Title VIII of [P.L. 119-75](https://www.congress.gov/law/119/pub/75) included 21 general provisions pertaining to local financial, administrative, and policy matters in DC. This section provides an overview on a selection of proposed and enacted general provisions pertaining to DC. For a full list of the general provisions enacted in Division E, Title VIII of [P.L. 119-75](https://www.congress.gov/law/119/pub/75), with complete legislative text, see [**Table A-1**](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#_Ref242954316).

Abortion Services

The use of public funding for abortion services in DC is a perennial issue debated in Congress during annual deliberations on DC appropriations.[40](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn40 "Since 1979, with the passage of the District of Columbia Appropriations Act of 1980 ( P.L. 96-93 ; 93 Stat. 719), Congress has limited or prohibited the use of public funds for abortion services for District residents .") President Trump's FY2026 budget request proposed to continue a general provision prohibiting the use of funds by the DC government for abortion services, with certain exceptions. [H.R. 5166](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119/hr/5166) and [S. 3290](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119/s/3290) also would have continued a provision prohibiting the use of funds for abortion services, with certain exceptions. As enacted, Division E, Title VIII, Section 810 of [P.L. 119-75](https://www.congress.gov/law/119/pub/75) prohibits the use of any funds by the DC government to provide abortion services, with certain exceptions, stating:

No funds available for obligation or expenditure by the District of Columbia government under any authority shall be expended for any abortion except where the life of the mother would be endangered if the fetus were carried to term or where the pregnancy is the result of an act of rape or incest.

DC Voting Representation in Congress

For several years, the general provisions of annual appropriation acts have prohibited the DC government from using federal or local funds to lobby for voting representation in Congress, including statehood.[41](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn41 "Several similar provisions date back to the 1980s and 1990s.") Division E, Title VIII, Section 810 of [P.L. 119-75](https://www.congress.gov/law/119/pub/75) included three specific provisions in sections 802, 804, and 806(a) limiting related activities:

* 1. SEC. 802. None of the Federal funds provided in this Act shall be used for publicity or propaganda purposes or implementation of any policy including boycott designed to support or defeat legislation pending before Congress or any State legislature.
* 2. SEC. 804. None of the Federal funds provided in this Act may be used by the District of Columbia to provide for salaries, expenses, or other costs associated with the offices of United States Senator or United States Representative under section 4(d) of the District of Columbia Statehood Constitutional Convention Initiatives of 1979 (D.C. Law 3–171; D.C. Official Code, sec. 1–123).
* 3. SEC. 806. (a) None of the Federal funds contained in this Act may be used by the District of Columbia Attorney General or any other officer or entity of the District government to provide assistance for any petition drive or civil action which seeks to require Congress to provide for voting representation in Congress for the District of Columbia.

These provisions were included in the President's budget request, [H.R. 5166](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119/hr/5166), and [S. 3290](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119/s/3290).

Needle Exchange

Addressing the spread of HIV and AIDS among intravenous drug users has been a policy issue of ongoing debate in congressional appropriations for DC.[42](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn42 "Delegate Eleanor Holmes Norton, \"HIV Progress in D.C. Accelerated by Federal Payments Norton Secures and Her Removal of the Needle Exchange Rider,\" press release, August 21, 2020, https://norton.house.gov/media-center/press-releases/hiv-progress-in-dc-accelerated-by-federal-payments-norton-secures-and .") Some appropriations acts have included provisions to prohibit or restrict the use of funds to establish a needle exchange program designed to reduce the spread of HIV and AIDS among users of illegal drugs.

The prohibition on the use of both federal and local funds for a needle exchange program was first approved by Congress as Section 170 of the District of Columbia Appropriations Act, 1999 (Division A of [P.L. 105-277](https://www.congress.gov/law/105/pub/277)). The FY1999 act did allow private funding of needle exchange programs. The Financial Services and General Government Appropriations Act, 2008 (Division D, Title VIII of [P.L. 110-161](https://www.congress.gov/law/110/pub/161)), contained language that further modified the needle exchange provision included in previous appropriations acts. This act allowed the use of local (but not federal) funds for a needle exchange program, a provision that has been continued in subsequent fiscal years. Under the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2010 (Division C, Title VIII of [P.L. 111-117](https://www.congress.gov/law/111/pub/117)), the provision was further modified to prohibit the use of federal funds in locations deemed by local professionals in public health or law enforcement to be "inappropriate" for needle exchange.

The President's FY2026 budget request proposed to continue a provision restricting the use of federal funds for the distribution of needles or syringes "for the purpose of preventing the spread of blood borne pathogens in any location that has been determined by the local public health or local law enforcement authorities to be inappropriate for such distribution." [H.R. 5166](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119/hr/5166) and [S. 3290](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119/s/3290) also would have continued this provision. As enacted, Division E, Title VIII, Section 807 of [P.L. 119-75](https://www.congress.gov/law/119/pub/75) continued the provision restricting the use of federal funds for this use, as stated below:

None of the Federal funds contained in this Act may be used to distribute any needle or syringe for the purpose of preventing the spread of blood borne pathogens in any location that has been determined by the local public health or local law enforcement authorities to be inappropriate for such distribution.

Schedule I Substances

Several general provisions included in appropriations acts have restricted or prohibited the DC government from implementing local laws related to the legalization or decriminalization of schedule I controlled substances, including marijuana.[43](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn43 "For a definition of schedule I substances, see 21 U.S.C. §812.") These provisions have varied depending on the legislation that they were designed to restrict, ranging from legalization to decriminalization of such substances for medical or recreational purposes.[44](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn44 "In 1998, District of Columbia voters approved Initiative 59, which allowed the use of medical marijuana to assist persons suffering from debilitating health conditions and diseases, includin g cancer and HIV infection. C ertification and implementation of the initiative, however, were delayed over a decade by Congress due to the passage of the \"Barr Amendment,\" which, in a ser ies of DC appropriations acts, prohibited the use of appropriated funds to conduct any ballot initiative that sought...")

In 2014, the DC council passed the Marijuana Possession Decriminalization Amendment Act of 2014.[45](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn45 "District of Columbia Council, Marijuana Possession Decriminalization Amendment Act of 2014 , July 2014, https://lims.dccouncil.gov/Legislation/B20-1064 .") The act decriminalized the possession of small amounts of marijuana by making such activity a civil violation subject to a civil fine of $25.[46](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn46 "Aaron C. Davis, \" D.C. Council Votes to Eliminate Jail Time for Marijuana Possession ,\" Washington Post , March 5, 2014.") The act went into effect in July 2014. Also in 2014, almost 65% of DC voters voted to approve Initiative 71 to legalize the possession, growth, and exchange of certain amounts of marijuana among individuals aged 21 and older in DC.[47](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn47 "District of Columbia Council, Legalization of Possession of Minimal Amounts of Marijuana for Personal Use Initiative of 2014 , February 2015, https://lims.dccouncil.gov/Legislation/B20-1064 .") Subsequently, the Consolidated and Further Continuing Appropriations Act, 2015 ([P.L. 113-235](https://www.congress.gov/law/113/pub/235)), included a general provision prohibiting the use of funds contained in the act to carry out such laws or regulations. Similar provisions have been included in subsequent appropriations acts. In November 2020, approximately 76% of DC voters voted to approve ballot Initiative 81, to decriminalize some psychedelic plants and fungi.[48](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn48 "Justin Wm. Moyer, \"D.C. Voters Approve Ballot Question to Decriminalize Psychedelic Mushrooms,\" Washington Post , November 3, 2020, https://www.washingtonpost.com/local/dc-politics/dc-magic-mushrooms-result/2020/11/03/bb929e86-1abc-11eb-bb35-2dcfdab0a345_story.html .")

The President's budget request, [H.R. 5166](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119/hr/5166), and [S. 3290](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119/s/3290) included provisions that would continue to prohibit the use of federal or local funds to enact or implement the legalization or decriminalization of schedule I substances. As enacted, Division E, Title VIII, Section 809 of [P.L. 119-75](https://www.congress.gov/law/119/pub/75) continued the provision prohibiting the use of federal and local funds for the legalization or decriminalization of schedule I substances in DC, as stated below:

(a) None of the Federal funds contained in this Act may be used to enact or carry out any law, rule, or regulation to legalize or otherwise reduce penalties associated with the possession, use, or distribution of any schedule I substance under the Controlled Substances Act (21 U.S.C. 801 et seq.) or any tetrahydrocannabinols derivative.

(b) No funds available for obligation or expenditure by the District of Columbia government under any authority may be used to enact any law, rule, or regulation to legalize or otherwise reduce penalties associated with the possession, use, or distribution of any schedule I substance under the Controlled Substances Act (21 U.S.C. 801 et seq.) or any tetrahydrocannabinols derivative for recreational purposes.

Local Government Operations During a Lapse in Federal Funding

The act included a provision that would authorize DC's use of locally generated revenues in the event of a shutdown during FY2027 (sometimes referred to as a shutdown provision). Additionally, if the federal government were to operate under a continuing resolution at any point in FY2027, Section 817 of Division E provides DC with the authority to expend local revenues at FY2027 budgeted levels during that period, unless a provision in the continuing resolution explicitly states otherwise.

Concluding Observations

As noted above, Congress maintains plenary authority over DC legislation and budgets, as granted under the U.S. Constitution. Some Members of Congress have introduced bills that would codify changes to DC's local budget authority and process. For example, the District of Columbia Local Budget Autonomy Act of 2012 ([S. 2345](https://www.congress.gov/bill/112/s/2345)) would have amended the Home Rule Act to expand DC's authority over the local budget process. A provision in the Make America Secure and Prosperous Appropriations Act, 2018 ([H.R. 3354](https://www.congress.gov/bill/115/hr/3354), Title VIII, Section 817), would have formally repealed the Local Budget Autonomy Act of 2012.

Other, broader proposals to grant DC statehood or retrocede parts of the current federal district to Maryland would modify the local governing structure and likely reduce the federal role in the DC budget process.[49](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn49 "For an overview of such proposals, see CRS In Focus IF11443, District of Columbia Voting Representation in Congress: Overview of Proposals , by Joseph V. Jaroscak .") Some Members of Congress have also proposed repealing the Home Rule Act, which could potentially lead to an expansion of congressional involvement in the local budget process, compared to its current role.[50](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49497.html#fn50 "For an example of this type of proposal, see the Bringing Oversight to Washington and Safety to Every Resident Act of the 119 th Congress, H.R. 1089 and S. 440 .")
