Summary

While residents of rural areas are potentially eligible to participate in the full range of housing programs administered by the federal government, the U.S. Department of Agriculture's (USDA's) Rural Housing Service (RHS) administers several housing programs targeted specifically to residents of rural communities. These programs are authorized under Title V of the Housing Act of 1949, as amended, and are usually referred to by the section of that act under which they are authorized. The definition of *rural* for the purposes of USDA housing programs is established in statute and is based on population thresholds and, in some cases, other characteristics.

Programs administered by RHS support both single-family and multifamily housing, generally through grants, direct loans, or loan guarantees. The programs are primarily targeted toward lower-income residents of rural areas; specific income thresholds and other eligibility criteria vary by program. Single-family programs include loans to purchase homes, loans and grants to repair homes, and grants to support self-help housing programs (i.e., programs where families contribute their own labor to the construction of their homes). Multifamily programs include loans to develop or rehabilitate rental housing, loans and grants to finance housing for farm laborers, and associated rental assistance.

There has long been concern about preserving the affordability of the existing stock of rental housing properties subsidized by some RHS programs. This is because when USDA mortgages on rural rental properties mature or are paid off early, affordability restrictions (agreed to by property owners benefitting from favorable USDA mortgage terms) end and the properties are eligible to convert to market-rate housing. Various policies to preserve USDA-assisted affordable rental properties have been implemented, several new policies were enacted in the 119th Congress as a part of the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act ([P.L. 119-101](http://www.congress.gov/law/119/pub/101)), and additional policies have been proposed.

Background

The U.S. Department of Agriculture's (USDA's) Rural Housing Service (RHS), part of USDA's Rural Development mission area, administers a number of federal housing programs[1](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn1 "The Rural Housing Service also administers several rural community facilities programs; those programs are not discussed in this report. For more information on the community facilities programs, see https://www.rd.usda.gov/programs-services/community-facilities .") designed to support homeownership or rental housing primarily for lower-income families in rural areas.[2](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn2 "The definitions of rural and rural area for the purposes of USDA rural housing progr ams are different than those used by the U.S. Census Bureau and are established in statute, based on population thresholds and other characteristics such as being \"rural in character\" or having a \"serious lack of mortgage credit for lower and moderate income families.\" See 42 U . S . C . § 1490. For a map of eligible and ineligible areas, see https://eligibility.sc.egov.usda.gov/eligibility/welc...") These programs are authorized by Title V of the Housing Act of 1949 (P.L. 81-171; 42 U.S.C. §§1471 et seq.), as amended, and are usually referred to by the section of that act under which they are authorized.

RHS housing programs are under the jurisdiction of the Senate Committee on Banking, Housing, and Urban Affairs and the House Committee on Financial Services. Appropriations for the RHS programs are provided through the annual appropriations acts that provide funding for the USDA, which are under the jurisdiction of the Agriculture, Rural Development, Food and Drug Administration, and Related Agencies subcommittees of the Senate and the House Appropriations Committees.

RHS housing programs are primarily grant and loan programs, including both guaranteed and direct loans.[3](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn3 "Like other government-insured or -guaranteed mortgages, single-family and multifamily mortgages guaranteed by RHS are eligible to be included in mortgage-backed securities guaranteed by Ginnie Mae. For more information on Ginnie Mae's role in facilitating a secondary mortgage market for government-backed mortgages, see https://www.ginniemae.gov/about_us/who_we_are/Pages/funding_government_lending.aspx .") For grant programs, annual appropriations acts specify the amount available for a program each year. For loan programs, annual appropriations acts specify a total dollar amount of loans that can be made or guaranteed (the loan authorization level), as well as provide any appropriations necessary to cover the expected costs of those loans (credit subsidy/budget authority).

RHS operates through a national office with staff in Washington, DC, and St. Louis, MO,[4](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn4 "In June 2026, USDA Rural Development announced a reorganization plan that included relocating additional RHS staff from Washington, DC , to St. Louis to \"to centralize and consolidate certain loan origination, processing, and servicing functions.\" For more information, see https://www.rd.usda.gov/reorganization .") and within Rural Development (RD) state offices, led by presidentially appointed state directors.[5](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn5 "Information on states offices is available at https://www.rd.usda.gov/about-rd/state-offices .") These state offices administer the federal assistance programs, including accepting and processing applications for direct loans and grants.

While the RHS housing programs are targeted specifically toward rural areas, rural communities and residents may also benefit from other federal housing programs, most of which are administered by the Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD).[6](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn6 "For an overview of the major federal housing assistance programs, see CRS Report RL34591, Overview of Federal Housing Assistance Programs and Policy .") For example, among other things:

* qualified individuals in rural areas can apply for mortgages insured by HUD's Federal Housing Administration (FHA) or for HUD Housing Choice Vouchers, or they may live in other HUD-assisted rental housing;
* housing developers in rural areas can compete for Low-Income Housing Tax Credits (a U.S. Department of the Treasury program administered by states); and
* rural communities can benefit from HUD grant funds provided to states (such as Community Development Block Grants).

In addition, some specific housing programs outside of the RHS programs may be largely used in rural areas, such as those that provide funding to Indian tribes.[7](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn7 "Federal funding for housing in tribal areas is provid ed primarily through HUD. While RHS programs can be used in tribal areas, there have been various efforts and proposals to increase their use . These efforts include the Native Community Development Financial Institutions Relending Program, discussed in the \" Native Community Development Financial Institutions (CDFI) Relending Program \" section of this report .")

RHS programs may be used in conjunction with these other federal program resources. For example, a multifamily rural housing property may have an RHS loan and a HUD rental assistance contract and apply to receive an allocation of Low-Income Housing Tax Credits to finance a redevelopment.

Defining *Rural*

There are numerous definitions of *rural* used for different policies, programs, and purposes across the federal government.[8](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn8 "See, for example, USDA Economic Research Service, \"Rural Classifications: What Is Rural? , \" updated February 9, 2026, https://www.ers.usda.gov/topics/rural-economy-population/rural-classifications/what-is-rural ; and Alexander Hermann and Whitney Airgood-Obrycki, \" In Search of Rural: How Varying Definitions Shape Housing Researach , \" Joint Center for Housing Studies of Harvard University, February 2024, https://www.jchs.harvard.edu/sites/default/files/research/files/har...") The definitions of *rural* and *rural area* for the purposes of USDA rural housing programs are established in statute. They are based on population thresholds and, in some cases, other characteristics. Specifically

the terms "rural" and "rural area" mean any open country, or any place, town, village, or city which is not [ ... ][9](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn9 "The statute includes exceptions in th e cases of Pajaro in California and Gua dalupe in Arizona.") part of or associated with an urban area and which (1) has a population not in excess of 2,500 inhabitants, or (2) has a population in excess of 2,500 but not in excess of 10,000 if it is rural in character, or (3) has a population in excess of 10,000 but not in excess of 20,000, and (A) is not contained within a standard metropolitan statistical area, and (B) has a serious lack of mortgage credit for lower and moderate-income families, as determined by the Secretary and the Secretary of Housing and Urban Development.[10](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn10 "42 U.S.C. §1490")

Over the years, Congress has amended this definition to grandfather certain communities that have had population growth that puts them at risk of losing eligibility for participation in USDA rural housing programs. Most recently, the statutory definition was amended in 2018 ([P.L. 115-334](https://www.congress.gov/law/115/pub/334)) to extend these grandfather provisions until receipt of data from the 2030 decennial census. Specifically

any area classified as "rural" or a "rural area" prior to October 1, 1990, and determined not to be "rural" or a "rural area" as a result of data received from or after the 1990, 2000, 2010, or 2020 decennial census, and any area deemed to be a "rural area" for purposes of this subchapter under any other provision of law at any time during the period beginning January 1, 2000, and ending December 31, 2020, shall continue to be so classified until the receipt of data from the decennial census in the year 2030, if such area has a population in excess of 10,000 but not in excess of 35,000, is rural in character, and has a serious lack of mortgage credit for lower and moderate-income families.[11](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn11 "42 U.S.C. §1490")

Housing Characteristics in Rural Communities

While rural areas experience many of the same housing challenges that affect the rest of the country, certain broad characteristics of housing in rural areas differ from housing in the United States as a whole. For example, as shown in [**Table 1**](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#_Ref235196874):

* homeownership rates are higher;
* single-family detached homes account for a larger share of the housing stock;
* manufactured housing is more common;
* A larger percentage of rental housing is in single-family properties (those with one to four units) and manufactured homes, while a smaller percentage of rental housing is in multifamily properties (those with five or more units);
* rural areas tend to have lower incomes; however, house prices and rents also tend to be lower, leading to lower rates of housing cost burden.

These data are drawn from the U.S. Census Bureau's American Community Survey, which uses a different definition of *rural* than the one used for RHS rural housing programs.[16](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn16 "The Census Bureau defines rural areas as those outside the boundaries of urban areas based on the 2020 Census. U rban area is defined as \"a statistical geographic entity consisting of a densely settled core created from census blocks and contiguous qualifying territory that together have at least 2,000 housing units or 5,000 persons.\" Rural areas include \"all population, housing, and territory not included within an urban area.\" See Census Bureau, \"Urban Area Criteria for the 2020 Census...") For example, as discussed in the "[Defining Rural](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#_Ref244504742)" section of this report, the definition of *rural* used for RHS programs allows communities with higher populations to be considered rural as compared to the Census definition.

RHS Rural Housing Programs

The rural housing programs administered by USDA are authorized by the Housing Act of 1949 (P.L. 81-171), as amended. That law declared a national goal of "a decent home and a suitable living environment for every American family" and took a variety of actions related to housing. Title V of the law authorized the Secretary of Agriculture to provide financial assistance to farm owners to construct, improve, alter, repair, or replace dwellings or other farm buildings in order to provide decent, safe, and sanitary living conditions and adequate farm buildings. In the 1960s, Congress expanded eligibility for these programs to owners of other real estate in rural areas (rather than just farm owners),[19](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn19 "Section 803 of the Housing Act of 1961 (P.L. 87-70) amended Section 501 of th e Housing Act of 1949 to allow assistance to be provided \"to owners of other real estate in rural areas to enable them to provide dwellings and related facilities for their own use and buildings adequate for their farming operations \" in addition to farm owners.") and then to rural residents more generally.[20](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn20 "See Section 1001 of the Housing and Urban Development Act of 1965 (P.L. 89-117). This law also expanded eligibility to allow for the purchase of previously occupied buildings and land for building sites.") Several other financial assistance programs were added via amendments to Title V throughout the 1960s, with additional programs added in the 1980s and 1990s. In 1994, a departmental restructuring was statutorily authorized, which resulted in USDA establishing the RHS and assigning it jurisdiction over these programs.[21](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn21 "See Section 233 of the Federal Crop Insurance Reform and Department of Agriculture Reorganization Act of 1994 ( P.L. 103-354 ) , which authorized the establishment of a Rural Housing and Community Development Service that could be assigned jurisdiction over the programs authorized by Title V of the Housing Act of 1949. USDA established this office in 1995 and later that year renamed it the Rural Housing Servic e; see USDA , \" Revision of Delegations of Authority, \" 60 Federal Register 66...") Additionally, various programmatic and policy changes have been enacted in appropriations acts or other laws or implemented through regulations or other administrative mechanisms over the years. Most recently, Section 502 of the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act ([P.L. 119-101](http://www.congress.gov/law/119/pub/101)), enacted in July 2026, made changes to several rural housing programs. As of the date of this report, RHS had not fully implemented those changes. Where relevant, they are noted in this report. For more information about the act, see CRS Report R49354, [*The 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act (P.L. 119-101)*](http://www.crs.gov/Reports/R49354).

This section of the report describes RHS's rural housing programs as they are currently structured. It is organized by type of program: programs that support homeownership,[22](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn22 "Depending on the specific program, eligible properties under the homeownership programs can include property types such as manufactured housing or individual condominium units.") programs that support multifamily rental housing, and one program that can support either.

Eligibility for most RHS programs is restricted to very low- or low-income households, depending on the specific program. These terms are defined as follows:[23](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn23 "Regulatory definitions for single-family direct loan and grant programs can be found at 7 C.F.R. Section 3550.10; for single-family guaranteed loan programs at 7 C.F.R. Section 3555.10; for multifamily direct loan and grant programs at 7 C.F.R. Section 3560.11; and for multifamily guaranteed loan programs at 7 C.F.R. Section 3565.3.")

* *very low-income households* are those with incomes at or below 50% of area median income,
* *low-income households* are those with incomes at or below 80% of area median income.

A few of the RHS programs can also serve moderate-income households. *M**oderate-income* is defined differently for the purposes of homeownership versus multifamily rental housing programs:

* in the case of homeownership programs, moderate income is defined as income at or below 115% of area median income;
* in the case of multifamily rental programs, moderate income is defined as income above the low-income limit but not exceeding $5,500 more than that limit.

USDA annually publishes the dollar amounts that correspond to the income eligibility thresholds for every geography.[24](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn24 "The thresholds for the multifamily programs can be found at https://www.rd.usda.gov/files/RD-MFHLimitMap.pdf ; the thresholds for single-family direct loans can be found at https://www.rd.usda.gov/sites/default/files/RD-DirectLimitMap.pdf ; the thresholds for single-family guaranteed loans can be found at https://www.rd.usda.gov/files/rd-grhlimitmap.pdf .")

**Recent Program Activity:** In FY2025, $32.7 million was obligated for 15 Section 514 loans, and $6.7 million was obligated for 3 Section 516 grants.[93](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn93 "USDA E xplanatory Notes , 2027, Rural Housing Service, P. 311-66 .")

Section 515 Rural Multifamily Rental Housing Direct Loans

The Section 515 multifamily housing program offers direct loans for the development of new, or rehabilitation of existing, rental housing for low-income individuals and families in rural areas. The properties developed with these loans often also receive rental assistance, commonly through the Section 521 Rental Assistance Program and in some cases through HUD's Section 8 project-based rental assistance program. With rental assistance, tenants pay a maximum of 30% of their income toward rent and utilities; without assistance, tenants pay a "basic rent," which is based on the amount needed to cover expenses in the project's approved budget (including the Section 515 loan payment).[94](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn94 "7 C.F.R. §3560.203.") RHS has not issued loans for the development of new units of Section 515 housing since FY2011; instead, all loan authority each year is used to support the preservation of existing Section 515 properties, through either refinancing existing loans or facilitating purchases of existing properties by entities committed to preserving them as affordable housing.

**Statutory Authority:**Title V, Section 515 of the Housing Act of 1949; 42 U.S.C. §1485.[95](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn95 "42 U.S.C. Section 1485 authorizes the Secretary of Agriculture to make guaranteed loans under the Rural Rental Housi ng program. However, 42 U.S.C. Section 1487(b), which governs the Rural Housing Insurance Fund (the account used to fund the program), authorizes the Secretary to make direct loans under the program .")

**Financing:** This program provides direct subsidized loans to eligible borrowers at a 1% interest rate, amortized over the lesser of the economic life of the property being financed or 50 years, and repaid over a 30-year term, with the remaining balance due in a lump sum at the end of the repayment term.[96](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn96 "7 C.F.R. § 3560.67 . Until 1997, Section 515 loan terms were 40 to 50 years.")

**Eligibility Criteria:** Very low- to moderate-income families are eligible; some properties may be designated specifically for persons who are elderly. Assistance for very low-income households must be prioritized.[97](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn97 "42 U.S.C. §1485(p); 7 C.F.R. § 3560.154 .") Loans are offered to for-profit and nonprofit organizations, as well as state and local government entities and Indian tribes.[98](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn98 "42 U.S.C. §1485(b); 7 C.F.R. §3560.55.")

**Recent Program Activity:** In FY2025, $8 million was obligated for three Section 515 loans.[99](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn99 "USDA E xplanatory Notes , 2027, Rural Housing Service, P. 311-66 .")

Section 521 Rural Rental Assistance

This program provides rental assistance to Section 515 and Section 514 off-farm properties to subsidize the rents of eligible tenants. Tenants living in units with Section 521 assistance pay 30% of their income towards their rent and the program pays the property owner an amount to cover the difference between the tenant contribution and the basic rent for the unit.

**Statutory Authority:**Title V, Section 521(a)(2) of the Housing Act of 1949; 42 U.S.C. §1490a.

**Financing:** RHS makes rental payments under the terms of the rental assistance contracts it has with property owners. Funding is first directed to renewing existing rental assistance contracts. Remaining funds are then awarded for expansions of existing contracts to cover additional units in existing properties and new contracts for newly constructed or acquired properties.[100](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn100 "7 C.F.R. §3560.255.")

**Eligibility Criteria:** Owners of properties with Section 515 and Section 514 off-farm loans may request rental assistance funding from RHS to the extent appropriations are available. Tenants who are eligible to live in the properties are generally eligible for rental assistance. In cases where rental assistance in a property is limited (i.e., not all units receive rental assistance), owners must offer priority for rental assistance to very low-income tenants.[101](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn101 "7 C.F.R. §3560.257.")

Historically, a property had to have an active Section 515 or Section 514 loan in order to receive Section 521 assistance. The FY2024 appropriations law ([P.L. 118-42](https://www.congress.gov/law/118/pub/42)) provided USDA with the authority to allow certain properties to retain their Section 521 assistance upon maturation of their Section 515 or Section 514 loans, referred to as Section 521 decoupling. The pilot has been extended through annual appropriations acts.[102](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn102 "More information is available on USDA's website at https://www.rd.usda.gov/programs-services/multifamily-housing-programs/section-521-stand-alone-rental-assistance .") USDA was given permanent authority to extend Section 521 rental assistance contracts for properties with maturing mortgages by the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act ([P.L. 119-101](http://www.congress.gov/law/119/pub/101)). As of the date of this report, implementing guidance for this authority had not yet been released.

**Recent Program Activity:** In FY2025, $1.6 billion was obligated to support 212,630 Section 521 units.[103](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn103 "USDA E xplanatory Notes , 2027, Rural Housing Service, P. 311-66 .")

Multifamily Housing Preservation and Revitalization (MPR) Demonstration

The Multifamily Housing Preservation and Revitalization (MPR) program was first created as a demonstration in the FY2006 annual appropriations law ([P.L. 109-97](https://www.congress.gov/law/109/pub/97)) to support the preservation of Section 515 and Section 514 off-farm properties as affordable housing as they age or near the end of their loan terms and affordability restrictions. The demonstration program was continued in each subsequent appropriations act. The 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act ([P.L. 119-101](http://www.congress.gov/law/119/pub/101)) permanently established the program by amending the Housing Act of 1949 (42 U.S.C. §1490u).

MPR provides RHS with preservation funding tools and authorities including debt deferral, subordinate or soft second loans, 0% loans, and grants to address an existing Section 515 or Section 514 project's health and safety needs. Properties receiving this assistance agree to extended affordability periods.

**Statutory Authority:**The program was created and maintained as a demonstration program by annual appropriations acts, beginning in FY2006, until it was authorized at Title V, Section 545, of the Housing Act of 1949 by the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act ([P.L. 119-101](http://www.congress.gov/law/119/pub/101)), enacted in July 2026. The act directs RHS to begin the rulemaking process to fully implement the MPR program within 180 days of enactment, with an interim final rule to be published within one year of enactment.

**Financing:** RHS makes grants and loans, including loan modifications, to restructure the debt of existing Section 515 and Section 514 borrowers.

**Eligibility Criteria:** Owners of properties financed with Section 515 or Section 514 loans can apply for assistance; RHS maintains a waiting list for assistance.

**Recent Program Activity:** In FY2025, $1.3 million was obligated for 13 loans, and $3.8 million was obligated for eight grants under MPR.[116](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn116 "USDA Explanatory Notes, 2027, Rural Housing Service, p. 311-6 2.")

Selected Issues and Trends

Multifamily Rural Housing Preservation

Over time, the multifamily housing financed through the Section 515 and Section 514 programs may be at risk of loss to the affordable housing stock. This can happen because the RHS loan is ending through prepayment, agency enforcement actions, or natural maturation—any of which can terminate affordability restrictions, leading to tenant displacement and loss of affordable housing in a community.[123](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn123 "Not all owners have the right to prepay their loans. Congress amended the law to prohibit prepayment of loans made after December 15, 1989. ( P.L. 101-235 §206).")

Initial rural housing preservation concerns centered on owners' ability to prepay their loans and exit the programs. Up until the late 1970s, there were no restrictions on loan prepayment, and there were concerns about owners exiting the programs before the end of their 40- to 50-year loan terms, at which point affordability restrictions ended. In response, several laws were enacted to restrict borrowers' ability to prepay and to change the terms of the programs so that later loans (those made after 1989) were ineligible for prepayment. For properties at risk of prepayment, these laws directed USDA to offer incentives to owners to remain in the programs or sell to preservation-focused purchasers; enacted various notification and first purchase right requirements; and authorized and then funded rental housing vouchers, under the Section 542 program, for tenants facing displacement upon owner prepayment (or mortgage foreclosure).[124](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn124 "While the authority for Section 542 was added to the law in 1992 ( P.L. 102-550 , Title VII, §706(2)), the program was not funded until the FY2006 appropriations law.")

Over time, concerns began to grow about the physical and financial condition of aging rural multifamily assisted housing properties. A report issued by USDA in 2004 found that no Section 515 properties had adequate reserves or sufficient cash flow to make needed repairs or meet maintenance needs over time.[125](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn125 "USDA, Rural Rental Housing-Comprehensive Property Assessment and Portfolio Analysis: Final Study Report , November 2004, prepared by ICF Consulting, available at https://peerta.acf.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/public/uploaded_files/Rural%20Rental%20Housing_ICF.pdf .") These properties risk falling into physical disrepair, which can both threaten the health and safety of residents, and put the property at risk of foreclosure. In response to these concerns, the FY2006 appropriations law ([P.L. 109-97](https://www.congress.gov/law/109/pub/97)) created a new demonstration program for the preservation and revitalization of USDA multifamily rental housing properties (see the "[Multifamily Housing Preservation and Revitalization (MPR) Demonstration](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#_Ref241757109)" section earlier in this report). The MPR program is intended to preserve Section 515 and Section 514 projects by funding their physical revitalization and extending their affordable use agreements. Under MPR, RHS can restructure existing loans, using debt deferral, soft second loans, 0% loans, and grants to preserve existing properties. The program was permanently established by the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act ([P.L. 119-101](http://www.congress.gov/law/119/pub/101)).

More recently, policymakers have focused on the preservation risks posed by maturing mortgages. When Section 514 and Section 515 loans mature, their affordability restrictions end. RHS has adopted some policies to encourage owners to refinance or otherwise re-amortize their mortgages in order to prevent loan maturation and extend use agreements and to notify tenants when a loan is reaching maturation.[126](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn126 "USDA, \" Management of Loan Payoffs in Multi-Family Housing Properties ,\" unnumbered l etter, December 28, 2016, available at https://www.rd.usda.gov/files/RDUL-Payoffs.pdf .") Until recently, Section 542 vouchers were available only for rent-assisted tenants facing displacement due to owner prepayment or default, not mortgage maturation. The FY2022 appropriations law[127](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn127 "P.L. 117-103 .")—and each appropriations law thereafter—authorized RHS to offer Section 542 vouchers to all residents facing displacement, both those with and without rent assistance, including due to mortgage maturation. In July 2026, the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act ([P.L. 119-101](http://www.congress.gov/law/119/pub/101)) amended the law to make these changes permanent.

Similarly, until recently, USDA lacked authority to renew Section 521 rental assistance contracts on properties that no longer have active Section 515 or Section 514 loans, including those with maturing mortgages. In the FY2024 appropriations law ([P.L. 118-47](http://www.congress.gov/law/118/pub/47)), Congress provided RHS with limited authority to test the renewal of Section 521 contracts for certain properties upon mortgage maturation, often referred to as Section 521 decoupling. This Section 521 decoupling authority—which RHS refers to as the Stand Alone Rental Assistance demonstration[128](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn128 "For more information, see RD, \" Section 521 Stand-Alone Rental Assistance ,\" https://www.rd.usda.gov/programs-services/multifamily-housing-programs/section-521-stand-alone-rental-assistance .")—was extended by subsequent appropriations laws[129](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn129 "As of the date of this report, the demonstration was available for properties with mortgages maturing between March 9, 2024, and September 30, 2026, with the time frame having most recently been extended by P.L. 119-37.") until it was statutorily authorized in July 2026 by the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act ([P.L. 119-101](http://www.congress.gov/law/119/pub/101)).

Both Section 515 direct loans and Section 538 guaranteed loans have been used for preservation purposes, to refinance and/or fund the rehabilitation of existing Section 515 and Section 514 properties, often through the MPR process. As noted previously, since FY2012 RHS has used all its new Section 515 direct loan authority each year to support the preservation of existing Section 515 properties, either through refinancing existing loans or facilitating preservation purchases of existing properties. In recent years, half of all new Section 538 authority has been used for Section 515 property preservation. These preservation transactions have increasingly involved the use of other federal resources, including Low Income Housing Tax Credits (LIHTCs).

RHS estimates that preservation demand in the Section 515 program will exceed $30 billion between December 2025 and 2050, the equivalent of $1.2 billion per year,[130](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn130 "USDA Explanatory Notes, 2027, Rural Housing Service, p. 311-64.") which exceeds recent Section 515 and MPR funding levels (see [**Table 2**](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#_Ref93404902)). The extent to which the expanded set of tools that have recently been made available to RHS through appropriations provisions and [P.L. 119-101](http://www.congress.gov/law/119/pub/101) will be sufficient to address these preservation concerns remains to be seen.

Trends in Section 502 Direct and Guaranteed Loans

Over the past several decades, the volume of loans made through the Section 502 Guaranteed Loan Program has grown substantially larger than the Section 502 Direct Loan Program, reflecting an increased emphasis on the loan guarantee program. As shown in [**Figure 1**](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#_Ref86392182), the number of Section 502 direct loans made annually peaked in FY1976, at nearly 133,000, and then began to steadily decrease, falling further since the guaranteed loan program became permanent in the early 1990s.[131](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn131 "Section 706 of the Cranston-Gonzalez National Affordable Housing Act of 1990 ( P.L. 101-625 ) established the Section 502 Guaranteed Loan Program on a permanent basis following a temporary demonstration program in the late 1980s.") Meanwhile, the number of guaranteed loans made each year increased, peaking at nearly 163,000 in FY2013. In FY2025, USDA guaranteed about 41,000 Section 502 loans and made nearly 3,000 Section 502 direct loans.

The number of loans made through each program in a given year reflects a variety of factors. One factor is the loan authorization levels provided in annual appropriations acts, which typically authorize a larger volume of guaranteed loans than direct loans.[132](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn132 "For example, in FY2026, the Continuing Appropriations, Agriculture, Legislative Branch, Military Construction and Veterans Affairs, and Extensions Act, 2026 ( P.L. 119-37 ) authorized up to $1 billion in Section 502 direct loans and up to $25 billion in Section 502 guaranteed loans.") Housing and mortgage market conditions, such as mortgage interest rates, can also affect program activity.[133](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn133 "Housing Assistance Council, \" Home Mortgage Demand Declined in Rural America Too , \" February 8, 2023, https://ruralhome.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/rural-research-note-usda-502-guarantee-declines.pdf .")

The increased focus on guaranteed loans in part reflects the cost of each program. The guaranteed program has long had a lower cost to the government than the direct loan program, and since FY2011 the guaranteed program has charged borrowers fees that are estimated to be sufficient to cover the costs of loan defaults and therefore has not required credit subsidy appropriations. (Appropriations are still provided for salaries and expenses related to administering the program.) The direct loan program does require credit subsidy appropriations, given the population it serves and the payment subsidy it provides. In FY2026, $130.6 million in appropriations was provided to support up to $1 billion in Section 502 direct loans.

At times, there have been proposals to eliminate the Section 502 Direct Loan Program. Most recently, the FY2026 budget justification proposed no new funding for the program,[134](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn134 "USDA Explanatory Notes, 2026, Rural Housing Service, p. 31-12, https://www.usda.gov/sites/default/files/documents/31-2026-CJ-RHS.pdf .") although Congress continued to fund the program in FY2026. The FY2027 budget justification did request funding for Section 502 direct loans.[135](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn135 "USDA Explanatory Notes, 2027, Rural Housing Service, p. 31-13.") Past budget requests that proposed no funding for the direct loans have argued that technological advances have increased access to credit in rural areas and that the guaranteed loan program could sufficiently serve rural needs.[136](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn136 "For example, USDA's FY2021 budget justifications stated that \"r ural areas once isolated from easy access to credit have shrunk as internet access and use has grown. Therefore, USDA is now in a position to utilize solely the guarantee program, and still achieve the Administration's goals for rural housing at a lower cost to taxpayers.\" See 2021 USDA Congressional Justification — Rural Housing Service , p. 31-26, https://www.usda.gov/sites/default/files/documents/29rhs2021notes.pdf . Si...") Many rural housing advocates, however, argue that there is an ongoing need for the direct loan program. They maintain that the program serves many borrowers who would not otherwise be able to qualify for a mortgage, even with a guarantee. These advocates point to differences between the programs, such as the interest rate subsidy that many direct loan borrowers receive and differences in the income levels of the populations served by the direct and guaranteed programs.[137](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn137 "For example, see National Rural Housing Coalition, \"Fast Facts! Section 502 Direct Loans Open Doors to Rural Homeownership, \" https://ruralhousingcoalition.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/Section-502-Direct.pdf ; and Statement for the Record of Lance George, Director of Research and Information, Housing Assistance Council, for a hearing before the Senate Banking Committee on The State of Housing, 2023 , February 9, 2023, p. 18, https://www.banking.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/George%2...")

Program Coordination

As noted earlier, RHS programs are sometimes used in conjunction with other federal affordable housing programs, and some RHS housing programs provide similar types of assistance to that provided by non-RHS housing programs. There have been various proposals over the years to coordinate and/or streamline RHS programs and these other housing assistance resources. These have ranged from proposals encouraging or requiring USDA and HUD to coordinate in various ways to proposals to amend the law to move RHS programs to HUD and potentially integrate them with existing HUD programs.[138](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn138 "For example, i n 2018, a Trump Administration government reorganization proposal included a recommendation to move the RHS single-family and multifamily loan guarantee and rental assistance programs to HUD, arguing that such a move would help achieve operational efficiencies by consolidating similar programs under one agency . Delivering Government Solutions in the 21 st Century Reform Plan and Reorganization Recommendations , p. 35, https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2018...") Proposals to move USDA rural housing programs to HUD, in particular, have been met with opposition from rural housing stakeholders, many of whom argue that USDA's expertise on rural issues and its network of local offices provide advantages in administering rural housing programs and ensuring that they reach rural residents.[139](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn139 "For example, see U.S. Congress, House Committee on Financial Services, Subcommittee on Housing, Community Development, and Insurance, The Affordable Housing Crisis in Rural America: Assessing the Federal Response , 116 th Cong., 1 st sess., April 2, 2019, Serial No. 116-13 (GPO, 2019), https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-116hhrg37396/html/CHRG-116hhrg37396.htm .")

Most recently, the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act ([P.L. 119-101](http://www.congress.gov/law/119/pub/101)) included a provision requiring USDA and HUD to enter into a memorandum of understanding to review and potentially revise the environmental review process and requirements across the two agencies and to explore the feasibility of joint physical inspections for properties assisted by both agencies. In late September 2026, USDA announced that it had entered into that required agreement with HUD.[140](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R47044.html#fn140 "USDA , \"USDA and HUD Historic MOU Establishes a Framework for Collaboration Across Federal Housing Programs,\" press release, September 29, 2026, https://www.usda.gov/about-usda/news/press-releases/2026/09/29/usda-and-hud-historic-mou-establishes-framework-collaboration-across-federal-housing-programs .")

The act also requires HUD and USDA to submit a report to Congress, not later than one year after enactment (July 11, 2027), that includes recommendations for legislative, regulatory, or administrative actions to improve the efficiency and effectiveness of housing projects funded by both agencies.
