oceanographic research vessels that are owned and operated or leased by federal departments and
agencies. Federally funded projects have included the mapping of the seafloor, which aids in the
delineation of the outer limits of U.S. maritime boundaries; the identification and study of marine
resources for protection, management, or exploitation; ocean exploration; U.S. Arctic and
Antarctic research; and U.S. ocean-based research broadly, including the installation, removal, and maintenance of U.S.
ocean observation networks. Some of these activities also contribute to various national security objectives. At issue for
Congress is understanding the current capacity of the federal oceanographic fleet and its future capability to support ocean
research and related national security priorities in a timely and cost-effective manner.

In March 2016, the Interagency Working Group on Facilities and Infrastructure (IWG-FI) published a federal oceanographic
research vessel status report entitled Federal Fleet Status Report: Current Capacity and Near-Term Priorities. In this report,
the IWG-FI inventoried the oceanographic research vessels that composed the federal fleet at the end of FY2015. The 2016
federal fleet status report also provided an outlook of the fleet’s capacity through FY2030 and discussed the potential impacts
associated with changes to capacity. The IWG-FI, a non-congressionally mandated working group that is cochaired by the
National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) and the Department of Navy, has not published a federal fleet
status report since 2016.

The 2016 federal fleet status report included vessels owned and operated or leased by NOAA, the Department of the Navy,
the National Science Foundation, and the U.S. Coast Guard. Since the publication of the 2016 fleet status report, several
oceanographic research vessels have retired from the fleet, entered into the fleet, or required maintenance to extend their
service lives. The IWG-FI began preparing a new updated federal fleet status report in 2023. In 2024, NOAA acknowledged
that an update to the 2016 federal fleet status report was being prepared, but the agency provided no timeline for its
publication. To date, the 2016 report remains the most comprehensive publicly available assessment of the federal
oceanographic fleet.

Congressional decisionmaking on the federal oceanographic fleet can substantially affect federal agencies’ abilities to fulfill
their missions (e.g., science and research, education, and navigation safety) and the fleet’s ability to address certain national
security concerns. Recurring status reports about the federal oceanographic fleet could inform congressional deliberation over
and decisions related to the fleet. Congress could pursue several options for mandating reporting on the federal fleet status; in
doing so, it could specify the report’s contents. For example, Congress could direct the report to inventory the fleet at the
time of publication; provide additional details about agencies’ future plans, including funding needs to maintain, expand, or
reduce the fleet’s size to meet evolving congressional priorities; or both. Alternatively, Congress could choose to maintain
the status quo and defer to the executive branch on whether, and if so when and how, to update the federal fleet status report.

Given that oceanographic research vessels are a shared federal asset, improved collaboration among federal agencies might
allow the government to reduce the number of vessels in the fleet and thus reduce operational costs. Congress has directed
federal agencies to increase their use of uncrewed marine systems (UMSs) as a cost-cutting measure. While deploying a
UMS may cost less than deploying an oceanographic research vessel, some UMSs would need to be deployed from a vessel.

Congress also may be interested in understanding how the size, capabilities, and utilization of the U.S. oceanographic
research fleet compare to the fleets of foreign governments (e.g., the People’s Republic of China [PRC] and/or Russia). Some
Members of Congress are concerned about the PRC’s expanding global deployment of its state-owned fleet of oceanographic
research vessels. Several stakeholders have expressed concerns about how data collected by PRC oceanographic research
vessels may present national security concerns, as these data have numerous applications, including for military use.
Congress could examine whether the current U.S. oceanographic research fleet is capable of collecting these types of data
and whether the United States should consider oceanographic research vessels for national security objectives, in addition to
research activities. Such an examination may elucidate congressional priorities for the renewal of the federal oceanographic
research fleet.

Introduction

The federal fleet of oceanographic research vessels (hereinafter federal oceanographic fleet)
serves as a primary tool for advancing knowledge of the global ocean system and the Great
Lakes.1 Some federal departments and agencies have referred to the federal oceanographic fleet
as “critical national infrastructure,” as it supports federal research and operational marine
missions (see the text box below).2 For example, data collected by the fleet help address evolving
national ocean policy priorities, such as mapping the seabed and identifying marine resources for
protection, management, or exploitation.3 In general, the fleet supports federal agency operations,
surveys, and research in U.S. ocean waters, the Great Lakes, and the high seas (i.e., international
waters).

Congress has defined an oceanographic research vessel in statute as

        a vessel that the Secretary [of the department in which the Coast Guard is operating] finds
           is being employed only in instruction in oceanography or limnology, or both, or only in
        oceanographic or limnological research, including studies about the sea such as seismic,
         gravity meter, and magnetic exploration and other marine geophysical or geological
         surveys, atmospheric research, and biological research.4

Furthermore, Congress determined that oceanographic research vessels are not to be considered
passenger vessels, meaning that they carry scientists, not passengers, and are not to be associated
with trade or commerce.5 For example, oceanographic research vessels carry science equipment,
not cargo, and fishing activities on board are for research purposes, not for commercial sale.

The Interagency Working Group on Facilities and Infrastructure (IWG-FI) has advised the
National Science and Technology Council’s Subcommittee on Ocean Science and Technology on
policies, procedures, and plans relating to oceanographic facility use, upgrades, and investments.6
IWG-FI is cochaired by representatives from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric
Administration (NOAA) and the Department of the Navy. The IWG-FI has published inventories
of the federal oceanographic fleet and provided information regarding the fleet’s aging dynamics.

Background on Federal Fleet of Oceanographic
Research Vessels

The federal oceanographic fleet is composed of vessels that are owned and operated or leased by
federal departments and agencies. The fleet is primarily composed of oceanographic research
vessels. Other types of vessels that are included in the fleet, such as U.S. Coast Guard (USCG)
polar icebreakers, support oceanographic research objectives. Some agency-owned vessels in the
federal oceanographic fleet are operated by U.S. universities and laboratories under the
University-National Oceanographic Laboratory System (UNOLS); this subset of vessels is
collectively known as the U.S. Academic Research Fleet (ARF).10 The ARF also includes vessels
owned and operated by U.S. universities and laboratories.11 The ARF is a federal asset of
“community-accessible” vessels made available for qualified private investigators and all
government agencies that need to conduct oceanographic research to support their missions.12

Vessels in the federal oceanographic fleet are typically greater than 130 feet in length and span
three classes of ships.

    1.  Global Class ships are the largest and most capable ships in the federal
       oceanographic fleet, as they can work worldwide with large scientific parties and
       have the longest endurance (i.e., amount of time a vessel can operate at sea).
    2.  Ocean (Intermediate) Class ships, slightly smaller than Global Class ships,
        typically work within an ocean basin and have slightly shorter endurance
       compared with Global Class ships.
    3.  Regional Class ships, smaller than Ocean Class ships, are optimized for
        operation in coastal waters, bays, and estuaries.13

Other federal agencies classify their vessels in different ways. For example, NOAA uses NOAA
Vessel (N/V) Class A through N/V Class D to classify its fleet based on the vessels’ primary
missions.14 The Navy classifies its oceanographic research vessels as Auxiliary General
Oceanographic Research (AGOR) ships. N/V Class A ships and AGOR ships are equivalent to
Global Class ships, as they are capable of oceanographic science and data collection in coastal
and deep ocean areas.15

In addition to these classes, oceanographic research vessels can be divided into research vessels
and survey vessels. In general, research vessels carry a broad array of scientific instrumentation
and have laboratories equipped with analytical equipment and computers that allow for
preliminary data analysis and sample storage. Survey vessels generally acquire a wide range of
data (including oceanographic, hydrographic, fisheries‐stock assessment, and ecosystem data) in
direct support of resource management and monitoring programs. Most survey ships are designed

Overview of the 2016 Federal Fleet Status Report

The 2016 federal fleet status report provided an update to the 2013 federal fleet status report.17 To
date, the 2016 report provides the most recent comprehensive public assessment of the federal
oceanographic fleet.

The IWG-FI provided an inventory of the oceanographic research vessels in the federal fleet at
the end of FY2015 in the 2016 federal fleet status report. The IWG-FI included the NOAA
research and survey fleet, some USCG polar icebreakers, vessels leased by the National Science
Foundation (NSF), and the ARF—which includes vessels owned by NSF and the Department of
the Navy’s Office of Naval Research (ONR).18 UNOLS provides a forum for the research and
education community and the federal government to work cooperatively on oceanographic
research, and schedules the research projects to take place on most NSF ships and some Navy,
NOAA, and USCG ships in the federal oceanographic fleet.19 While the 2013 federal fleet status
report included military Auxiliary General Surveying (AGS) ships as well as vessels owned and
operated by the Environmental Protection Agency, the 2016 report excluded them.20

The 2016 federal fleet status report inventoried the capacity of the federal oceanographic fleet at
the close of FY2015 and provided information about capacity gaps.21 According to the IWG-FI,
capacity refers to the fleet’s ability to support federally funded projects in a “timely and cost-
effective manner.”22 The IWG-FI determines capacity by considering the following aspects of the
fleet:
    •   total operating budget;
    •   total number of ships (or number of ships in a particular class of ships);
    •   each ship’s design, size, range, and endurance;
    •   total number of available science berths;
    •   ships’ mission configurations; and
    •   the federal oceanographic fleet’s geographic distribution.

The 2016 federal fleet status report also projected the fleet’s future capacity outlook. Specifically,
the report identified that at the end of FY2015 the fleet had 35 ships and projected that with
congressional appropriations the fleet size would be “22 ships by FY 2030 compared to 18 ships

Federal Departments and Agencies with
Oceanographic Research Vessels

According to the 2016 federal fleet status report, the federal oceanographic fleet is composed of
vessels owned and operated or leased by NOAA, NSF, the Department of the Navy, and USCG.
Of the 35 vessels listed in the report, 16 were owned by NOAA, 6 were owned by ONR, 5 were
owned by NSF, 2 were owned by USCG, and the 6 remaining were owned by nonfederal entities
(as part of the ARF or leased by NSF).26

Ships operated by federal departments and agencies are designed to meet specific statutory
requirements.27 Some federal departments and agencies may use oceanographic research vessels
owned by other federal agencies to fulfill aspects of their missions. For example, the National
Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) has used federal oceanographic fleet ship time to
support the calibration of space-based sensors and the validation of space-based data products.
Between 2003 and 2013, NASA used approximately six months of ship time per year for its
ocean research activities.28

National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration

Congress has directed the Secretary of Commerce, acting through the Assistant Administrator of
NOAA for Marine and Aviation Operations, to operate and maintain a fleet of vessels that would
meet the mission and functions of NOAA, and authorized appropriations for such purposes.29 In
this context, NOAA’s mission includes safe navigation, weather and climate services, hazard
detection and warning, and ocean exploration, as well as surveying living marine resources to
inform their protection and management. NOAA owns a variety of vessel types, ranging from
large oceanographic research vessels to smaller ships, to accomplish these activities.30

As of FY2015, NOAA owned 16 research vessels. In 2019, NOAA decommissioned Research
Vessel (R/V) Hi’ialakai, an Ocean Class ship, due to structural issues, including extensive
corrosion, pipe failures, and propulsion problems.31 As of the date of this report, the agency states
that it “operates a fleet of 15 research and survey vessels and is in the process of building
more.”32 The average age of these 15 vessels is older than 30 years. NOAA anticipates that six of
the vessels will likely reach the end of their service lives by 2030.

Issues for Congress

Congress has a continuing interest in understanding the current capacity of the federal
oceanographic fleet and its future capability to support ocean research and related national
security priorities in a timely and cost-effective manner. Previously published federal fleet status
reports have provided some of this information. An oceanographic research vessel can cost
hundreds of millions of dollars.79 These vessels may be considered generational investments for
the federal government as well as the U.S. oceanographic research community.80 Congress may
have questions related to the current and future capacity of the federal oceanographic fleet, such
as the following:
    •  What number of oceanographic research vessels is needed for the fleet to meet
        congressional priorities, and would this require increased, sustained, or reduced
        federal investment?
    •  How can the federal oceanographic fleet advance U.S. science leadership and
        help address potential national security concerns in line with congressional
         priorities?
    •  How might the increased deployment of new marine technology, such as
       uncrewed marine systems (UMSs), affect the number of oceanographic research
        vessels in the federal fleet?

Right-Sizing the Federal Oceanographic Research Fleet

Congress may also be interested in right-sizing the federal oceanographic research fleet, in regard
to costs or national priorities. According to the IWG-FI, right-sizing means identifying the
“proper size of the Federal Fleet to meet the Nation’s mission requirements.”83 In addition to
ensuring the fleet covers the geographic ocean regions and ocean research objectives identified by
Congress and the executive branch, right-sizing might also help to address rising costs associated
with the current fleet or potential national security concerns.

One observer contends that “despite being unarmed, [PRC oceanographic research] vessels and
their crew are capable of carrying out a range of missions that subtract from U.S. power and
influence—from eroding America’s longstanding advantages in undersea warfare to advancing
the PRC’s preferred vision of international maritime order.”100
In the 119th Congress, the Senate Committee on Foreign Relations advanced legislation that
would aim to limit PRC military and oceanographic research vessels from operating in or near
U.S. Arctic waters (S. 4708). The bill cites concerns related to the PRC’s increased “use of dual-
use military-scientific vessels ... while remaining opaque about its intentions.” S. 4708 would
prohibit the Secretary of State from approving an application for a marine research project in U.S.
waters for any foreign vessels owned by or associated with a foreign adversary “that threatens the
security of the United States,” including the PRC.101 According to one observer, S. 4708 recasts
ocean knowledge “as a strategic asset, and its foreign production as a security threat.”102

Congress may consider the appropriate level of funding and support for the federal oceanographic
fleet given the PRC’s current comparative advantage. According to the USCG, “the Coast Guard
is America’s only surface presence in the Arctic—a growing zone of strategic global
competition”;103 some U.S. oceanographic research vessels conduct research expeditions in the
Arctic.104 Executive Order (EO) 14269 of April 9, 2025, “Restoring America’s Maritime
Dominance,” is intended, in part, to help the United States better compete with the PRC in ocean
research. In its FY2027 budget request, NOAA requested an increased funding level to accelerate
the acquisition of a vessel to support EO 14296.105 Some private companies and observers,
however, contend that leasing a vessel can be more cost-effective for the federal government,
compared with purchasing a vessel.106 Increasing the number of vessels in the federal
oceanographic fleet to address national security concerns, such as competing with China in the
ocean-research space, would require Congress to increase appropriations and support for federal
investment in oceanographic vessels.

Alternatives to Research Vessels

In the decade since the 2016 federal fleet status report, certain federal agencies have increasingly
deployed marine technology, including UMSs. For example, NOAA has deployed UMSs for “the
collection and utilization of critical, high accuracy, and time-sensitive data.”107 According to
NOAA, the benefits of UMSs include lower costs to own and operate, and reduced safety risk in
remote and extreme environments.108 Congress also has encouraged NOAA to expand UMS
operations across the agency and to coordinate research and acquisition of UMSs with the Navy
and other federal agencies, industry, and academia.109 UNOLS contends that “despite rapid
advances in satellites, robotics, and autonomous platforms, research vessels remain
indispensable,” given they provide for the direct sampling of the ocean and seafloor; the
deployment, recovery, and servicing of marine technologies (e.g., buoys); and other services.110
To illustrate this, in May 2026, NOAA announced a $21.6 million award for the purchase of
UMSs to be deployed from new NOAA charting and mapping vessels.111 As another example,
that same month, NSF announced a plan to remove a network of ocean observation arrays,
collectively referred to as the Ocean Observatories Initiative, “subject to ship scheduling and
other operational constraints.”112 NSF has since paused its plans to remove these arrays in
response to congressional and other stakeholder concerns.113 Congress may seek to better
understand the relative costs and benefits of advanced marine technologies, including UMSs,
versus research and survey vessels for fulfilling national maritime priorities.

Congress has previously directed NOAA to provide information about the agency’s use of
“chartering agreements, uncrewed assets capable of operating independent of vessels, and
commercial data purchase agreements” when its oceanographic research vessels are out of
commission.114 Congress could consider directing other agencies to do the same. It could also
direct the IWG-FI to broaden such analysis—in a federal fleet status report or elsewhere—to
include information about how certain federal agencies are using UMSs and commercial data
