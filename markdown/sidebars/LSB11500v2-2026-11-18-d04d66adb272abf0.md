Federal District Court Holds That
18 U.S.C. § 611, the Federal Bar on Alien
Voting, Is Unconstitutional


November 18, 2026

In October 2026, the Department of Justice (DOJ) made several announcements that it had charged aliens
in multiple states with election-related federal crimes, including unlawfully casting votes in U.S. elections
in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 611 (Section 611). This statute makes it “unlawful for any alien to vote in any
election held solely or in part for the purpose of electing a candidate” in federal elections. Those indicted
by the government included unlawfully present aliens and aliens admitted for lawful permanent residence.

DOJ also cited Section 611 as the basis for a complaint it filed against the State of California on October
15, 2026, in which it alleged that the state failed to take action to address a list of persons that the federal
government “believes are likely noncitizens who were sent a vote-by-mail ballot from California to
participate in the federal election.” According to the federal government, California’s failure to act
amounted to “knowingly imposing an obstacle on the United States’ ability to enforce 18 U.S.C. § 611 by
facilitating unlawful voting rather than working with the Federal Government to prevent it,” in violation
of the Supremacy Clause of the U.S. Constitution. In denying the United States’ request for a temporary
restraining order requiring California to take action, the district court found, among other things, that the
United States failed to show sufficiently that (1) it had standing to bring suit against the state, (2) Section
611 preempts California election law, and (3) the actions demanded by the United States were consistent
with federal law. A majority of a panel of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit then determined
on appeal that the district court had not abused its discretion and upheld the denial of the temporary
restraining order. The United States applied to the Supreme Court for an injunction against California
pending its appeal of the lower court orders, but that application was denied without noted dissent. DOJ
voluntarily dismissed that case on November 6, 2026.

On October 1, 2026, a district judge in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of Florida issued
an opinion in United States v. Cox concluding, as a matter of nationwide first impression, that Congress
did not have the constitutional authority to enact Section 611. The government has appealed that decision,
and its appeal is pending before the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit. After the district
court’s decision issued, a magistrate judge in the Western District of Wisconsin reached the same
conclusion in dismissing two other criminal cases, using much the same reasoning as the district court.

Those dismissals have been appealed to the relevant district court. Additionally, a district court judge in
the Northern District of Texas summarily rejected the Cox opinion as a basis for a similar challenge made
on the eve of a trial for violation of Section 611.

The decisions concluding that Section 611 is unconstitutional are particularly notable in light of DOJ’s
recent enforcement efforts. The question of the statute’s constitutionality may therefore play a prominent
role in the federal government’s current and future enforcement of the statute and related litigation.
Because of the depth of the district court’s opinion in Cox and the prospect of it yielding the first appellate
court opinion on this question, this Legal Sidebar outlines its reasoning in finding Section 611 to be
outside of the scope of congressional authority.

The Federal Bar on Alien Voting

As the district court in Cox recounted, “since our earliest days as a republic,” states have had different
legal regimes “allowing (or forbidding) non-citizen voting,” but states have “largely prohibited non-
citizen voting since 1926.” Congress enacted Section 611 in 1996; under Section 611(a), it is “unlawful
for any alien to vote in any election held solely or in part for the purpose of electing a candidate for the
office of President, Vice President, Presidential elector, Member of the Senate, Member of the House of
Representatives, Delegate from the District of Columbia, or Resident Commissioner.” This prohibition
does not apply if an election “is held partly for some other purpose” and “aliens are authorized to vote for
such other purpose under a state constitution or statute or a local ordinance.” Some jurisdictions do afford
aliens voting opportunities in local elections. In such circumstances, Section 611(a)(3) mandates that the
method of voting must be such that “an alien . . . [does] not [have] an opportunity to vote for a candidate
for any one or more of” the listed federal offices. Section 611(c) additionally provides that an alien is not
subject to its prohibition if, among other criteria, the alien “reasonably believed at the time of
voting . . . that he or she was a citizen of the United States.”

An alien found to have violated the statute may be fined and imprisoned for up to one year. There are also
potential immigration consequences for violation of the statute.

Constitutional Authorities

Elections for federal office in the United States are governed by various constitutional provisions that
allocate specific authorities between the states and the federal government. As detailed below, these
authorities address the qualifications necessary for voting in federal elections and the way in which
federal elections are carried out. Congress also has independent constitutional authority over immigration
in the United States, and, in the exercise of this authority, it may preempt state laws related to aliens.

Constitutional Authorities of the States Regarding Elections

Three provisions of the Constitution set forth state authorities for establishing the qualifications of voters
in elections for Congress and the President. Article I, Section 2, clause 1, of the U.S. Constitution
provides that, for elections to the House of Representatives, voters “shall have the Qualifications requisite
for Electors of the most numerous Branch of the State Legislature.” Voters in Senate elections, under the
Seventeenth Amendment to the Constitution, “shall have the qualifications requisite for electors of the
most numerous branch of the State legislatures.” With respect to the President, electors are, under Article
II, Section 1, clause 2, appointed by each state “in such Manner as the Legislature thereof may direct.”

Constitutional Authorities of Congress Related to Elections and
Immigration

Congress has two primary constitutional authorities that the United States has urged in litigation pertain to
the prohibition against aliens voting in federal elections. First, the Elections Clause of the Constitution
provides that “[t]he Times, Places and Manner of holding Elections for Senators and Representatives,
shall be prescribed in each State by the Legislature thereof; but the Congress may at any time by Law
make or alter such Regulations, except as to the Places of chusing Senators.” This authority permits states
(or Congress, if it chooses to act) “to enact the numerous requirements as to procedure and safeguards
which experience shows are necessary in order to enforce the fundamental right involved.”

Second, Congress has “plenary power” to regulate immigration, powers that the Supreme Court has
identified as deriving from both certain constitutional provisions and the nature of the sovereignty of the
government.

United States v. Cox (S.D. Fl.)

In Cox, the United States charged, through a one-count information, an alien with “knowingly voting in a
federal election” in 2020 while “fully aware that she was not a United States citizen,” in violation of
Section 611. The defendant moved to dismiss the information on the ground that Section 611 is an
unconstitutional attempt by Congress to pass a voter qualification law for federal elections, a power that
the defendant argued the Constitution instead reserves for states by conferring on them the sole authority
to set qualifications for such elections under the provisions discussed above.

The district court concluded that “the text, structure, and original public meaning of the Constitution show
that Congress does not have the constitutional power to enact” Section 611. In doing so, the court
(1) rejected the government’s contention that Supreme Court precedent required it to uphold the statute,
(2) held that only states are empowered by the Constitution to set voter qualifications, including
qualifications related to citizenship status, and (3) further held that no other provision of the Constitution
empowered Congress to pass Section 611. As part of its analysis, it noted that there was little dispute from
the parties that Section 611 functions as a voter qualification law, insofar as it “dictates who may vote in
federal elections.”

Applicability of Supreme Court Precedent

The government argued in Cox that the Supreme Court’s decision in Oregon v. Mitchell, which upheld a
law setting the minimum voting age to 18 in federal elections, demonstrated that Congress was
empowered to set voter qualifications in federal elections, including on the basis of citizenship.

The district court acknowledged that, to the extent Mitchell was binding, Section 611 would be
constitutional. It did not consider application of Mitchell so straightforward, however. The district court
explained that, in Mitchell, the Justices agreed on a result—Congress could set an age-based voter
qualification in federal elections—but could not agree on what constitutional authority lay behind that
result. The district court pointed out that five Justices “rejected the view that the Elections Clause
empowered Congress to regulate voter qualifications in federal elections.” It also pointed out that five
Justices “also rejected the view that the Fourteenth Amendment empowered Congress to lower the voting
age.” Faced with this set of divergent rationales, the district court concluded that it was bound by
Supreme Court and circuit precedent to view Mitchell as establishing only that “Congress can regulate
voting-age qualifications in federal elections,” and therefore could not control questions concerning other
types of voting qualifications. It further found that subsequent precedent “further diminish[ed] Mitchell’s
force.” In particular, the district court recited the Supreme Court’s statement in Arizona v. Inter Tribal

Council of Arizona (ITCA), that “the Elections Clause empowers Congress to regulate how federal
elections are held, but not who may vote in them.” The district court conceded that this statement was
dicta, but “the kind of dicta that merits careful attention from the lower courts,” and, when considered
alongside other precedent, further reason to believe that Mitchell did not control.

Scope of State Authority over Voter Qualifications

Having identified no binding Supreme Court precedent resolving the case, the district court in Cox then
held that “[t]he Constitution commits to the States, and the States alone, the general power to set voter
qualifications in federal elections” as a matter of both its text and history. The court examined the clauses
of the Constitution addressing election of Representatives, Senators, and the President, and concluded that
they all reflected the same principle: “The States have sole authority to set voter qualifications.”
According to the court, the constitutional provisions addressing congressional elections establish that
“voters’ qualifications are a function of each State’s rules” and the provision addressing presidential
elections provides that “the States have the prerogative to establish whatever rules for selecting electors
they see fit.” This reading reflected a “foundational principle of our constitutional order,” namely, a
federal government of limited powers.

The district court identified further support for this reading of the text of the Constitution in the history
surrounding its adoption and the adoption of subsequent amendments. As to the former, the court
recounted that the framers of the Constitution considered and rejected “a proposal . . . that would have
assigned the federal government the power to set voter qualifications for federal elections.” As to the
latter, the court recounted the rejection of a similar proposal during consideration of the Reconstruction
Amendments and noted further that subsequent amendments addressing voting “[did] not alter the
Framers’ original design.”

Scope of Congressional Authority over Voter Qualifications

The district court in Cox then rejected both congressional plenary power over immigration and
congressional authority under the Elections Clause as adequate bases for Section 611.

The government characterized Section 611 as a means to address foreign interference in elections, placing
it well within congressional authority over immigration and foreign affairs. The court in Cox agreed that
congressional authority in this respect is “broad indeed,” but was not convinced that Section 611 was a
means of “legislat[ing] against foreign interference in federal elections.” Textually, the district court
focused on the statute’s exception for state and local elections, finding that “[i]f Congress really intended
the statute as an exercise of its plenary authority over aliens, there’s no reason it would have cleaved off
non-federal elections.” It also found the generalized nature of congressional power over immigration and
foreign affairs to cut against the ability of Congress to override the specific authorities concerning voting
qualifications in federal elections that the Constitution textually commits to the states. It explained that
these latter specific clauses reflected “a foundational limit on federal authority,” and such “[f]oundational
constitutional limits do not give way, even to capacious general provisions of enumerated congressional
power.”

The government also characterized Section 611 as a means to ensure the integrity of federal elections,
thereby making it a legitimate exercise of congressional authority under the Elections Clause. Relying
largely on ITCA, the district court held that Section 611 fell outside of that authority because the Elections
Clause reflects “[t]he power to regulate the ‘mechanics’ of federal elections” and not “the power to decide
who may vote” in federal elections. The court further determined that the government had offered no
evidence that Section 611 was “designed to further” the purpose of preventing voter fraud.

Enforcement Consequences

In light of the exclusive authorities to set voter qualifications given by the Constitution to the states, and
the concomitant lack of congressional authority to independently set voter qualifications, the district court
in Cox held that Section 611 was unconstitutional as applied to the defendant. “To hold otherwise,” it
reasoned, “would allow Congress to establish and modify the parameters of its own special electorate,
entirely divorced from what . . . any . . . State may decide,” a “result [that] is precisely what our federal
constitutional republic does not allow.”

The district noted three further points related to its holding. First, it explained that the state in which the
defendant voted “can and does” punish the same conduct. Second, it made clear that its opinion did not
“address the Government’s power to remove a non-U.S. citizen who engages in the same conduct.” Third,
while disclaiming any definitive conclusion, it suggested that a federal statute criminalizing voting in
federal elections in violation of state law would present “very different” legal considerations.

Considerations for Congress

Because the district court in Cox considered only an as-applied challenge to Section 611 in the posture of
a motion to dismiss, the effect of its constitutional holding is limited to the defendant. If, in the appeal to
the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit, that court rejects the government’s position and
upholds the district court’s decision, similarly situated aliens in that circuit may be able to rely on this
precedent to claim that the statute is also unconstitutional as applied to them. Should that court or other
appellate courts reach broadly applicable determinations that the statute is unconstitutional, several paths
are available to Congress, including waiting for a definitive Supreme Court decision; repealing the statute
in favor of state enforcement of state voter qualification laws; or amending the statute, as contemplated by
the district court, to criminalize voting in federal elections in violation of state voter qualification laws.
