Introduction

The Unemployment Insurance (UI) system is constructed as a joint federal-state partnership of
which the Unemployment Compensation (UC) program is the foundation. Created under the
Social Security Act of 1935 (SSA; P.L. 74-271), the UC program provides income support
through weekly benefit payments to involuntarily unemployed workers. Although there are broad
requirements under federal law regarding UC benefits and financing, the specific parameters are
set out under each state’s laws. States administer UC benefits with U.S. Department of Labor
(DOL) oversight, resulting in 53 different UC programs operated in the states, the District of
Columbia, Puerto Rico, and the U.S. Virgin Islands.1
The Federal-State Extended Unemployment Compensation Act of 1970 (P.L. 91-373)2 established
the Extended Benefits (EB) program to provide additional weeks of unemployment benefits if
high unemployment exists within a state. After UC benefits are exhausted, the EB program, also
administered by states, may provide up to an additional 13 or 20 weeks of benefits, depending on
worker eligibility, state law, and economic conditions in the state. Under permanent law, the EB
program is funded 50% by the federal government and 50% by the states.3

Designated as a trust fund for federal accounting purposes, the Unemployment Trust Fund (UTF)
in the U.S. Treasury accounts for UC and EB financial transactions. The UTF’s structure, which
involves 59 separate accounts, and complexity, which includes four types of statutory transfers
between key UTF accounts, present challenges in tracking the annual flow of UI income and
outlays. Congress may be interested in understanding how federal unemployment tax revenue
moves through UTF accounts. In particular, there may be interest in which UTF flows are based
on statute and statutory thresholds and which flows require annual appropriations. Additionally,
Congress has expressed interest in reconsidering the method through which funding for state UI
administration is collected and distributed via the UTF.4

This report illustrates the operations of the UTF using FY2026 projections from DOL on UI
income, outlays, and end-of-year balances as well as relevant FY2026 annual appropriations data,
which are enacted amounts under the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026 (P.L. 119-75).
Included is a description of sources of revenue, UTF accounts, statutory transfers, federal loans to
states, UC appropriations out of the relevant UTF account, and outlays on UI benefits. Relevant
statutory authorities are also provided throughout. The Appendix provides a glossary of
acronyms used in this report.

Sources of Unemployment Trust Fund Revenue8

Sources of UTF revenue are depicted in Figure 1 as gray ovals.

The federal-state UI program is financed by federal payroll taxes under FUTA and by state
payroll taxes under SUTA.9

FUTA

FUTA is set out under Title 26, Sections 3301-3311, of the U.S. Code and establishes a federal
payroll tax that employers pay to fund both federal and state administrative costs as well as the
federal share of the EB program (50%), loans to insolvent state UC accounts, and state
employment services. If a state UC program complies with all federal requirements, the net FUTA
tax rate for employers is 0.6% on the first $7,000 of each worker’s annual earnings. Federal law
defines which jobs a state UC program must cover for the state’s employers to avoid paying the
maximum FUTA tax rate (6.0%) on the first $7,000 of each employee’s annual pay.10

FUTA revenues are deposited in the U.S. Treasury and credited to ESAA within the UTF (see
“ESAA” section).
In FY2026, income from FUTA was projected to be $7.30 billion.11

For additional information on FUTA, see CRS Report R44527, Unemployment Compensation:
The Fundamentals of the Federal Unemployment Tax (FUTA).

EUCA is authorized under SSA, Section 905.20
If a state has an active EB program,21 EUCA distributions are made for the federal portion (50%)

Reed Act Distributions

As explained above, at the end of the fiscal year, there is a limitation on the balance in ESAA—
the account balance cannot exceed 40% of the prior fiscal year’s congressional appropriation
(SSA, §901(f)).37 If the balance in ESAA exceeds this limitation, the excess is distributed to
EUCA. After the distribution, if the balance in EUCA exceeds the applicable EUCA limitation,38
the excess is distributed to FUA. If after the distribution from EUCA, the FUA balance exceeds
the limitation,39 the excess is distributed as a Reed Act distribution to the states under SSA,
Section 903.40

In FY2026, there were no projected Reed Act distributions, as the account balances for EUCA (-
$4.88 billion) and FUA ($13.94 billion) were both under the FY2026 ceilings of $48.84 billion.

Federal Loans to States

Federal loans to states are depicted in Figure 1 as a solid line with an arrow between FUA and
the state unemployment accounts.
If states do not have enough funding in their state accounts, Title XII of SSA41 allows the states to
borrow funds from FUA within the UTF. States may borrow from other sources, although some
states are prohibited from doing so under state laws.42 The issuing of a loan to the state would
require that FUA redeem securities. This redemption would decrease the federal debt. If FUA is
insolvent and the other federal accounts within the UTF do not have sufficient balances to lend
the funds that states need (as occurred in FY2010-FY2015 and began again in May 2020), Title
XII of SSA allows FUA to borrow funds from the U.S. Treasury. If Treasury were to issue new
securities in order to lend funds to FUA, this would increase the federal debt. When a state pays
back the loan from FUA, FUA then uses those funds to repay its debt to Treasury, and the federal
debt decreases.

In FY2026, there was a projected $5.38 billion in income to FUA, which included interest
earnings ($0.17 billion), state repayments and reduced credits ($2.69 billion and $1.84 billion,
respectively), and state interest payments ($0.67 billion).43 States were projected to have a
cumulative $19.77 billion in outstanding loans at the end of FY2026.

State UI Administration Grants
SSA, Section 901(c)(1)(A)(I),47 authorizes administrative grants to the states (subject to
appropriations limits) from ESAA. The discretionary annual appropriations to DOL for
administrative expenses are not based on the size of FUTA collections. Instead, each year, DOL
assesses state budgetary requirements,48 which are provided within budget justification
documents for considerations by appropriators. The subsequent appropriations customarily
include a base level of funding as well as an additional contingent appropriation. The
appropriations language also customarily provides a baseline estimate of national unemployment
as measured by the volume of UC claims expected to be filed per week—the average weekly
insured unemployment (AWIU). Additionally, the contingent funding includes a trigger based on
the average volume of weekly UC claims exceeding the AWIU baseline. For example, under P.L.
119-75, for every 100,000 increase in the total AWIU above the 3,075,000 baseline, an additional
$28.6 million in state administrative funding would have been available.

Once the AWIU is above the contingent appropriation threshold, DOL notifies the Office of
Management and Budget, which then releases funds from the contingent appropriation. If the
need for contingency funds exceeds the base appropriation, but the national AWIU is less than the
contingent appropriation threshold, then either Congress must enact a supplemental appropriation
or states must reduce administrative expenses or provide additional state funding from different
revenue streams or state general funds (as SUTA funds may pay only for UC and EB).49

As shown in Figure 1, in FY2026, P.L. 119-75 provided $3.23 billion to be appropriated for state
administrative grants.

For additional information on funding state UI administration, see CRS In Focus IF10838,
Funding the State Administration of Unemployment Compensation (UC) Benefits.

UC, UCFE, UCX, and EB Benefits

Outlays for UC, UCFE, UCX, and EB benefits are depicted in Figure 1 with a dark blue box.

Titles III, IX, and XII of SSA authorize the joint federal-state UC program to provide
unemployment benefits. Most states provide up to a maximum of 26 weeks of UC benefits.
Former federal workers may be eligible for unemployment benefits through the UCFE program.
Former U.S. military servicemembers may be eligible for unemployment benefits through the
UCX program. The Emergency Unemployment Compensation Act of 1991 (P.L. 102-164)
provides that ex-servicemembers be treated the same as other unemployed workers with respect
to benefit levels, the waiting period for benefits, and benefit duration.

The EB program was established by the Federal-State Extended Unemployment Compensation
Act of 1970 (P.L. 91-373).63 P.L. 91-373 extends the duration (i.e., number of weeks) of
unemployment benefits (extended benefits) at the state level if certain economic conditions exist
within the state.

In FY2026, outlays on UC, UCFE, and UCX benefits were projected to be $41.21 billion. No EB
was projected to be payable in FY2026.

For more on UC, UCFE, UCX, and EB benefits, see CRS Report R46687, Unemployment
