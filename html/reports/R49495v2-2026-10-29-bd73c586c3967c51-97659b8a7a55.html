Summary

Decommissioning offshore oil and gas platforms and related infrastructure generally means removing these facilities from the seafloor, permanently plugging all wells, plugging or removing pipelines, clearing the seafloor of obstructions, and taking related actions associated with the end of oil and gas production from an offshore lease. Pursuant to the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA; 43 U.S.C. §§1331-1356c), the Department of the Interior (DOI) has issued decommissioning regulations for oil and gas operators on the U.S. outer continental shelf (OCS). Regulating OCS decommissioning has posed challenges for DOI's ocean energy management agencies—the Bureau of Ocean Energy Management (BOEM) and the Bureau of Safety and Environmental Enforcement (BSEE), which are being merged into a new DOI agency called the Marine Minerals Administration.

Although DOI's regulations and lease provisions make offshore oil and gas lessees responsible for infrastructure decommissioning, lessees have not always fulfilled their decommissioning responsibilities. In 2024, the Government Accountability Office (GAO) reported that, of roughly 1,600 platforms and 8,000 wells existing in the Gulf of Mexico (now Gulf of America) at that time, more than 500 platforms and 2,700 wells were overdue for decommissioning under DOI regulations. In some cases, offshore operators have been unable to meet their decommissioning responsibilities—for instance, due to bankruptcy—leaving the federal government responsible for decommissioning such so-called orphaned infrastructure. Financial bonds required by DOI from offshore operators to cover decommissioning costs have not been sufficient to cover all liability in such cases.

Congress and successive presidential Administrations have considered how to address offshore decommissioning needs and how to reduce federal financial liability for decommissioning. Some approaches have involved changes to DOI's financial assurance and supplemental bonding regulations. Other approaches have considered ways to reduce lessees' decommissioning costs, especially through options for decommissioning in place. Under these approaches, lessees do not need to fully remove all infrastructure, but can leave some structures on the seafloor to serve various purposes. A prominent existing framework for decommissioning in place is the Rigs-to-Reefs program, under which some structures may remain in the ocean to serve as artificial reefs for marine life. Some Members of Congress and other stakeholders seek to grow this program, citing potential enhancements to fisheries and some marine industries, while others question whether the structures could pose environmental or navigational hazards. Other potential uses for legacy offshore infrastructure, such as repurposing for other offshore energy development, offshore aquaculture, or sea-based space launches, also have been proposed. In a regulatory agenda published in August 2026, DOI announced a proposal to make toppling in place (one method of decommissioning in place) the default standard for OCS infrastructure, under specified circumstances. Also, in September 2026, President Trump issued an executive order, "Restoring American Saltwater Angling and Recreation," which among other provisions directed DOI to establish a program to facilitate conversion of decommissioned offshore oil and gas and other-use structures into permanent artificial reefs through a variety of reefing methods.

Congress may continue to consider how to address economic and environmental issues related to offshore decommissioning and whether to promote alternatives to full infrastructure removal. Bills under consideration in the 119th Congress would address decommissioning enforcement (H.R. 8099, S. 4239), offshore lessees' financial strength and bonding requirements (H.R. 9034, H.R. 9035, S. 4715), federal appropriations for decommissioning activities (H.R. 9171), and artificial reefing and the Rigs-to-Reefs program (H.R. 5745).

Introduction

Hundreds of offshore oil and natural gas platforms and associated facilities lie on the U.S. outer continental shelf (OCS), primarily in the Gulf of America.1 These include platforms and other facilities that are actively used for oil and gas exploration and production, as well as some that are no longer in active use. Oil and gas developers have installed such infrastructure under leases issued by the Department of the Interior (DOI) for offshore oil and gas exploration and development, pursuant to the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA; 43 U.S.C. §§1331-1356c).2 Under DOI's regulations, when platforms, wells, and related facilities in offshore lease areas are "no longer useful for operations," lessees must remove the platforms from the OCS, permanently plug all wells, clear the seafloor of obstructions, and take related actions that are collectively known as decommissioning.3

Although the regulations make offshore oil and gas lessees responsible for infrastructure decommissioning, lessees have not always fulfilled their decommissioning responsibilities.4 In 2024, the Government Accountability Office (GAO) reported that, of roughly 1,600 platforms and 8,000 wells existing in the Gulf of Mexico (now Gulf of America) at that time, more than 500 platforms and 2,700 wells were overdue for decommissioning under DOI regulations.5 Offshore oil and gas infrastructure that is no longer used for operations but has not been decommissioned is sometimes referred to as idle infrastructure or idle iron. In some cases, offshore lessees are unable to meet their decommissioning responsibilities—for instance, due to bankruptcy—which could leave the federal government responsible for decommissioning such so-called orphaned infrastructure. As part of its leasing terms, DOI requires lessees to provide bonds that may be used to cover decommissioning costs if an operator fails to perform its decommissioning responsibilities.6 However, the bonds have sometimes been insufficient to account for the full costs of decommissioning. GAO estimated that, collectively, the bonds held by DOI for all oil and gas structures on the OCS are billions of dollars short of the total estimated decommissioning cost of all such structures.7 In recent years, Congress has provided some discretionary appropriations to DOI's Bureau of Safety and Environmental Enforcement (BSEE) for decommissioning existing orphaned infrastructure on the OCS.8

Congress and multiple presidential Administrations have considered how to facilitate lessees' compliance with offshore decommissioning requirements and how to reduce potential federal financial liability for decommissioning. Some approaches have involved changes to DOI's financial assurance and supplemental bonding regulations. Other approaches have considered ways to reduce lessees' decommissioning costs, especially through options for decommissioning in place.9 Under these approaches, lessees do not need to fully remove all infrastructure, but can leave some structures on the seafloor—for instance, as part of the Rigs-to-Reefs program (under which some structures may remain in the ocean to serve as artificial reefs for marine life) or by repurposing for different types of offshore energy development, sea-based space launches, or other purposes.10 Congress may continue to consider how to address policy concerns related to offshore decommissioning and whether to promote alternatives to full infrastructure removal.

What Is Offshore Decommissioning?

Decommissioning generally occurs when an offshore lease ceases to produce oil and gas in paying quantities, thus terminating the lease (unless a lease suspension has been approved or other specified conditions are met).11 DOI's regulations state that within one year after a lease terminates, all wells must be permanently plugged and all infrastructure and equipment used in the exploration and production of oil and gas (Figure 1) must be removed from the marine environment.12

Decommissioning an offshore platform generally entails multiple steps:

  • permanently plugging all wells supported by the platform (including severing the well casings 15 feet below the mudline)13 within one year after the lease terminates;
  • removing all platforms and other facilities to at least 15 feet below the mudline within one year after the lease terminates, unless the platforms and other facilities are to be used for other approved activities;
  • decommissioning all pipelines and removing any pipelines the BSEE regional supervisor determines are an obstruction or pose adverse environmental effects;14 and
  • clearing the seafloor of all obstructions associated with oil and gas operations within 60 days after well plugging and platform removal.15

The removal of the platform and its associated equipment also includes its transportation to an area onshore for recycling or disposal.16 According to BSEE, "over the past decade, the offshore energy industry has averaged 127 platform removals per year."17 In 2023, a study estimated the average costs to decommission a shallow-water or a deep-water well located on the OCS (i.e., federal waters) in the Gulf of America to be $660,000 and $24 million, respectively.18 The estimated cost does not include the cost for decommissioning the platform or associated infrastructure. In its 2024 report, GAO stated that decommissioning all infrastructure on a lease could cost tens of millions of dollars per lease in shallow water and hundreds of millions per lease in deep water.19

Although DOI's regulations generally require lessees to "remove all platforms and other facilities" and to "clear the seafloor of all obstructions" created by the facilities,20 the regulations provide for certain alternatives to full removal of the platform from the marine environment. One option is to allow all or part of a platform to remain on the OCS as part of a Rigs-to-Reefs program (i.e., as an artificial reef site).21 This generally involves one of three decommissioning methods that allow the structure to serve as an artificial reef for marine life:22

  • partial removal—severing the platform at a permitted depth (typically at least 85 feet below the surface of the water) that allows a vessel to navigate in the area unobstructed, and placing the severed portion on the seafloor beside the base of the structure;
  • toppling-in-place—severing the entire structure from the seafloor and toppling it onto its side; or
  • tow-and-place—severing the structure from the seafloor and towing it to a selected reef.

Another option is to reuse or repurpose the offshore oil and gas facility for uses other than an artificial reef. For example, an offshore operator may apply to reuse a facility for activities that "support OCS oil and gas production and transportation" or for other energy-related and marine-related uses (e.g., offshore wind; subseafloor storage of natural gas or other substances).23 An operator would need DOI approval to reuse or relocate an offshore oil and gas facility. Options for repurposing offshore oil and gas structures are discussed further below (section on "Decommissioning in Place").

Decommissioning Needs by Region

DOI divides the OCS into four regions: the Gulf of America, Pacific, Atlantic, and Alaska regions.24 Almost all the OCS oil and gas infrastructure is located in the Gulf of America region, which accounts for 99% of U.S. offshore oil and gas production.25 DOI data as of October 2026 show 1,306 oil and gas platforms in the Gulf of America region.26 Many of these platforms are currently producing oil and gas, or are not due for decommissioning for other reasons.27 However, some platforms are on leases that have terminated, meaning that decommissioning would typically be required. According to BSEE data updated in 2026, of 1,260 Gulf platform structures of selected types, 492 were located on leases that had terminated.28 Of these, BSEE had received a decommissioning application for 240 structures.29

The Pacific OCS region hosts 23 oil and gas platforms, all lying off the coast of Southern California. These platforms were installed in the 1960s to 1980s, and some have ceased to produce oil and gas in paying quantities. As of January 2026, BSEE listed 8 of the 23 platforms as being "in various stages of decommissioning."30

Infrastructure decommissioning issues are less immediately relevant in the Atlantic and Alaska OCS regions than in the Gulf of America and Pacific regions. There are no extant oil and gas platforms in the Atlantic OCS region. The Beaufort Sea planning area of the Alaska region has one project, a joint unit in federal and state waters, that continues to produce oil and gas.31 The other Alaska planning areas where federal leasing has taken place—Cook Inlet and Chukchi Sea—have no existing infrastructure in federal waters. (Alaska's Cook Inlet has oil and gas platforms in state waters.)

For facilities in the Gulf and Pacific regions, some observers have identified risks associated with delays in decommissioning idle infrastructure. In its 2024 report, GAO identified "potentially serious" safety and environmental risks (in addition to financial risks to the federal government from decommissioning liability).32 GAO stated that safety concerns on idled platforms could stem from platform deterioration, such as degraded walkways that could endanger personnel accessing platforms. Environmental risks could include increased risk of oil spills due to a loss of tank or pipeline integrity. Some recent offshore incidents, such as an April 2025 oil spill in state waters off Plaquemines Parish in Louisiana, have been associated with aging idle infrastructure.33

Financial Responsibility for Decommissioning

DOI regulations, as well as individual oil and gas leases, generally assign financial and operational responsibility for decommissioning to oil and gas lessees (subject to agency approval of decommissioning applications and plans).34 Under the regulations, some of these responsibilities extend not only to the entity that holds the lease at the time it is terminated, but also to predecessor lessees that previously held the lease.35

Financial Assurance

To help ensure that lessees fulfill their decommissioning responsibilities, DOI requires lessees to provide financial assurance, typically in the form of bonds, as part of their lease obligations. DOI regulations require lessees to provide financial assurance in specified amounts at several stages of the oil and gas leasing and development process, including before receiving a lease, before commencing lease exploration, and before engaging in development and production activities.36 Beyond the base amounts required at each stage, DOI officials may determine that supplemental financial assurance is necessary to ensure compliance with lease obligations, including decommissioning obligations.37 (If the lessee satisfactorily performs the lease obligations, including decommissioning, then DOI cancels the bond.)38

The base and supplemental financial assurance that DOI requires from lessees has sometimes proven insufficient to cover all decommissioning costs when leases terminate. This has left the federal government with responsibility for decommissioning orphaned infrastructure when operators have suffered bankruptcies and predecessor lessees are not available to decommission. As of October 2026, DOI reported that the government was carrying $129.9 million in uncovered liability for existing orphaned facilities, after applying the base and supplemental financial assurance provided by lessees for these facilities.39 In recent years, BSEE has received about $3.0 million in annual appropriations for an offshore decommissioning fund to cover some of its costs for decommissioning such orphaned infrastructure (see the section on "Federal Appropriations for Decommissioning" for further discussion). In terms of potential future exposure, the 2024 GAO report found that, as of June 2023, DOI held about $3.5 billion in total supplemental bonds to cover $40 billion-$70 billion in total estimated decommissioning costs for all wells and platforms on the OCS, meaning that the government would face at least $36.5 billion in total uncovered liabilities if all operators failed to meet their decommissioning responsibilities.40 (Not all of this potential future exposure would be realized, as the liability would only fall to the federal government if the current and predecessor lessees did not perform their decommissioning responsibilities.)

Changes to Financial Assurance Regulations

Partly in response to shortfalls between collected financial assurance and actual decommissioning liability, DOI has evolved its regulatory framework for financial assurance over the years. Most recently, both the Biden Administration and the second Trump Administration have taken regulatory actions related to financial assurance.41

In 2024, under the Biden Administration, DOI's Bureau of Ocean Energy Management (BOEM) revised multiple aspects of its financial assurance regulations.42 The revised regulations aimed to "protect taxpayers from covering costs that should be borne by the oil and gas industry when offshore platforms require decommissioning."43 The revision altered BOEM's criteria for determining whether supplemental financial assurance is needed from a lessee and, if so, how much is needed. The revised rule established that BOEM would determine a lessee's supplemental financial assurance obligations based on the lessee's credit rating (using an investment-grade credit rating threshold or a proxy credit rating equivalent) and the lease's reserve value (the ratio of the value of proved oil and gas reserves on the lease to the decommissioning liability associated with those reserves).44 DOI stated that the latter factor was important because if a lease still had significant reserves available, a new operator would be likely to acquire the lease in the event of a lessee's bankruptcy.45 The rule also finalized the use of new methods to estimate decommissioning costs when setting supplemental financial assurance amounts. BOEM projected that a total of $6.9 billion in new supplemental financial assurance (2023 dollars) would be required from existing lessees and grant holders on the basis of the changes in the 2024 regulations.46

The 2024 regulatory changes drew mixed reactions from stakeholders. Some oil and gas lessees, particularly smaller companies and those whose business models involve acquiring offshore leases near the end of their productivity, expressed concern that the changes would be prohibitively costly. For example, in comments on BOEM's proposed rule, one industry group stated that the rule would constitute "a de facto moratorium on continued development in the Gulf of Mexico by all but the major oil and gas companies," given that "non-integrated developers, or smaller companies, who collectively contribute more than one-third of GOM [Gulf of Mexico] oil and gas production, would bear the majority of this increased burden."47 In contrast, some other industry groups and some major oil and gas companies largely supported the regulations, noting among other things that stronger bonding requirements could reduce the likelihood that predecessor lessees would be called on to decommission if current lessees failed to perform their responsibilities.48

In 2026, under the Trump Administration, BOEM proposed further revisions to its supplemental financial assurance regulations, including actions to reverse some changes made in 2024. In a proposed rule published on March 9, 2026, BOEM stated that "new market conditions" warranted changes to the 2024 regulations.49 Among other changes, BOEM proposed to revise its credit rating threshold for requiring supplemental assurance (allowing some lessees with lower credit ratings than the 2024 rule permits to be exempted from supplemental assurance obligations), to revise the decommissioning cost estimates it uses to determine supplemental financial assurance amounts, and to consider the financial strength of predecessor lessees when assessing supplemental financial assurance requirements for current lessees.50 BOEM estimated that its proposed changes would reduce the financial obligations to existing lessees under the 2024 rule by about $6.2 billion (2025 dollars), thus "increas[ing] the amount of capital available for oil and gas exploration and production on the OCS."51 In comments on the proposed rule, some stakeholders supported the changes, stating, for instance, that taking into account the financial strength of predecessor lessees more appropriately measures actual risk to taxpayers from operator bankruptcies, since predecessor lessees—often major oil and gas companies—would be called on to perform decommissioning before a facility would be considered orphaned infrastructure.52 Other oil and gas industry groups, as well as some stakeholders in the surety and fidelity bonding sector, opposed the changes, expressing particular concern about BOEM's proposed reliance on the financial strength of predecessor lessees when assessing bonding requirements.53 As of this report's publication date, DOI had not issued a final rule.

Other Potential Decommissioning Enforcement Mechanisms

DOI's regulations include other steps, beyond using lessees' financial assurance, that DOI could take to enforce decommissioning obligations against lessees and their predecessors. For instance, BSEE officials could issue a notice of incident of noncompliance to offshore lessees and/or predecessor lessees who fail to decommission, could apply civil penalties to lessees and/or their predecessors, or could disqualify them as OCS operators.54 In its 2024 report, GAO found that such enforcement tools "have not effectively compelled operator compliance."55 GAO stated that DOI's process for issuing citations can extend for years and that the citations and notices were "unlikely to motivate operators."56 GAO also found that DOI rarely takes further punitive action, such as assessing penalties or disqualifying operators.57 Additionally, BSEE's data systems do not "readily track" information about predecessor lessees, which can complicate the agency's ability to enforce obligations against these parties.58

Decommissioning in Place

Separately from issues related to financial assurance, Congress and DOI have considered the type and extent of decommissioning that lessees must perform. In particular, some DOI authorities allow lessees to leave certain end-of-life oil and gas infrastructure on the seafloor to be used for other purposes, sometimes known as decommissioning in place. Under its regulations, DOI may approve partial platform removal or toppling in place so that infrastructure can be used in a state artificial reefing program, known as a Rigs-to-Reefs program.59 Offshore oil and gas infrastructure also could potentially be reused in other ways, such as for offshore wind energy development, offshore aquaculture, or military or space launches, among other uses.60 Such alternative uses may depend on the location, water depth, condition of the structure, local environmental (including meteorological) conditions, and economic considerations. DOI generally would administer such alternative uses under an Alternate Use right-of-use and easement (RUE).61 Under DOI regulations, the holder of an Alternate Use RUE is responsible for decommissioning obligations related to the RUE, once that use terminates.62

Repurposing an offshore oil and gas platform could reduce decommissioning costs, and could potentially reduce the impacts of a platform's complete removal and the installation of new structures for other uses.63 According to a 2018 article specific to the decommissioning of offshore oil and gas platforms in the North Sea, nearly 95% of stakeholders (36 out of 38) spanning academia, government, and industry supported a "case-by-case approach to decommission."64 The coauthors of the study, including a BOEM marine ecologist,65 recommended allowing for partial removal of platforms based on environmental considerations and broadening those considerations to include the ecosystem services provided by the offshore structures, among other recommendations.66 For example, partial removal of a platform could protect the seafloor from bottom trawling,67 an industrial fishing practice that physically disturbs seafloor habitat.68 Some observers have asserted that "if an alternative use were not pursued, the complete ecosystem associated with the structure would be destroyed if the structure were taken onshore and dismantled."69 Others have observed mixed effects on natural ecosystems following the partial removal of a decommissioned oil and gas facility, including postremoval differences in fish community composition, some of which entailed removals of exotic species.70 Some other studies identify risks of pollution, habitat damage, food web alterations, and invasive species proliferations, among others, associated with leaving certain oil and gas infrastructure in place.71

Currently, DOI's regulations require lessees to fully remove offshore infrastructure, unless DOI officials approve decommissioning in place under specified conditions.72 In a regulatory agenda published in August 2026, DOI discussed a potential change to this framework.73 DOI announced it would explore revising the regulations to "set 'topple in place' as the default decommissioning standard" provided lessees comply with U.S. Coast Guard navigational requirements.74 DOI anticipates issuing a notice of proposed rulemaking regarding this change in July 2027.75

Rigs-to-Reefs Program

A common approach for repurposing decommissioned offshore oil and gas facilities has been their conversion to artificial reefs.76 Given their size and composition, submerged oil and gas facilities (e.g., platforms) can attract both sessile (attached in place) and mobile organisms to components of these facilities at varying depths.77 Some stakeholders note that since the first offshore leases for oil and gas infrastructure were issued in the late 1940s, petroleum platforms have essentially functioned as artificial reefs, with those in place presently serving as hard substrate for a variety of marine organisms.78 Some scientists also identify platforms and associated infrastructure as a source of additional habitat for these species, potentially providing a substitute for or enhancing degraded or limited natural habitats.79 The development of these biological communities and ecological succession of a variety of fish and invertebrate species also has led to use of these reefs by commercial and recreational fishers, among other interested parties (e.g., SCUBA divers).80 In light of these and other factors, Congress passed the National Fishing Enhancement Act of 1984 (P.L. 98-623, Title II; 33 U.S.C. §§2101-2106), which included provisions governing the use of artificial reefs composed of various materials, including decommissioned oil and gas infrastructure, primarily for commercial and recreational fishing interests in U.S. waters. Consequently, associated Rigs-to-Reefs programs have been developed in addition to other artificial-reef-related programs in U.S. waters.81 Collectively, these programs have built on earlier efforts by multiple coastal states focused on the development and use of artificial reefs.82

In accordance with the act, the Secretary of Commerce, in consultation with other parties, developed the National Artificial Reef Plan in 1985. The plan guides artificial reef managers and policymakers regarding artificial reef development and use.83 This plan was most recently amended by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration in 2007.84 Since publication of the National Artificial Reef Plan, several coastal states, particularly those in the Gulf of America region, have joined with partners to implement the plan's recommendations, including in administering Rigs-to-Reefs programs.85 Among its contents, the plan implements congressional directives regarding artificial reefs, which stipulate that their siting, construction, monitoring, and management are to be based on the "best scientific information available"; enhance fishery resources to the "maximum extent practicable"; facilitate their access and use by recreational and commercial fishers; minimize conflicts with competing uses as well as environmental and health risks; and avoid obstruction of navigation pathways, among other requirements.86 The act also authorizes the Secretary of the Army to issue permits for the construction and management of artificial reefs and requires the Secretary to ensure permittees' compliance with environmental, human safety, and property-related protections.87 It further stipulates that permittees and their insurers are liable for certain associated damages (i.e., those not in compliance with the terms and conditions of a given permit) as determined under applicable law.88

Federal decommissioning and reefing efforts are administered by DOI and the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers in accordance with implementing regulations.89 The decommissioning process for Rigs-to-Reefs involves both federal and state authorities, with the federal government responsible for approving a structure's use in a state reefing program as a method of decommissioning, and the state responsible for accepting the structure into its reefing program and managing it thereafter. On September 17, 2026, President Trump issued an executive order (EO), "Restoring American Saltwater Angling and Recreation."90 Section 6 of the EO directs the Secretary of the Interior, acting through the Marine Minerals Administration,91 to establish a federal OCS reef-in-place program to facilitate the conversion of decommissioned offshore oil and gas and other-use structures into permanent artificial reefs through a variety of reefing methods. The EO requires this program to be established within 60 days after the date of the order and requires interagency coordination to "streamline" review and permitting of qualifying reef projects.92 Potential actions to implement the EO were unclear as of the publication of this CRS report.

In the Gulf of America, the majority of decommissioned infrastructure converted to artificial reefs is off Louisiana and Texas (Figure 2), which each supported reef conversion decommissioning programs prior to the passage of the National Fishing Enhancement Act and initial publication of the National Artificial Reef Plan.93 In the time since, all five Gulf coast states have approved artificial reef plans, accepted donations of decommissioned oil and gas rig components, and incorporated decommissioned platforms into their artificial reef programs.94 California, while having authorized the conversion of oil and gas structures to artificial reefs under certain requirements (e.g., agreement by the California Department of Fish and Wildlife [CDFW] to manage the reef), had not decommissioned any platforms for these purposes as of October 2026.95 According to CDFW, oil and gas infrastructure is not included in the definition of artificial reefs in the California Fish and Game Code and will not be included in the California Artificial Reef Plan.96 As of October 2026, BSEE did not identify additional Rigs-to-Reefs programs or reef conversation programs for oil and gas infrastructure in other U.S. states.97

Researchers have carried out investigations regarding the effectiveness of Rigs-to-Reefs programs and how decommissioned oil and gas facilities may function as artificial reefs. Studies find that Rigs-to-Reefs programs can provide additional fishing opportunities for anglers, including a broad diversity of species to target in certain cases.98 Researchers also document increased overall species diversity, differing reef fish community composition, and larger reef fishes on certain oil and gas platforms with increasing depth, suggesting association of a variety of species with these structures.99 Some have debated whether artificial reefs, such as decommissioned oil and gas platforms, can enhance fish populations or if they may only attract individuals from these populations (i.e., attraction vs. production debate).100 Some studies suggest that decommissioned platforms may serve as productive fish habitats, given the large sizes of these structures, with potential enhancements to fish populations.101 Other studies find no significant changes in federally managed fish species following removal of certain offshore oil and gas platforms, suggesting that changes to certain fish assemblages or fish populations may be temporary or species or location dependent.102 Some also caution that the stock-enhancing potential of these structures may vary among reef fish species and may depend on additional factors (e.g., artificial reef age, proximity to natural habitats, maturity of resident fishes, fishing pressure); they argue for continued monitoring to assess any enhancement potential for fishery populations.103

Relatedly, some stakeholders and researchers call for increased examination of how fish assemblages in artificial and natural habitats may differ and the implications for fishery populations.104 Other stakeholders and researchers argue for continued focus on natural and essential habitats to ensure the viability of commercially and ecologically important fish species, and assert that artificial habitats should provide comparable ecological value to natural habitats.105 Some also caution about any contamination or pollution effects that decommissioned platforms may have on marine environments and natural habitats.106 Such concerns in certain non-U.S. regions (e.g., North Sea) have led to prohibitions on decommissioned platforms' use as artificial reefs,107 while other non-U.S. regions continue to explore their utility for enhancing biodiversity and fishing opportunities.108 Further, some suggest that investments in both natural and artificial habitats (including decommissioned oil and gas infrastructure), and concurrent efforts to sustainably manage fishing pressure, may be effective for ameliorating habitat loss and addressing management needs for certain fisheries.109

Other Alternatives for Offshore Infrastructure Reuse

Congress provided the Secretary of the Interior authority to regulate the use of offshore oil and gas structures for alternative marine and energy applications (Section 388 of the Energy Policy Act of 2005; P.L. 109-58).110 Some stakeholders assert that repurposing decommissioned offshore oil and gas platforms has the potential to turn an uneconomic platform into an asset for a new industry rather than a liability to the marine environment and other marine industries.111

Decommissioned offshore oil and gas platforms have been or could be reused for various purposes. For instance, in addition to fishing and diving uses discussed above (section on "Rigs-to-Reefs Program"), decommissioned platforms also could be used for offshore aquaculture for aquatic plants, shellfish, or finfish.112 Under this scenario, high-value marine species would be raised in net cages or other contained structures close to or directly off a platform. Potential drawbacks could include escape of non-native species; introduction of diseases and parasites to wild populations; and impacts associated with the application of drugs and antibiotics.113

Stakeholders also have proposed reusing decommissioned platforms for emerging offshore energy uses, including carbon capture and storage (CCS).114 CCS involves technologies that capture human-made carbon dioxide from point sources, such as power plants, with the aim of permanently storing the carbon underground.115 In 2021, Congress amended the OCSLA to allow DOI to issue leases, easements, and rights-of-way for CCS on the outer continental shelf (P.L. 117-58, §§40307 and 40343).116 Stakeholders have noted that some of the infrastructure associated with decommissioned oil and gas sites could be used or adapted to store carbon dioxide underground.117 In addition, some pipelines previously used to move petroleum products from the seabed to onshore could potentially be reversed to move carbon dioxide from onshore industrial sites to depleted oil and gas reservoirs in the seabed.118 Some stakeholders assert that adapting CCS to the offshore environment could accelerate and scale a proven method for reducing net greenhouse gas emissions.119 Others contend that offshore CCS is insufficiently tested and would create risks of leakage, compromise reservoir pressure, and pose other environmental challenges.120

Some governments and companies have launched space missions from the ocean as a means to avoid congested schedules at major land-based launch sites and to be closer to the equator (the Earth moves faster around its axis the further the distance from the poles, giving rockets a speed boost).121 Sea-based launches could take place from barges, modified vessels, or decommissioned offshore oil and gas platforms, among other structures. In July 2026, DOI sought public input regarding the potential use of existing offshore facilities on the OCS for space launch-related activities.122 SpaceX has explored using decommissioned oil and gas platforms for its sea-based launches. In 2020, SpaceX acquired two offshore platforms, but it ultimately sold them because they were not the "right platform."123 Launches from decommissioned oil and gas platforms would typically be limited to small rockets.124 Separate from proposals for space launches, in 2024, a report indicated that the U.S. Navy was supporting research into a concept that would repurpose decommissioned offshore oil and gas platforms into mobile missile defense and resupply bases.125 Repurposing, and potentially relocating, a standing platform for space missions or missile defense purposes would likely require some decommissioning steps, such as permanently plugging wells and severing well casings. Some note that launches from the ocean can reduce the potential for impacts and accidents near human population centers.126 Others contend that rocket emissions and chemical pollution associated with launches may pose threats to the marine environment.127

Decommissioned platforms and their associated equipment (e.g., foundations, cables) could potentially be used for offshore wind development.128 Offshore wind farms generally are composed of multiple wind turbines that require specific site and operational conditions. To repurpose offshore oil and gas jacket foundations for this purpose, offshore wind developers might have to store jacket foundations onshore to install at approved sites at a later date (Figure 1).129 In addition, the topsides would have to be repurposed to support turbines. Retrofitting platforms and their associated equipment (e.g., making structural modifications and operational adjustments) for offshore wind applications might come at an additional unknown cost.130

Stakeholders also have proposed repurposing offshore oil and gas platforms and pipelines to transport energy products generated offshore, notably green hydrogen (i.e., hydrogen produced via an electrolyzer using electricity from renewable energy sources).131 Under this concept, produced hydrogen would be transported to shore using pipelines that were previously used for transporting petroleum products and have been converted to accommodate hydrogen.132 Such repurposed platforms might be collocated with renewable energy generation or have electricity delivered via undersea cable. Other proposed uses for decommissioned offshore oil and gas platforms have included desalination infrastructure, marine and weather research and monitoring, and electrical hubs for wind-, wave-, and current-generated energy.133

Issues and Legislation in the 119th Congress

Congress has debated issues and potential actions related to offshore oil and gas infrastructure decommissioning. Topics of interest have included decommissioning enforcement, offshore lessees' financial strength and bonding requirements, federal appropriations for decommissioning activities, and artificial reefing and the Rigs-to-Reefs program. Congress may continue to consider legislation regarding these topics and to conduct oversight of DOI's offshore decommissioning activities and policies.

Decommissioning Enforcement

Both GAO and the DOI Office of Inspector General have identified DOI's enforcement of offshore decommissioning regulations and deadlines as a management challenge for the federal government.134 As noted above (section on "Other Potential Decommissioning Enforcement Mechanisms"), GAO found in 2024 that DOI's tools for compelling operator compliance with decommissioning regulations and deadlines have generally not been effective. GAO recommended that Congress consider enacting oversight mechanisms "such as requiring annual reporting [by DOI] on the status of decommissioning enforcement efforts and associated liabilities."135 As of February 2026, GAO stated on its website that this recommendation to Congress remains "Open," as do related recommendations to DOI regarding steps the agencies could take to more proactively enforce regulations.136 However, GAO noted on its website steps taken by BSEE in 2024 and 2025 to review its regulations and policies with the aim of ensuring more consistent decommissioning enforcement.137

Bills in the 119th Congress (H.R. 8099, S. 4239) would require the Secretary of the Interior to submit to Congress, and make public, an annual report on the numbers of offshore oil and gas wells, platforms, and pipelines for which decommissioning is overdue, the numbers of decommissioning applications received and approved by DOI, and the status of enforcement actions, including notices of incidents of noncompliance, civil penalties, and operator disqualifications, among other related data. In the past, legislators also have proposed other actions, such as imposing fees on idled wells as an incentive to operators to engage in timely decommissioning.138 In considering such proposals, legislators may weigh federal liabilities from delayed decommissioning, potential burdens to industry from proposed enforcement actions, agency staff capacity to administer and report on decommissioning compliance, and other factors.

Lessees' Financial Strength and Bonding Requirements

Several bills in the 119th Congress address lessees' financial strength and DOI's financial assurance requirements. As discussed above (sections on "Financial Assurance" and "Changes to Financial Assurance Regulations"), under the Biden Administration DOI promulgated new criteria for determining the financial assurance offshore operators must provide. The department projected the new criteria would increase the overall amount of financial assurance required from regulated operators. The Trump Administration has proposed revisions to repeal some of these changes and institute other criteria that are anticipated to shrink overall bond amounts required from operators. Bills in the 119th Congress would make additional changes that differ from both the Biden and Trump Administration rules; some of these changes were considered but not implemented by the Biden Administration.

H.R. 9034 and S. 4715 would amend the OCSLA to establish "fitness-to-operate" standards for offshore lessees and holders of easements and rights-of-way. Prior to issuing, extending, or transferring a lease, easement, or right-of-way, DOI would be required to certify that the party seeking the authorization is fit to operate, based on the party's past compliance record (including with respect to decommissioning), its financial solvency, and other criteria. These bills also would require every offshore leaseholder to make payments to a decommissioning escrow account on a schedule established by DOI, with timing and amounts determined so that within five years the account balance would be sufficient to fully meet the estimated costs of decommissioning the infrastructure on the lease. (After completion of decommissioning, any remaining balances would be returned to the lessee.) Separately, H.R. 9035 would address the issue of infrastructure orphaned through operator bankruptcies by providing that payment of expenses related to "the complete cleanup of fossil fuel operations and retirement of fossil fuel assets" (for both onshore and offshore leases) would have priority in bankruptcy proceedings under specified terms.

Federal Appropriations for Decommissioning

Starting in FY2022, BSEE requested annual appropriations for a new Offshore Decommissioning budget activity to support its decommissioning work on orphaned infrastructure (i.e., cases where operator bankruptcies have left the federal government responsible for decommissioning infrastructure). As discussed above under "Financial Assurance," as of October 2026, DOI reported that the government was carrying $129.9 million in uncovered liability for existing orphaned facilities, after applying the base and supplemental financial assurance provided by lessees for these facilities.139 BSEE requested $30.0 million for the Offshore Decommissioning budget activity in each of FY2022-FY2024, $12.0 million in FY2025, and no funding in each of FY2026 and FY2027. Congress provided $3.0 million for this activity in each of FY2022 and FY2023 and $2.9 million in each of FY2024-FY2026. During this time, some appropriations committee reports expressed "strong disagreement that the ultimate responsibility for these activities should fall to the American taxpayer," and expressed that the appropriated funding is to give the agency a "foundation" for contracting decommissioning work, but "once current identified decommissioning needs are provided for," this line item would be discontinued.140 For FY2027, House committee-reported H.R. 9171 would again provide $2.9 million for this account. (As of October 2026, no full-year FY2027 Interior and Related Agencies appropriations bill had been introduced in the Senate.)

Artificial Reefing

Some Members of Congress have introduced legislation and discussed issues regarding the use of decommissioned oil and gas facilities as artificial reefs (see "Rigs-to-Reefs Program" for further discussion). In the 119th Congress, H.R. 5745 would amend the National Fishing Enhancement Act of 1984 (33 U.S.C. §§2101-2106) to expand on current provisions governing the construction and management of artificial reefs and include specific requirements for decommissioned oil and gas infrastructure. Among other provisions, the bill would amend the definition of artificial reef and allow an eligible party responsible for decommissioning an inactive structure to reef in place that structure following submission to BSEE of a notice of intent to reef and approval by the agency.141 The bill would require BSEE (or a delegated individual such as the eligible party), in consultation with NOAA, to assess the "localized corals, fish species, and other marine life that use the inactive structure," as well as the economic benefits of reefing in place.142 The agencies would determine eligibility to reef in place based on whether an established reef ecosystem is present or potentially may develop "on, under, or in the immediate vicinity" of that structure, or as appropriate to criteria in the National Artificial Reef Plan.143 Following such a determination, the authorized party could reef in place the eligible structure if certain environmental, navigational, and liability transfer criteria also had been met and if the Secretary of the Interior had determined that the structure is "sound and secure." The bill would amend OCSLA to prohibit removal of an inactive structure while its eligibility for reefing in place is pending or under other specific circumstances.144

The House Committee on Natural Resources (HNR), Subcommittee on Energy and Mineral Resources (EM), held a hearing on the bill in January 2026 and ordered the bill reported in September 2026.145 During the hearing, some Members of Congress and witnesses highlighted progress in states' implementations of Rigs-to-Reefs programs, including scientific findings regarding the use of certain decommissioned platforms by various fishery species and corresponding patterns in recreational fishing activity.146 Some contended that the bill would assist with supporting state-led Rigs-to-Reefs programs and comparable artificial reef siting efforts.147 Alternatively, some Members and witnesses expressed concerns about potential environmental impacts from decommissioned infrastructure, as well as broad definitions of terminology regarding marine ecosystems in the bill language, and stated that H.R. 5745 would allow oil and gas companies to receive financial or regulatory benefits, such as avoiding responsibilities of towing old rigs back to shore.148 Further, some Members and witnesses noted that investments in protecting and restoring natural habitats, such as nearshore nurseries, may more effectively address habitat-related bottlenecks for fishery populations, as suggested by some scientists.149

In considering these proposals and findings, Congress may evaluate the practicality of codifying specific directives for the conversion of decommissioned oil and gas infrastructure to artificial reefs or leaving such considerations to authorized agencies' discretion in accordance with the National Artificial Reef Plan. Relatedly, Congress may evaluate whether proposed approaches in H.R. 5745 would create additional efficiencies or challenges for carrying out current Rigs-to-Reefs programs, such as resource and staffing challenges identified by NOAA.150 Further, Congress may examine the utility of artificial and natural habitats in supporting and enhancing fishery populations and whether adjustments to appropriations regarding associated restoration and siting efforts may be necessary. Congress also may examine whether further research regarding the effectiveness of Rigs-to-Reefs and other related programs for fishery populations and marine ecosystem functioning may be warranted and the applicability of current findings for informing conservation and management objectives. Outside the context of H.R. 5745, if DOI proceeds with its announced intention to initiate a regulatory revision to make toppling in place its default decommissioning option (see section on "Decommissioning in Place"), Congress may conduct oversight of any proposed regulatory changes and their implementation. Congress also may conduct oversight of agencies' implementation of the EO, "Restoring American Saltwater Angling and Recreation," including considerations with respect to authorities governing living marine resources, artificial reefs, and recreational fisheries.151
