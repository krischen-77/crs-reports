The Social Security program pays monthly cash benefits to retired or disabled workers and their      Specialist in Social Policy
family members and to the family members of deceased workers. Social Security’s receipts and
expenditures are accounted for through two federal trust funds: the Federal Old-Age and
Survivors Insurance (OASI) Trust Fund and the Federal Disability Insurance (DI) Trust Fund.
The trust funds represent funds dedicated to pay current and future Social Security benefits.

The OASI and DI programs each have three sources of income: payroll tax revenues, federal income taxes paid by some
beneficiaries on a portion of their benefits, and interest income from the investment of asset reserves in U.S. government
securities. Payroll tax revenues provide about 90% of program income, federal income taxes on Social Security benefits
provide about 5% of program income, and interest provides about 5% of program income. By law, Social Security tax
revenues must be invested in U.S. government obligations (debt instruments of the U.S. government). The OASI and DI
programs each have three sources of cost: monthly benefits, administrative costs, and costs associated with the Railroad
Retirement Board interchange. Monthly benefits typically account for over 99% of program costs. The interactions between
these revenues, costs, and trust fund balances (i.e., accumulated excess revenues from previous years) determine each
program’s ability to pay full scheduled benefits on time.

According to the 2026 annual report of the Social Security Board of Trustees, the OASI trust fund has been
operating under an annual deficit—total costs exceeding total revenues—since 2021. Furthermore, the Social
Security trustees project this financial shortfall to continue indefinitely. Specifically, the trustees show that while
OASI’s total revenues are increasing, the program’s total costs are increasing more rapidly. This trend can exist,
with the OASI program able to pay full benefits, only so long as a positive trust fund balance exists to cover the
shortfall.

Under their intermediate assumptions and current law, the Social Security trustees project that the OASI trust fund will
become depleted (i.e., insolvent) in 2032, whereas the DI trust fund will not become depleted in the 75-year projection
period. The two trust funds are legally distinct and do not have authority to borrow from each other. However, Congress has
authorized the shifting of funds between OASI and DI in the past to address shortfalls in a particular fund. For this reason,
and because of the projected insolvency of the OASI trust fund, the two trust funds are often considered on a combined (i.e.,
OASDI) basis. The trustees project that the combined Social Security trust funds would become depleted in 2034. At the
point of trust fund depletion, a program’s (e.g., OASI’s) incoming revenue would be sufficient to pay only a fraction of the
benefits scheduled to be paid under current law. Trust fund insolvency does not mean that Social Security will be unable to
pay any benefits.

If a trust fund were to be depleted and current receipts were insufficient to cover current expenditures, there would be a
conflict between two federal laws. Under the Social Security Act, beneficiaries would still be legally entitled to their full
scheduled benefits. However, the Antideficiency Act prohibits government spending in excess of available funds, so the
Social Security Administration (SSA) would not have legal authority to pay full Social Security benefits on time.

The Social Security Act does not stipulate what happens to benefit payments if a trust fund becomes depleted. Thus, it is
unclear what specific actions SSA would take if a trust fund were insolvent. After depletion, a trust fund would continue to
receive tax revenues from which a portion of scheduled benefits could be paid. One option would be to pay full benefits on a
delayed schedule; another would be to make timely but reduced payments.

Eliminating OASI’s financial shortfall would require reductions in Social Security benefits, increases in tax revenues, or
some combination of the two. The sooner that Social Security policy changes are undertaken, the less abrupt the changes
would need to be, because they could be spread over a longer period and would therefore affect a larger number of workers
and beneficiaries. Enacting adjustments to Social Security policy sooner, rather than later, would give workers and
beneficiaries more time to plan and adjust their work and savings behavior.

Introduction

Known collectively as Social Security, the Old-Age and Survivors Insurance (OASI) and
Disability Insurance (DI) programs provide monthly cash benefits to eligible retired or disabled
workers and their dependents, and to the eligible survivors of deceased insured workers.1 The
programs, referred to on a combined basis as OASDI, are authorized under Title II of the Social
Security Act and are administered by the Social Security Administration (SSA).2

Workers obtain insurance by working for a sufficient number of years in jobs covered by Social
Security. A worker’s job is considered covered if the earnings derived from that job are subject to
the Social Security payroll tax and are thus creditable for program purposes. In 2026, an
estimated 185 million people (or about 94% of all workers) will work in paid employment or self-
employment covered by Social Security.3 In August 2026, more than 71 million people collected
Social Security benefits.4 Approximately 77% of those beneficiaries are retired workers, and
another 10% of those beneficiaries are disabled workers.

Monthly Social Security benefits are based on a retired or disabled worker’s career-average
earnings in jobs covered by Social Security. The monthly benefits are designed to replace a
portion of the income lost to a family due to the retirement, disability, or death of the worker. In
August 2026, the average monthly benefit for retired workers was $2,088, whereas the average
monthly benefit for disabled workers was $1,636.5 Family members of retired, disabled, or
deceased workers accounted for the remainder of the benefit population (about 13%) and
collected an average monthly benefit of $1,325.

Social Security’s receipts and expenditures are accounted for through two federal trust funds: the
Federal Old-Age and Survivors Insurance (OASI) Trust Fund and the Federal Disability
Insurance (DI) Trust Fund. The trust funds represent funds dedicated to pay current and future
Social Security benefits. The OASI and DI programs’ ability to pay the full amount of scheduled
monthly benefits depends on the relationship between the revenues, costs, and asset reserves held
in the respective trust fund.

Each year when the Social Security trustees release their annual report, attention is focused on the
projected year in which the Social Security trust funds will become depleted (i.e., the year in
which the amount of asset reserves falls to zero). In their 2026 report, the trustees project that the
OASI trust fund will become depleted in 2032, whereas the DI trust fund is not projected to
become depleted at any time in the next 75 years.6 The trustees project, on a hypothetical
combined basis, that the OASDI trust fund would be depleted sometime in 2034. These
projections, and the projections presented in this report, are based on the trustees’ intermediate
assumptions, which reflect their best understanding of the status of the Social Security trust funds

The Social Security Trust Funds

Within the U.S. Treasury, numerous accounts are in place for internal accounting purposes.
Although all the monies within the U.S. Treasury are federal monies, the designation of an
account as a trust fund allows the government to track revenues dedicated for specific purposes
(as well as expenditures). In addition, the government can affect the level of revenues and
expenditures associated with a trust fund through changes in the law.

Social Security program revenues (i.e., income or receipts) and costs (i.e., outgo or expenditures)
are accounted for in two separate trust funds authorized under Title II of the Social Security Act:
(1) the Federal Old-Age and Survivors Insurance (OASI) Trust Fund and (2) the Federal
Disability Insurance (DI) Trust Fund.10 The OASI trust fund was created by the Social Security
Act Amendments of 1939 (P.L. 76-379) and superseded the Old-Age Reserve Account established
by the original Social Security Act in 1935 (P.L. 74-271).11 The DI trust fund was established as
part of the Social Security Amendments of 1956 (P.L. 84-880).12 The trust funds have existed on a

In the federal accounting structure, a trust fund is an accounting mechanism used by the
Department of the Treasury to track and report receipts dedicated for spending on specific
purposes, as well as expenditures made to beneficiaries that are financed by those receipts, in
accordance with the terms of a statute that designates the fund as a trust fund.13 The OASI trust
fund records receipts and expenditures associated with retired workers, their dependents, and
survivors of deceased insured workers. The DI trust fund records receipts and expenditures
associated with disabled workers and their dependents. Under current law, the two trust funds are
legally distinct and do not have authority to borrow from each other. However, several times in
the past, lawmakers have authorized the reallocation of the Social Security payroll tax rate and
have authorized interfund borrowing to preserve the solvency of the two trust funds.14 This
precedent is important given projections showing that the asset reserves held by the OASI fund
will be depleted in 2032, whereas the asset reserves held by the DI fund are not projected to be
depleted at any time during the next 75-year period.15 Because of the prior authorization to shift
funds between OASI and DI, analysts often treat the two funds collectively on a hypothetical
basis as the combined OASDI trust funds.

A government trust fund has different attributes than private sector trust funds. The Government
Accountability Office (GAO) notes the following:

         In the federal budget the meaning of the term “trust” differs significantly from its private
         sector usage. In the private sector, a person creates a private trust fund using his or her own
         assets to benefit a stated individual(s). The creator of the trust names a trustee who has a
         fiduciary responsibility to manage the designated assets in accordance with the stipulations
         of the trust. In the federal sector, the Congress creates a federal trust fund in law and
         designates a funding source to benefit stated groups or individuals. However, in contrast to
        a private trust fund, the federal government does not have a fiduciary responsibility to the
          trust beneficiaries, and it can raise or lower future trust fund collections and payments or
        change the purposes for which the collections are used by changing existing laws.
        Moreover, the federal government has custody and control of the funds as well as the
        earnings of most federal trust funds.16

government as a whole, a trust fund—such as the two Social Security trust funds—is required to
be available for a specific program or use.17 Under current law, monies held in the Social Security
trust funds may be spent only on program costs. In the case of Social Security, costs are largely
synonymous with benefit payments. It is argued that the decision to tie a program to a trust fund

Social Security Trust Funds and Investment

The Secretary of the Treasury is required by law to invest Social Security revenues in securities
backed by the U.S. government (i.e., special U.S. government obligations).21 These securities are
held in the Social Security trust funds, and the trust funds receive interest on these holdings. Each
U.S. government security issued by the U.S. Treasury for purchase by the Social Security trust
funds must be a paper instrument in the form of a bond, note, or certificate of indebtedness.22

Any interest or proceeds from the sale of U.S. government securities held by the Social Security
trust funds must be paid in the form of paper checks from the General Fund of the Treasury to the
Social Security trust funds.23 The interest rates paid on the securities issued to the Social Security
trust funds are tied to market rates. Specifically, for any newly-issued security, the average yield
is calculated as the average market yield on any marketable interest-bearing security of the
federal government that is not due or callable until after four years from the last business day of
the prior month, rounded to the nearest eighth of one percent.24

For internal federal accounting purposes, when special U.S. government obligations are
purchased by the Social Security trust funds, the U.S. Treasury is shifting surplus Social Security
revenues from one government account (the Social Security trust funds) to another government
account (the General Fund). The special U.S. government obligations are physical documents
held by SSA, not the U.S. Treasury. The securities held by the Social Security trust funds are
redeemed on a regular basis. These special U.S. government obligations, however, are not

Social Security Trust Funds and Off-Budget Status

For federal budget purposes, on-budget status generally refers to programs that are included in the
annual congressional budget process, whereas off-budget status generally refers to programs that
are not included in the annual congressional budget process.

Social Security’s receipts and most of its outlays are designated by law as off-budget. The off-
budget designation has no practical effect on program funding, spending, or operations. The
annual congressional budget resolution, in its legislative language, separates the off-budget totals
from the on-budget totals. The report language accompanying the congressional budget resolution
usually shows the unified budget totals (which combine the on- and off-budget amounts) as well
as the separate on- and off-budget totals. The President’s budget tends to use the unified budget
measures when referring to the budget totals. The President’s budget documents also include the
totals for the on- and off-budget components, as required by law. The Congressional Budget
Office uses the unified budget numbers in its analyses of the budget; it generally does not include
on- and off-budget data in its regular annual reports.

The unified budget framework is important because it includes all federal receipts and outlays,
providing a more comprehensive picture of the size of the federal government and the federal
budget’s impact on the economy. In the unified budget, the Social Security program is a large
source of both federal receipts and federal outlays.26 For purposes of the unified budget, the
annual Social Security cash flow surplus or deficit is counted in determining the overall federal
budget surplus or deficit.27

Social Security Trust Funds and the Level of Federal Debt

As part of the annual congressional budget process, the level of federal debt (the federal debt
limit) is established for the budget by Congress. The federal debt limit includes debt held by the
public, as well as the internal debt of the U.S. government (i.e., debt held by government
accounts). Borrowing from the public and the investment of the Social Security trust funds in
special U.S. government obligations both fall under the restrictions of the federal debt limit. As a
result, the balance of the Social Security trust funds has implications for the federal debt limit.28

Program Financing

Social Security program financing determines the program’s ability to pay benefits to current and
future beneficiaries. Revenues for the OASI and DI programs are credited to their respective trust
funds, whereas costs for the OASI and DI programs are debited from their respective trust funds.

Sources of Income

Social Security has three sources of income or revenue: (1) payroll tax revenues, (2) tax revenues
from the taxation of benefits, and (3) interest earned on asset reserves held in the trust funds.
Taken together, payroll tax revenues and tax revenues from the taxation of benefits are referred to
more generally as tax revenues or non-interest income. Interest earned on asset reserves held in
the trust funds is commonly referred to as interest income.

Payroll Taxes

Payroll taxes provided under the Federal Insurance Contributions Act (FICA) and Self-
Employment Contribution Act (SECA) are the primary source of financing for both the OASI and
DI programs.29 FICA taxes are paid by both employers and employees, but it is employers who
remit the taxes to the U.S. Treasury. Employers remit FICA taxes on a regular basis throughout
the year (e.g., weekly, monthly, quarterly, or annually), depending on the employer’s level of total
employment taxes (including FICA and federal personal income tax withholding). For past FICA
and SECA tax rates, see Table A-1.

Under current law, the FICA tax rate is 7.65% of covered earnings for each employees and
employers (i.e., each party pays 7.65%, yielding a combined 15.3% payroll tax).30 The FICA tax
has two components: 6.2% of covered earnings for Social Security and 1.45% of covered
earnings for Medicare Hospital Insurance (HI).31 The SECA tax rate is 15.3% of covered earnings
for self-employed individuals, with 12.4% of covered earnings for Social Security and 2.9% of
covered earnings for Medicare HI.32

The Social Security portions of FICA and SECA taxes are split between the two Social Security
trust funds. FICA taxes are divided, with both employees and employers paying 5.3% of covered

Taxation of Benefits

The second type of tax revenue credited to the Social Security trust funds is revenue from the
taxation of benefits.39 Beginning in 1984, up to 50% of Social Security benefits became subject to
federal income tax for beneficiaries whose provisional income exceeds $25,000 and couples
whose provisional income exceeds $32,000.40 The proceeds from taxing up to 50% of Social
Security benefits are credited to the trust funds. Beginning in 1994, up to 85% of Social Security
benefits became taxable if provisional income exceeds $34,000 for single-filing beneficiaries and
$44,000 for couples. Proceeds from taxes on the first 50% of Social Security benefits are credited
to the trust fund (e.g., OASI or DI) from which they were paid. Proceeds from taxes on Social
Security benefits that exceed 50% are credited to the Medicare HI trust fund, regardless of
whether benefits were paid from the OASI or DI trust funds.41

The two sets of thresholds mentioned above are fixed in law. That is, unlike the CBB (i.e., taxable
maximum), they are not indexed. Because the thresholds are fixed, the share of Social Security
beneficiaries subject to income taxation of a portion of their Social Security benefits is expected
to increase as overall economy-wide wages and monthly benefit amounts increase.42 Recent
projections suggest that more than half of Social Security beneficiaries will be subject to federal
income taxes on a portion of their Social Security benefits each year between now and 2050.43

Sources of Cost

Social Security has three sources of cost: (1) monthly benefits, (2) administrative costs, and (3)
costs associated with the Railroad Retirement Board (RRB) interchange. On a combined basis,
monthly benefits typically account for over 99% of program costs.50 Conversely, when taken
together, administrative costs and transfers required for the RRB interchange account for less than
1% of program costs.

Monthly Benefits

The Social Security program pays benefits on a monthly schedule to all program beneficiaries.
Retirement benefits and survivors benefits are paid out (i.e., debited) from the OASI trust fund;
disability benefits are paid out (i.e., debited) from the DI trust fund.

In aggregate nominal dollars, the amount paid in monthly benefits has increased in every year of
the program’s history.51 The total amount of monthly benefits can be thought of as the product of
average benefits and the number of beneficiaries, both of which are projected to increase in future
years. (Figure A-1 and Figure A-2 show the intermediate projections for the number of
beneficiaries and initial monthly benefits by earnings level.) As such, the total amount of
scheduled benefits (i.e., benefits calculated under current law) are projected to increase as well.

                         Social Security Benefit Calculation
  Social Security benefits for retired and disabled workers are calculated using the same benefit computation
  process. The computation process includes three main steps. First, a summarized measure of lifetime Social
  Security-covered earnings is computed. This measure is called the average indexed monthly earnings, or AIME.
  Second, a progressive benefit formula is applied to a worker’s AIME to compute his or her primary insurance
 amount, or PIA. The PIA is commonly referred to as the basic monthly benefit (i.e., the monthly benefit before any
  adjustments are applied). Third, adjustments are applied to the worker’s PIA. The two most common adjustments
  are cost-of-living adjustments and adjustments for claiming age. Benefits for eligible dependents and survivors are
  based on a worker’s PIA. For a detailed explanation of Social Security benefits, including example calculations, see
 CRS Report R46658, Social Security: Benefit Calculation.

Cash Flow Balances

Cash flow balances show the difference between the OASI and DI programs’ current revenues
and current costs. This measure does not consider interest income earned on asset reserves held in
the respective trust funds, as these are generally earned based on accumulated excess revenues
from prior years. When noninterest income (i.e., tax revenues) exceeds total cost, the program has
a cash flow surplus. When noninterest income (i.e., tax revenues) is less than total costs, the
program has a cash flow deficit. If either program operates a cash flow deficit, it may use interest
income or accumulated holdings in its trust fund to help cover costs. Table 9 shows that the OASI
program has operated cash flow deficits (i.e., negative cash flow balances) for the past five years

program has operated cash flow surpluses in each of the past five years, a trend that is expected to

hypothetical OASDI basis, the combined trust funds are projected to experience cash flow deficits

                      Operating with Cash Flow Deficits
 When a trust fund (e.g., OASI) operates with annual cash flow deficits, the U.S. Treasury can continue to pay
  benefits scheduled under current law as long as the accumulated balance in the trust fund is sufficient to cover the
  costs. This is because the Social Security program has budget authority to pay benefits in full and on time if there
  is a positive balance in the designated account. However, if Social Security cannot cover benefits, the U.S.
 government must raise the funds necessary to pay back the bonds held in the trust funds. To do this, the
 government can increase taxes or other income, reduce non-Social Security spending, borrow from the public
  (i.e., by replacing bonds held by the trust funds with bonds held by the public), or a combination of these
  measures.

Annual Balances

The occurrence of cash flow deficits—total costs exceeding noninterest revenues (i.e., tax
revenues)—does not necessarily indicate an inability of a trust fund to pay full scheduled benefits
(i.e., remain solvent).

         In fact, in a perfectly pay-as-you-go (PAYGO) financing approach, with the assets in the
          trust fund maintained consistently at the level of a “contingency reserve” targeted at one
         year’s cost for the program, the program might well be in a position of having negative
        cash flow on a permanent basis. This would occur when the interest rate on the trust fund
         assets is greater than the rate of growth in program cost. In this case, interest on the trust
        fund assets would be more than enough to grow the assets as fast as program cost, leaving
       some of the interest available to augment current tax revenue to meet current cost.58

If a trust fund’s total revenues (i.e., noninterest income plus interest income) were to exceed total
cost, the program would be operating an annual surplus. On one hand, a program could operate
simultaneous cash flow deficits and annual surpluses, wherein the cash flow deficits were offset
by interest income, allowing for full scheduled benefits to be paid.

On the other hand, the ability of a trust fund to pay full benefits could be weakened by sustained
annual deficits where total revenues (i.e., noninterest income plus interest income) are less than
total cost. A trust fund experiencing sustained cash flow deficits large enough to result in
declining trust fund reserves would eventually threaten a program’s ability to remain solvent.
That is, a trust fund cannot sustain continued cash flow deficits and annual deficits.

Asset Reserves Held in the Trust Funds

When a trust fund spends more than it receives in tax revenue and interest income, as it would
under an annual deficit, it can redeem accumulated assets to pay scheduled benefits. Essentially,
an annual deficit can be offset by drawing from accumulated assets (i.e., excess revenues accrued
during years of annual surpluses). This process—redeeming assets reserves (i.e., bonds) held in
the trust funds—results in a decrease in trust fund balances and can only occur when a positive
trust fund balance exists.

Historical Combined Trust Fund Operations

The trust funds have run annual surpluses in most years. Except for the first decades of the
program and a few years beginning in the late 1960s, these annual surpluses were typically small
relative to the size of the trust funds’ expenditures. Beginning in 1975, the combined trust funds
ran annual deficits. The trust funds made up the difference between revenues and cost during
these years by redeeming some of the bonds accumulated in earlier years.

The Social Security program experienced significant financial difficulties during the mid-1970s
through the early 1980s. A combination of high inflation, high unemployment, and slow real
wage growth during this period caused the program’s costs to exceed its income, which eroded
the balances of the Social Security trust funds. To address these challenges, Congress passed a
series of legislative measures aimed at mitigating program costs and increasing revenues.60

What Caused the Projected Shortfall?

The projected shortfall is caused by demographic factors (e.g., fertility, mortality, and
immigration), economic factors (e.g., wage growth, price growth, and productivity), and program-
specific factors (e.g., automatically adjusted parameters). Considered together, projections of
these factors predict a continued trend of program costs growing faster than program revenues.

Projected demographic, economic, and program-specific factors affect all aspects of the future
financial status of the program, such as the number of covered workers and their levels of
earnings, as well as the size and makeup of the beneficiary population and the level of monthly
benefits. Assumptions for all three categories of factors contribute to the projected imbalance. For
instance, economic factors—such as recessions—could lead to lower-than-expected payroll tax
revenues and higher-than-expected costs.

According to the trustees, the growing difference between costs and revenues is largely
attributable to demographic factors. Demographic factors alone are expected to cause the costs to
increase markedly in coming years. The trustees write, “Under the intermediate assumptions,
demographic factors cause the projected cost rate to rise rapidly until about 2085.”74 Specifically,
the U.S. population has been experiencing a shift in age structure toward older ages and an
increase in the median age, termed demographic aging. Two demographic effects have
contributed to this aging over time: decreasing fertility and increasing longevity. The Social
Security population’s changing age distribution is creating a situation in which fewer workers in
covered employment are supporting a growing number of people collecting benefits (see Table
A-3).75 This relationship is temporarily sustainable, as the OASI program can draw upon the $2.1
trillion in asset reserves (i.e., the trust fund balance at the beginning of 2026) held in the trust
funds to augment program revenues and fulfill all scheduled benefit payments. However, the
OASI program’s ability to pay 100% of scheduled benefits becomes unsustainable when these
asset reserves are depleted.

What Happens to Benefits in the Case of Insolvency?

The Social Security Act states that every individual who meets program eligibility requirements is
entitled to benefits.80 As an entitlement program, the federal government is legally obligated to
pay Social Security benefits to all those who are eligible for them as set forth in the statute.81 If
the federal government fails to pay the benefits stipulated by law, beneficiaries could take legal
action. Insolvency would not relieve the government of its obligation to provide benefits.

However, the Social Security Act specifies that benefit payments shall be made only from the
trust funds.82 Another law, the Antideficiency Act, prohibits government spending in excess of
available funds.83 Consequently, if the Social Security trust funds become insolvent—that is, if
current tax receipts and accumulated assets are not sufficient to pay the benefits to which people
are entitled, as the OASI trust fund is projected to experience in 2032—the law effectively
prohibits full Social Security benefits from being paid on time.

The Antideficiency Act prohibits government agencies from paying for benefits, goods, or
services beyond the limit authorized in law for such payments. The authorized limit in law for
Social Security benefits is the balance of the trust fund. The Social Security Act does not stipulate
what would happen to benefit payments if the balance of the trust funds is not sufficient to cover

Possible Scenario #2: Reduced On-Time Payments

The 2026 annual trustees report states:

         After reserves are depleted, the trust funds would be unable to pay scheduled benefits in
          full on a timely basis if annual income were less than annual cost.87

This language is seemingly more neutral than previous (i.e., 1982) language and could be
interpreted to mean reduced benefits could be paid on a timely basis after a trust fund reached
insolvency. Paying reduced but timely benefits could take many forms. For instance, all benefits
could be reduced proportionally across the board, such that projected payable benefits in any year
would resemble those in Figure 2. In such a scenario, all beneficiaries would experience the same
percentage reduction in benefit amounts. Alternatively, reduced benefits could be structured to
protect certain groups, such as disabled or low-income beneficiaries. For instance, some
researchers argue that in the event of trust fund insolvency, the President has “considerable
discretion to allocate those limited funds in a reasonable manner.”88 This research further posits
that the President could not reduce total benefits paid by more than an amount needed to ensure
costs do not exceed revenues, and that the President could not provide benefits to any
beneficiaries (i.e., low-income beneficiaries) that were higher than calculated under law.89

What Can Congress Do?

Under current law and intermediate assumptions, the trustees estimate that in about six years the
OASI program will be unable to pay scheduled benefits in full and on time. Generally speaking,
the types of measures needed to eliminate the projected shortfall and maintain the program’s
solvency can be characterized as revenue-increasing or cost-reducing.

In practice, a wide range of solvency-related measures are available to lawmakers. For instance,
each year the SSA’s Office of the Chief Actuary updates a list of financial effects for over 100
commonly proposed solvency-related measures.90 The Congressional Budget Office provides a
similar function in its Options for Reducing the Deficit and Social Security Policy Options.91
These reports generally agree that no single provision is likely to entirely eliminate the financial
shortfall.92 For this reason, many proposals addressing the program’s finances include both
