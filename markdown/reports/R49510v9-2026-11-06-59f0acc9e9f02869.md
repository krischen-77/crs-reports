The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026 (BAAJA; S. 5653) was introduced      Legislative Attorney
on September 30, 2026. The bill as introduced contains seven titles within two divisions, the first
of which—Division A—aims to reform certain federal environmental review and permitting
processes (e.g., those contained within the National Environmental Policy Act [NEPA], Clean
Water Act, Endangered Species Act) and provide new provisions associated with project
certainty and judicial review. Division B contains reforms to the National Historic Preservation Act and has provisions
focused on specific project types (e.g., electricity transmission lines, natural gas pipelines, geothermal energy development),
including some broader reforms to permitting processes and reviews across project types. This report provides background on
the permitting and environmental review processes. It also analyzes BAAJA provisions specific to Division A, Title I, which
primarily include proposed amendments to NEPA (Subtitles A [§§1101-1110] and B [§1121]) and provisions broadly related
to federal permitting improvements (Subtitle C [§1131-1133]) as compared to existing NEPA requirements.

Section 1101 of BAAJA would specify that NEPA prescribes a procedure to inform the public about environmental effects of
federal actions, describes the result of the NEPA process as an informational document, and does not mandate a particular
outcome. Section 1102 would reorganize NEPA’s definitions, remove two existing definitions, modify several existing
definitions, add 15 new definitions, and make conforming amendments. For example, it would expand the list of exclusions
in the definition of “major federal action,” remove the definition of “participating Federal agencies,” and add definitions for
terms such as “authorization” and “make public.” Section 1103 would require federal agencies to update their agency-specific
NEPA procedures and direct the Council on Environmental Quality (CEQ) to promote standardized implementation of
NEPA. Section 1104 would require federal agencies to implement NEPA consistent with minimum functional requirements
and data and technology standards (as specified in BAAJA Section 1121). Section 1105 would amend the process for
determining whether and what level of environmental review applies to a proposed action.

Section 1106 would, among other changes, expand requirements governing the roles and responsibilities of lead, joint lead,
and cooperating agencies for federal decisions “relating to an authorization”; add interagency scheduling, coordination, and
deadline requirements; eliminate consideration of certain alternatives; specify aspects of CEQ’s role in resolving disputes
associated with participating agencies and deadlines; place limitations on when an agency is required to consider new
scientific or technical research; provide administrative consequences if an agency misses a deadline for an environmental
review or related authorization; expand judicial review for missed deadlines; and specify annual reporting requirements for
agencies and CEQ. Section 1107 would extend the timeline for reliance on programmatic environmental documents. Section
1108 would require CEQ to maintain a central public record of all categorical exclusions (CEs) and specify the criteria under
which agencies could use a listed CE maintained by another agency. Section 1109 would establish application completeness
and level of review determination deadlines and require project sponsors to submit specified permitting, stakeholder
engagement, and other information in a permitting-related application. Section 1110 would affect the timing, venue, available
remedies, and other considerations associated with judicial review of NEPA claims and potentially related claims, including a
150-day statute of limitations to file a claim and a general prohibition on any judicial remedy imposing vacatur or injunctive
relief if based on a NEPA violation, among other requirements.

Subtitle B (Section 1121) would direct CEQ to lead development of electronic tools for environmental reviews and
authorizations and create an interconnected federal permitting data system with a common and publicly accessible
authorization portal. Section 1121 would also increase authorized annual CEQ appropriations from $1 million to $5 million.
Subtitle C (Sections 1131-1133) would require CEQ to maintain an online database to provide information on and track the
status of environmental reviews and related authorizations for specified environmental documents.

Overview

The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026 (BAAJA; S. 5653) was introduced
on September 30, 2026. The bill as introduced contains seven titles within two divisions, which
aim to reform certain federal environmental review and permitting processes to expedite a wide
range of project types, with an emphasis on energy-related projects.

BAAJA is organized according to two divisions:
    •   Division A—Committee on Environment and Public Works—has four titles: the
        National Environmental Policy Act of 1969 (NEPA), the Clean Water Act
      (CWA), the Endangered Species Act of 1973 (ESA), and Project Certainty.
    •   Division B—Committee on Energy and Natural Resources—has three titles:
        Electric Transmission, Energy Permitting, and Historic Preservation.

This report examines the subtitles and sections in BAAJA specific to NEPA (Division A, Title I):
   • Subtitle A—Declaration of Environmental Policy
      • Section 1101. Purposes; procedure; savings provision
      • Section 1102. Definitions
       • Section 1103. Implementing procedures
       • Section 1104. Cooperation of agencies; reports; availability of information;
           recommendations; international and national coordination of efforts
       • Section 1105. Procedure for determination of level of review
       • Section 1106. Timely and unified Federal reviews
       • Section 1107. Programmatic environmental document
       • Section 1108. Use of categorical exclusions by other agencies
       • Section 1109. Applications for authorizations
       • Section 1110. Judicial review
   • Subtitle B—Council on Environmental Quality
       • Section 1121. Council on Environmental Quality
   • Subtitle C—Federal Permitting Improvement
       • Section 1131. Definitions
       • Section 1132. Environmental Review and Permitting Tracker
       • Section 1133. Permitting Improvement Fund

The report provides background on the permitting and review provisions BAAJA would amend
and compares each NEPA-related provision in BAAJA to existing NEPA requirements.

Background on Environmental Reviews and Federal
Permitting

Certain activities that support the nation’s economy and infrastructure, including energy
production, transportation, and natural resource development, require one or more federal
authorizations.1 Many federal permitting processes (e.g., issuing permits, licensing, or granting
rights-of-way across federal lands) require compliance with the National Environmental Policy
Act of 1969, as amended (NEPA; 42 U.S.C. §§4321 et seq.). NEPA establishes a national policy
with respect to environmental quality and a process for integrating environmental considerations
into federal decisionmaking (i.e., through an environmental review). Although NEPA often
informs federal action—and thus decisions relating to certain authorizations—it does not itself
authorize or prohibit projects; rather, it operates alongside the substantive statutes that govern
individual federal authorizations.

Congress made substantial amendments to NEPA in the Fiscal Responsibility Act of 2023 (FRA;
P.L. 118-5).2 Among other changes, the FRA amended NEPA to address circumstances in which
the statute applies, the procedure for determining the level of review, page and time limits, and
the use of programmatic environmental documents.3 Congress amended NEPA again in the
FY2025 budget reconciliation act (P.L. 119-21) to allow project sponsors to pay a fee for
expedited environmental reviews.4 Congress has also enacted and considered a variety of other
measures that would affect various aspects of NEPA with respect to federal permitting processes.
One such example is Title 41 of the Fixing America’s Surface Transportation Act (“FAST-41”;
P.L. 114-94).5 Among other things, FAST-41 established the Federal Permitting Improvement
Steering Council (“Permitting Council”), authorized a streamlined administrative process for
certain covered infrastructure projects, and directed the Permitting Council to identify the types of
environmental reviews and authorizations most commonly required for certain types of
infrastructure projects. It also directed the Permitting Council to maintain an online database
known as the “Permitting Dashboard” to track the status of federal environmental reviews and
authorizations for covered projects.6 FAST-41 is an example of a separate statutory federal

Division A Title I of BAAJA would affect environmental reviews and certain procedural aspects
of federal permitting. NEPA’s environmental reviews serve as inputs to federal decisionmaking on
permitting and other federal activities. Statutory requirements for permitting processes other than
environmental reviews—such as deadlines and coordination between agencies—are not found in
NEPA but rather in other laws. BAAJA would amend NEPA to connect the environmental review
process to additional aspects of federal permitting and authorizations—for example, by setting a
unified schedule for the timing of certain decisions.

While NEPA’s environmental review requirements apply broadly across the federal government,
the specific federal permitting requirements and the agencies involved for any particular project
depend on factors such as the nature of the proposed action, applicable jurisdictions, and
anticipated environmental impacts. Though beyond the scope of this report, state and local
permits (e.g., zoning approvals, land use permits, and state-specific environmental certifications)
are often required in addition to the approvals or requirements imposed at the federal level.

Unless Congress exempts an action from environmental review, NEPA requires agencies, prior to
making final decisions, to assess reasonably foreseeable environmental effects, identify
“irreversible and irretrievable” resource commitments, and consider a reasonable range of
alternatives for “major Federal actions significantly affecting the quality of the human
environment.”8 A federal agency’s issuance of a permit or other authorization is often considered
a major federal action under NEPA.9 During an environmental review, agencies typically consider
and analyze social, ecological, and health impacts. NEPA does not require agencies to select the
most environmentally protective outcome or any other specific alternative option. Rather, for
major federal actions likely to have significant impacts, NEPA requires agencies to conduct
thorough analyses before making final decisions.

How a federal agency demonstrates compliance with NEPA depends on the level of the proposed
action’s impacts. If the proposed action has “a reasonably foreseeable significant effect on the
quality of the human environment,” an agency prepares an environmental impact statement
(EIS).10 If the proposed action “does not have a reasonably foreseeable significant effect on the
quality of the human environment, or if the significance of such effect is unknown,” an agency

NEPA establishes a procedural framework for evaluating the environmental impacts of a given
project and for informing federal agency decisionmaking; it does not itself confer authority to
approve or deny a project or replace the need for specific permits or other authorizations
mandated by regulatory statutes. In contrast, federal permitting statutes grant agencies substantive
decisionmaking authority and define the criteria under which an authorization may be issued. In
practice, agencies often integrate NEPA analyses with permitting requirements.13 NEPA
documents may inform permitting decisions; completion of NEPA does not guarantee issuance of
a permit, and a permit may be denied after an environmental document is finalized.
