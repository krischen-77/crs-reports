Brazil’s Political Situation
On October 25, 2026, President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva (Lula; 2003-2010, 2023-present) of the left-of-center Workers’
Party narrowly won a fourth term. He defeated Senator Flávio Bolsonaro of the right-wing Liberal Party in a second-round
runoff election, 51.8% to 48.2%. That is roughly double the margin by which Lula defeated Flávio’s father, then-incumbent
President Jair Bolsonaro (2019-2023), in the 2022 presidential runoff. Jair Bolsonaro is currently serving a 27-year prison
sentence under house arrest for attempting a coup d’état to overturn his election loss. Senator Bolsonaro initially stated that
he intended to challenge the results of the 2026 runoff, which have been recognized by an Organization of American States
election observation mission, many foreign governments, and most of Brazil’s political leadership, including Bolsonaro’s
party. Days later, on October 28, 2026, Senator Bolsonaro entered the United States, fleeing ongoing criminal investigations
in Brazil and claiming political persecution.

President Lula is to be inaugurated to his fourth four-year term on January 5, 2027. He has largely pledged continuity with
his current administration, which has focused on increasing Brazilians’ upward social mobility while addressing
environmental destruction in the Brazilian Amazon and expanding Brazil’s international influence. Lula has advanced
portions of his domestic agenda—such as stricter environmental enforcement—through executive action and secured
congressional approval for some of his proposed socioeconomic policies. The center-right Brazilian congress has blocked
some of Lula’s initiatives and appointments, however, and advanced some legislation opposed by the president. Both houses
of the Brazilian congress are to shift further to the right during Lula’s fourth term, which could result in increased inter-
branch conflict.

U.S.-Brazil Relations
Over the past decade, U.S.-Brazil relations have waxed and waned, depending, in part, on the relative ideological alignment
between the administrations in power. Bilateral relations have been strained since 2025. The second Trump Administration
imposed tariffs on goods imports from Brazil and sanctions on some Brazilian supreme court justices in response to the
criminal prosecution of former President Bolsonaro and the Brazilian judiciary’s approach to regulating social media, which
the Administration characterized as political persecution and censorship. The Lula administration has condemned these and
other U.S. actions—including the designation of two Brazilian criminal groups as foreign terrorist organizations—as
politically motivated attacks on Brazilian sovereignty. As of October 28, 2026, the Trump Administration had not recognized
the results of Brazil’s 2026 presidential election.

Congressional Action
Some Members of Congress have monitored developments in Brazil and sought to influence the trajectory of U.S.-Brazilian
relations. Environmental conservation has been a major area of focus for some Members. In the National Security,
Department of State, and Related Programs Appropriations Act, 2026 (P.L. 119-75, Division F), Congress designated
$38.0 million for environmental programs across the Amazon region. Some Members also have expressed concerns about
Brazilian judicial orders regulating social media platforms and have expressed support for the Trump Administration’s
sanctions against Brazilian supreme court justices who allegedly infringed on freedom of expression. Some other Members
have criticized the Trump Administration for purportedly using U.S. sanctions and trade policy to undermine Brazil’s
democracy and rule of law. Congress may assess whether—and, if so, how—to continue shaping U.S.-Brazil relations on
these and other issues as it considers FY2027 appropriations and exercises its other legislative and oversight prerogatives.

Brazil’s Political and Economic Situation

Background

Brazil declared independence from Portugal in 1822, initially establishing a constitutional
monarchy and retaining a slave-based, plantation economy. Approximately 5 million enslaved
Africans were forcibly transported to Brazil between the mid-1500s and 1888, when Brazil
abolished slavery, accounting for about 45% of all enslaved Africans brought to the Americas.5
Brazil became a republic in 1889, but economic and political power remained concentrated in the

During the 1910s and 1920s, for example, roughly 2%-3% of Brazil’s population voted.6 The
authoritarian government of Getúlio Vargas (1930-1945) began to incorporate the working classes
into politics but exerted strict control over labor as part of its broader push to centralize power in
the federal government. Vargas also began to implement a state-led development model, which
endured for much of the 20th century as successive governments supported the expansion of
Brazilian industry.

Brazil experienced two decades of multiparty democracy from 1945 to 1964 but struggled with
political and economic instability that ultimately led the military to seize power. A 1964 military
coup ushered in two decades of authoritarian rule. The U.S. government dispatched military
assets to potentially aid the pro-coup forces and expressed support for the post-coup government.7
Brazilian security forces killed at least 434 dissidents and detained and tortured an estimated
30,000-50,000 others during the dictatorship.8 The military government allowed the judiciary and
congress to nominally function during its tenure but stifled representative democracy and civic
action, carefully preserving its influence during Brazil’s protracted democratic transition back to
civilian democratic governance.9

Brazil restored civilian rule in 1985, and a national constituent assembly, elected in 1986,
promulgated a new constitution in 1988. The constitution divides power among three branches of
government: an executive branch led by a president with extensive policymaking authority; a
legislative branch consisting of the 513-member Chamber of Deputies and the 81-member
Senate; and an independent judicial branch charged with interpreting and applying the numerous
political, economic, and social rights enshrined in the constitution.10 The 1988 constitution also
established universal suffrage, eliminating literacy requirements that had disenfranchised more
than half of the population during the previous democratic era and disproportionately affected
Afro-Brazilians.11 Under Brazil’s federal structure, the national government shares authority with
26 states, a federal district that includes the capital city of Brasília, and 5,568 municipalities.
Organizations that attempt to track respect for democracy globally generally recognize Brazil’s
democracy for its competitive elections and political pluralism but identify some shortcomings,
including endemic corruption and high levels of political polarization and violence.12

Lula Administration (2023-Present)

Brazilian President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva (Lula) of the left-of-center Workers’ Party (Partido
dos Trabalhadores, or PT) was inaugurated to a third four-year term on January 1, 2023, marking
a significant political comeback. Lula originally rose to prominence during the 1970s as the
leader of Brazil’s metal workers union. He subsequently helped found the PT amid the push to
restore democracy in Brazil and led the party in three unsuccessful presidential bids before being
elected in 2002. Lula presided over a period of sustained economic growth and improving living
conditions in Brazil during his first two terms (2003-2010), and left office with an 87% approval
rating.13 He was convicted on corruption charges in 2017, however, and imprisoned for 19 months
before Brazil’s Federal Supreme Court (Supremo Tribunal Federal, or STF) annulled those
convictions on procedural grounds and ruled that the presiding judge had acted with bias.14 Lula
then narrowly defeated incumbent President Jair Bolsonaro (2019-2022), a right-wing populist
affiliated with the Liberal Party (Partido Liberal, or PL), 50.9% to 49.1%, in an October 2022
presidential runoff election.15

During his third term, Lula has focused on raising Brazilians’ living standards, addressing
environmental concerns, and expanding Brazil’s international influence. He has implemented
portions of his agenda through executive action, such as expanding land rights for Indigenous
groups and Quilombolas (inhabitants of communities founded by individuals who escaped or
were freed from slavery), strengthening environmental conservation efforts, and engaging in
presidential diplomacy. (For further discussion, see “Approach to the Amazon Forest” and
“Brazil’s Foreign Policy.”)

Lula has sought to advance other priorities through the fragmented Brazilian congress, in which
17 political parties or party federations have representation.16 Although legislators aligned with
Lula held approximately 27% of seats in the Chamber of Deputies and 19% of seats in the Senate
at the start of Brazil’s 57th legislature (2023-2027), Lula forged working majorities with the
conditional support of a bloc of centrist and center-right parties that typically work with whatever
administration is in power to increase the parties’ influence over government policy and
staffing.17 Among other Lula administration proposals, the Brazilian congress has approved
minimum wage increases, an expansion of a conditional cash transfer program for low-income
Brazilians, a progressive income tax reform, and a labor reform to reduce Brazil’s work week
from six days to five days (and from 44 to 40 hours).

Lula’s working legislative majorities have not always held. In April 2026, for example, the
Brazilian Senate rejected Lula’s nominee for a vacant seat on the STF—reportedly the first such
rejection since 1894.18 The Brazilian congress also has overridden some presidential vetoes. In
April 2026, for example, the congress overrode Lula’s veto to enact legislation intended to reduce
prison sentences for some individuals convicted of crimes related to January 8, 2023, riots, in
which Bolsonaro supporters stormed Brazil’s congress, supreme court, and presidential palace

Socioeconomic Conditions

During his 2022 campaign, President Lula pledged to restore the upward social mobility that
characterized his first two terms in office, when Brazil’s gross domestic product (GDP) expanded
by an average of 4.1% per year, driven in part by a surge in international demand (particularly
from the People’s Republic of China [PRC, or China]) for Brazilian commodities such as oil,
iron, and soybeans. Brazil’s GDP growth, which averaged less than 1.0% between 2011 and 2022,
accelerated to an average of 3.0% between 2023 and 2025.23 This acceleration was driven, in part,
by rising household consumption tied to improving labor market conditions, an expansion of
credit, and government income transfer programs.24 Between the second quarter of 2022 and the
second quarter of 2026, Brazil’s unemployment rate fell from 9.3% to 5.4%, and real average
monthly incomes increased by about 19%.25 Likewise, Brazil’s poverty rate fell from 31.6% in
2022 to 23.1% in 2024; the poverty rates of Black and mixed race Brazilians remained higher
than the national average, at 25.8% and 29.8%, respectively.26 Despite these socioeconomic
improvements, a majority of Brazilians surveyed in September 2026 reported that their household
incomes either had not risen or had not kept up with the cost of living over the past year.27
The Economist Intelligence Unit projects 1.8% GDP growth for Brazil in 2026.28 This slowdown
is due, in part, to the U.S.-Israel conflict with Iran, which has contributed to higher inflation and
interest rates, dampening domestic consumption. Consumer prices in Brazil increased by 4.6%
between October 2025 and September 2026, with gasoline and diesel prices increasing by 5.6%

Approach to the Amazon Forest

About 62% of the greater Amazon region, encompassing the Amazon Forest and the Amazon
Basin, is located within Brazil.32 Deforestation in the Brazilian Amazon,33 which fell by nearly
77% over the course of Lula’s first two terms, began rising in 2012 and reached a 15-year high of
5,034 square miles in 2021 under the Bolsonaro administration (see Figure 2). Some analysts
linked the 2012-2021 increase in deforestation to a series of government policy reversals that cut
federal funding for environmental enforcement, reduced the size of protected areas, and relaxed
conservation requirements.34 Market incentives, such as international prices for beef, soybeans,
and gold, among other commodities, also appear to have contributed to deforestation trends.35
Some scientists have warned that the Amazon forest may be nearing a tipping point at which the
forest, no longer able to sustain itself, could transition to a drier, savanna-like ecosystem.36 This
cycle of deforestation and drought could reduce the forest’s capacity to absorb and sequester
carbon, as well as reduce the precipitation that fuels forest regeneration and growth and Brazil’s
agricultural and hydropower production.37

the Brazilian Amazon, which encompasses 58.9% of Brazil’s territory and is home to 26.7 million
inhabitants.39 Among other measures, the Lula administration has increased environmental

Brazil’s Foreign Policy

Although the short-term areas of emphasis of Brazilian foreign policy have varied since Brazil’s
return to democracy, successive Brazilian administrations generally have sought to increase the
country’s influence on global affairs while maintaining Brazil’s autonomy.54 While pursuing these
objectives, Brazilian officials have emphasized the principles of multilateralism, peaceful dispute
settlement, and nonintervention in the affairs of other countries.55 In practice, this approach has
involved the pursuit of cooperative relations with international partners of varying ideologies and
systems of government, including the United States, the European Union (EU), Brazil’s South
American neighbors, and fellow members of the BRICS group (originally named for Brazil,
Russia, India, China, and South Africa).56

Since returning to office in 2023, President Lula has sought to reassert Brazil’s influence abroad
after two Brazilian administrations that arguably were more focused on domestic challenges. He
has placed particular emphasis on presidential diplomacy, convening and hosting various
gatherings of world leaders. These gatherings have included a summit of South American leaders
(May 2023), a meeting of Amazon Basin countries (August 2023), a Group of 20 (G-20) summit
(November 2024), a BRICS summit (July 2025), and the 30th session of the Conference of the
Parties to the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (November 2025).

Security Cooperation

Although U.S.-Brazilian security cooperation has been limited at times due to political disputes
and policy differences, bilateral military and law enforcement ties grew closer between 2015 and
2024. During that period, the U.S. and Brazilian governments held regular high-level security
discussions, the countries’ armed forces participated in joint training and exercises, and U.S. and
Brazilian law enforcement agencies cooperated on counternarcotics efforts, among other issues.
The United States also provided some military and law enforcement aid to Brazilian security
forces intended to strengthen their capacities and foster interoperability and collaboration. Some
security cooperation appears to have continued during the second Trump Administration, despite
differing approaches to some security challenges and tensions in the broader bilateral
relationship.

Defense

The United States and Brazil are both parties to the Inter-American Treaty of Reciprocal
Assistance, a collective security pact signed in 1947, but bilateral defense cooperation has varied
over time.177 During the 1970s, for example, Brazil withdrew from a series of military agreements
with the United States in response to U.S. criticism on human rights issues and U.S. opposition to
a Brazilian nuclear agreement with West Germany. The countries did not conclude a new
umbrella Defense Cooperation Agreement until 2010. That same year, Brazil and the United
States signed a General Security of Military Information Agreement intended to facilitate the
sharing of classified information. The Brazilian congress did not approve either of those
agreements until 2015, due in part to a cooling of relations after press reports revealed that the
U.S. National Security Agency had engaged in extensive electronic surveillance in Brazil.178
These defense agreements, negotiated as executive agreements, did not require U.S.
congressional approval.179

As noted previously, in 2019, President Trump designated Brazil as a major non-NATO ally for
the purposes of the Foreign Assistance Act of 1961, as amended (22 U.S.C. §§2151 et seq.), and
the Arms Export Control Act (22 U.S.C. §§2751 et seq.).180 That designation made Brazil eligible
for privileged access to the U.S. defense industry and increased joint military exchanges,
exercises, and training, among other benefits. According to the U.S. Embassy in Brazil, a 2020
Research, Development, Test, and Evaluation Agreement enabled bilateral collaboration on basic,
exploratory, and advanced technologies at a “level enjoyed only by NATO allies and the closest
strategic security partners of the United States.”181
