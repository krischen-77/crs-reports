Credit card debt may be a rational response to smoothing consumption or behavioral biases in
favor of present consumption or larger self-control issues. Consumers may be utilizing credit
card debt to adjust to worsening affordability conditions. Diagnosis of different causes of credit
card debt may result in differential policy responses.

Credit card debt has increased since the end of the COVID-19 pandemic. Aggregate credit card debt, a stock measure, has
increased in nominal terms from $770 billion in the first quarter of 2021 to $1.28 trillion in the third quarter of 2026.
Aggregate debt trends look different when adjusted for inflation or adjusted for population, with increases appearing less
dramatic. Such numbers are still at overall population-adjusted highs or nearing inflation-adjusted highs from the Great
Recession (2007-2009). This remained true even after credit card debt largely stabilized and even decreased during the
pandemic because of additional economic support, loan forbearance, and decreased consumption, among other potential
explanations. There is evidence that consumers might not be able to sustain these higher debt levels and may be unable to pay
their current debts. Transition into 30 or more days of delinquency has increased from 4.6% to 8.4% over the same time
period, with transitions into serious delinquency showing similar trends over time.

Congress has previously addressed the credit card market in the Truth in Lending Act (TILA; first enacted in 1968 as part of
the Consumer Credit Protection Act, P.L. 90-321). TILA mandated a disclosure regime for certain types of consumer credit,
requiring disclosures at origination and periodically thereafter. TILA was further modified in 2009 by the CARD Act (P.L.
111-24), which imposed additional pricing and disclosure requirements to further constrain certain potentially troublesome
behaviors by credit card issuers. Other statutory features include rules around the collection and credit reporting of credit card
debt, among other forms of consumer debt. Certain policymakers have raised concerns about the level of credit card debt and
the design of such products.

In response, policymakers have proposed additional price regulation in the form of interest rate caps or late fee caps, as
previously finalized (and later challenged and withdrawn by the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau). Analyzing the
relative effects of such programs on credit and consumer welfare remains an important policy issue. Many of the borrowers
who have high levels of credit card debt appear to have lower levels of financial literacy. Thus, Congress may consider
certain policy responses to assist such borrowers, either tasking financial regulators with additional responsibility in such an
area or revisiting disclosure regimes intended to reduce the information asymmetry between borrowers and lenders. Finally,
certain provisions of the CARD Act imposed new disclosure regimes aimed at increasing repayment on credit cards and
targeted disclosures for borrowers paying at the minimum level. Credit card issuers may be setting minimum payments at a
low level to extend the repayment period, increase the amount of interest paid, and anchor borrowers at that minimum
payment level. Congress could revisit such minimum payment formulas, which are currently primarily constrained by
guidance issued by bank regulators in 2003 to prevent negative amortization by certain banks.

Introduction
A credit card provides a consumer with unsecured revolving credit.1 Consumers have a number of
options to smooth consumption and to facilitate payments, but unsecured credit cards are the most
common and the focus of this report.2 Consumers often value credit cards because they are a
convenient payment option, particularly relative to cash or check. Individuals may earn rewards
associated with their credit card transactions but might also pay fees and interest, depending on
the payment structure and type. Credit cards may present certain risks for consumers with certain
behavioral biases, limited savings, or lower levels of financial literacy. Since the first quarter of
2022, overall credit card balances and transitions into certain stages of delinquency have
precipitously increased. The aggregate increases in balances are lower when adjusted for the
increases in inflation or population, but they are still notable. At the same time, the average
annual percentage rate (APR) on credit cards has increased (along with interest rates on other
forms of credit), making repayment of existing balances more difficult.

These increases in balances and delinquency have led some Members of Congress to raise
concerns about the trends in credit card debt and whether certain policy responses, such as credit
card interest rate caps, should be taken to confront this potential policy issue. Hearings pertinent
to this topic have been held in recent Congresses.3 In the 119th Congress, various bills (such as
those discussed below) would cap credit card APRs at 10% until January 2031 (S. 381/H.R. 1944)
or cap fee and interest rates or APRs for most forms of consumer credit at 36% (S. 2781, S.
3793). Other bills would cap credit card late fees (S. 3660) or enable states to set their own
interest rates for consumers who live in their states (S. 3721).

The first half of this report introduces the credit card market and how it relates to debt. It also
provides an overview of the pertinent statutory framework and congressional oversight of
regulators. It then describes characteristics of credit card users, the causes of credit card debts,
and aggregate trends in credit card debt. The latter half of the report analyzes certain policy issues
discussed by Congress and the effects of different policy proposals. Such policy issues include

Congressional Context

With the rise in use of credit cards, Congress has implemented legislation to impact one aspect or
another of the marketplace. The federal government imposed pricing disclosures for credit cards,
building on fractured state mandates for certain products. Other, more general legislation
regulating debt collection and credit reporting has also applied to credit cards. In the run-up to
and during the Great Recession (2007-2009), congressional concerns about hidden and complex
credit card fees and various potentially exploitative practices led to further action to constrain
card issuers.

Statutory Background

Truth in Lending Act (TILA)
TILA was first enacted in 1968 as part of the Consumer Credit Protection Act (P.L. 90-321).4 In
general, the law requires creditors to disclose terms and costs of consumer credit. It has been
amended multiple times to revise these disclosures and provide additional consumer protections
outside of the primarily disclosure-based regime as originally passed. Most prominently, TILA
was modified around the time of the global financial crisis in 2007-2009, namely by the Credit
Card Accountability Responsibility and Disclosure (CARD) Act of 2009 (P.L. 111-24), which,
among other things, imposed new restrictions on credit cards and those provisions discussed in
greater detail below.

TILA requires lenders to provide a number of different disclosures to borrowers, including
disclosures at origination, periodic statements, and application disclosures for some products. The
information that card issuers are required to provide includes finance charges, APR, payment
information, fees, and the costs associated with the product. Credit card issuers require consumers
to complete an application or renewal prior to issuance and thus cannot be issued on an
unsolicited basis. TILA provides for enforcement actions against financial institutions that violate
such provisions.

Credit Card Accountability Responsibility and Disclosure (CARD) Act of 2009
The CARD Act was enacted in 2009, revising TILA and other statutes.5 There were a number of
impactful provisions pertinent to credit card debt.6 As originally passed, the CARD Act was
implemented by the Federal Reserve; however, responsibilities were moved to the Consumer
Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) under the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer
Protection Act (Dodd-Frank, P.L. 111-203). Certain provisions are discussed by title below.

Title I required issuers to take the following actions:
    •   Provide written notice 45 days prior to the effective date of an increase in APR or
        other significant change, accompanied by a written notice that customers may
        cancel before the effective date. Such changes could apply only to outstanding
        balances under certain conditions.
    •  Review accounts with increased APRs every six months to determine whether
       market conditions warrant a decreased APR.
    •   Consider consumers’ ability to pay required payments under the terms of the
        account.
    •   Provide periodic statements for payments to be mailed 21 days before due dates.
    •  Apply any payment to a credit card issuer, in addition to minimum payments, to
        the highest interest rate card and then to each lower interest rate card.
    •   Require that the total fees paid (excluding late fees, over-the-limit fees, or
         insufficient funds fees) during the first year a card was in use not be more than
     25% of the credit limit on a credit card.
    •  Charge penalty fees that are “reasonable and proportional” to a violation of a card
       agreement, such as a late fee or an over-the-limit fee.

Title I also prescribed certain enhanced civil penalties for card issuers for violations of TILA.

Title II revised the current disclosure requirements for credit cards. Among the changes were
requirements for a new disclosure outlining payoff timing associated with minimum payments,
for disclosure of changed information about credit card renewals, and for periodic disclosures
associated with late payments.

Title III provided new protections for younger consumers, including requirements for cosigner
(parent/guardian) and primary borrower approval for credit limit increases in jointly held
accounts, certain protections on credit card marketing toward students, and applications under
TILA for consumers under 21 to be signed by a cosigner who may repay debts incurred by the
younger consumer if that younger consumer cannot show means of repayment.

Title V required certain reports to Congress by the Government Accountability Office (GAO), the
biennial consumer credit card report (now issued by the CFPB), and a joint study between
multiple departments on financial literacy.

Other Relevant Laws

The Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA, 15 U.S.C. §§1681 et seq.) established consumers’ rights in
credit reports and scores and requires certain disclosures. The FCRA defines the types of
information that can be included in credit reports and allows consumers to ask for their credit
scores and obtain all of the information in their credit reports, which ultimately create credit
scores. This statute impacts both how credit card debt is reported to credit bureaus and how such
bureaus compile pertinent credit reporting data to generate scores and furnish them back to credit
card companies. Both items play a role in the overall credit card underwriting process.

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA, 15 U.S.C. §§1692 et seq.) regulated collection
agencies’ and debt collectors’ communications with consumers, disclosure requirements, and
consumers’ rights in debt collection. The FDCPA aimed to eliminate abusive practices by debt
collectors and provided consumers with rights in debt collection for credit cards and other
consumer debts. The CFPB shares enforcement powers over the FDCPA with the Federal Trade
Commission.

Oversight of Regulators

Congress plays a role in oversight that can broadly translate to “review, monitoring, and
supervision of the executive and the implementation of public policy.”7 Pertinent to the credit
card space, oversight may include ensuring proper implementation of previously discussed
statutes, whether through the regulators or with the firms in the credit card industry. Certain
agencies have jurisdiction over the credit card market and specifically on credit card debt for
which Congress could exert their oversight. For example, the CFPB implements a number of
pertinent laws within this policy area, including TILA, the FCRA, and the FDCPA. Banking
regulators may take enforcement actions against financial institutions within their jurisdiction for
consumer compliance or consider credit card policies for safety and soundness risk.8 Credit card
debt is among the focus areas that Congress may question the CFPB director about during their
biannual testimony in front of its committees of jurisdiction, the Senate Committee on Banking,
Housing, and Urban Affairs and House Committee on Financial Services. Committees may
pursue other independent oversight actions related to credit card debt issues, for example, by
holding hearings pertinent to the topic, including certain related hearings.9 Congress confirms
appointed heads of agencies that supervise credit card issuers and may consider their policies on
credit card debt in consideration for appointments to their positions.

Members of Congress may also request that the GAO conduct independent analysis and oversight
over specific policy issues, either formally through mandates in legislation or informally through
requests by Members.10 Such oversight sometimes occurs through traditional committee
structures. An example of a statutorily mandated GAO report pertinent to credit card debt is
Credit Cards: Consumer Costs for Debt Protection Products Can Be Substantial Relative to
Benefits but Are Not a Focus of Regulatory Oversight, written in response to a provision in
Section 509 of the CARD Act.11 In contrast, an example of a relevant non-statutorily mandated
GAO report on credit card debt is Credit Cards: Pandemic Assistance Likely Helped Reduce
Balances, and Credit Terms Varied Among Demographic Groups, requested by Senators Sheldon
Whitehouse and Bernie Sanders.12

The statutory framework for credit cards specific to debt issues has been addressed incrementally
as key issue areas have arisen. TILA was meant to enable consumers to better understand the
terms associated with credit (and specifically credit cards) and to comparison shop, while the
CARD Act was meant to stem certain purported abuses and add additional disclosure elements.
Congress may wish to reconsider the structure of this statutory framework or undertake additional
oversight activities if Members are concerned with the degree of credit card debt that consumers
have in the marketplaces. Certain existing proposals contain requirements around capping credit
card costs in the form of interest rates or late fees, but Congress may wish to evaluate revising
disclosure requirements or the structure of credit card payments.

Overview of the Credit Card Market

Credit cards are ubiquitous in the United States. As of 2024, 81% of adult Americans had credit
cards, and this share has declined slightly over time from a peak of 84% in 2021.13 According to a
CFPB report, at the end of 2024, there were 793 million credit card accounts, translating to
roughly three credit card accounts per adult, on average.14

Credit cards are valuable to consumers, in part, because they are flexible. Both the amount
borrowed and the amount paid can vary each month according to the consumer’s needs. For
example, if a consumer experiences financial shock, such as unemployment or an unexpected
home repair, they can use credit cards to borrow money quickly and easily. A consumer can then
pay back the debt when they are able to do so. Credit cards can also be used to smooth
consumption over time, which may be particularly valuable to households with tight budgets.

Card Issuers

Relative to other forms of consumer financial services such as auto loans or mortgages, credit
card issuance is fairly concentrated among financial institutions. The top 10 issuers by average
credit card dollars outstanding represented 83% of all credit cards in 2022, which was a decline
from 87% in 2016.23 Such analysis was not updated as of the most recent consumer card report
issued by the CFPB in 2025. The largest issuers overall are large national banks that specialize in
consumer payments and loans.

Banks generate card revenue primarily through cardholder interest, late and annual fees, and
interchange fees, while their expenses include marketing costs, fraud prevention, rewards
programs, and operational expenses.

Interest and Fees Revenue

According to the CFPB, in 2024, Americans paid roughly $190 billion in interest and fees. Most
of this amount ($160 billion) went toward interest and represented the primary consumer expense
associated with credit cards.24 The total cost of credit as a percentage of cycle-ending balances
was roughly 20%, the highest in the CFPB-recorded data since 2015.25 As of 2024, the average
APR inclusive of interest rate and certain fees associated with general-purpose credit cards was
25%, among the highest levels since 2015.26

Interchange Revenue
Interchange fees are a critical revenue stream for credit card issuers.27 These are fees paid by
merchants to banks whenever a customer uses a credit card for a purchase and have been the
subject of intense policy discussion. In 2023, interchange revenue was one of the largest income
sources for credit card issuers, totaling around $72 billion.28 Such numbers may not be
comparable to the interest and fees calculation above, as they come from a separate source.
Interchange revenue particularly drives the overall composition of revenue for transactor
borrowers, who generally pay their balance in full each month and so pay little if any interest, as
discussed in more detail below in the “Revolvers and Transactors” section.

Rewards Expenses

Credit card issuers spend billions of dollars annually on rewards programs, which are a way
banks attract and retain customers. These rewards can include cashback, travel points, and
merchandise. According to analysis by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, the rewards
expenses for the six largest card issuers were roughly $68 billion in 2023.29 Rewards expenses
have increased over time.30 Such expenses are often closely tied to the total amount of
interchange that a firm charges. Rewards programs can sometimes cost more than revenue earned
from interchange fees; for example, Citi reported $11 billion in expenses associated with rewards
programs and partner programs and around $9 billion in interchange revenue in 2023.31 The level
of rewards varies substantially by card and over time. Such variations may depend on the degree
of customer acquisition a firm is interested in for a particular product.

Other Expenses

Other expenses may include operating costs, such as marketing, customer service functions, and
other salaries to review applications or assess risk. Research from the Federal Reserve Bank of
New York estimated that such operational expenses were 4.9% of assets.32 Banks also incur
interest expenses associated with the cost of obtaining funds through deposits or other sources.
Fraud is a concern for credit card issuers, as fraud on their platforms can harm overall confidence
in their product, resulting in up-front investment. In addition, card issuers are liable for certain
unauthorized card payments on their platform.

Credit Card Debt

While credit cards may be a convenient payment method, they present certain risks if consumers
do not pay off their debt each month and revolve a certain amount of balance with interest.
Certain policymakers have raised concerns about the level of credit card debt present in the
United States and the effects of the debt on consumers.

Revolvers and Transactors

Within the credit card landscape, consumers can often be divided into two broad categories based
on their repayment behavior: revolvers and transactors. Revolvers are consumers or accounts that
do not pay their balance in full by their due date and as a result have a remaining balance
revolving into the subsequent statement period with interest and possibly other fees, depending on
payoff behavior. Transactors are consumers or accounts that pay their balance in full by their due
date and utilize the credit card primarily to transact without paying any interest or fees associated
with the card. This distinction is important in public policy, as revolvers bear the potential

negative consequences of having a credit card, such as additional fees and interest rates and
eventually higher delinquency, default, and charge-off rates. From a credit card company’s
perspective and related to either transaction revenues or fee/interest revenue, “the majority of
credit card revenues are paid by revolvers.”34

Over time, as tracked quarterly by the CFPB, roughly half of general-purpose active accounts
revolve.35 Over a year, this share would be higher, as certain accounts go back and forth between
revolving and transacting behavior, sometimes known as transitioners, while many remain in one
group or the other.36

Policymakers may be particularly interested in understanding the dynamics of revolvers within
the credit ecosystem and identifying ways to decrease their aggregate amount of credit card debt.
Credit card debt can crowd out current consumption, can crowd out future investment in more
durable items such as further education or a home, and may have adverse effects on medical and
physical health.37 The effects of credit card debt on consumer financial health can originate from
multiple different mechanisms. These include the reporting of higher utilization or missed
payments on credit scores and as overall debt and the payments affect the ability for a consumer
to otherwise consume, save, or invest in other areas. The effects on consumers depend on the
principal borrowed, income of the consumer, APR, and the payment leading to the degree to
which the principal will amortize. Because card APRs are much higher than certain other forms of
consumer lending, such as cars or mortgages, loans of the same principal can result in a much
higher payment of interest, relative to such products.

Causes of Credit Card Debt

Economists make certain assumptions that, under a rational model, consumers make optimal
choices for maximizing their utility, alternately defined as welfare or well-being.38 This suggests
that policy changes generally would leave consumers worse off by intervening with those optimal
consumption choices. In a rational model of consumption, individuals generally would borrow
from credit cards to smooth consumption or otherwise invest in durable goods. Credit cards may
offer advantages relative to the transaction costs associated with potentially inflexible installment
loans with designated timelines and fixed amounts. Additionally, certain consumers may prefer to
pay interest as opposed to holding a higher emergency fund that could also guard against
shocks.39 Certain evidence has pointed to expense shocks as a driver of credit card debt.40
Consumers may prefer making an investment in a new durable good, such as a part for a major
car repair, and place it on their credit card with the expectation they will pay it off with interest,

Affordability

The price of goods and services underpins consumer finance. If the prices of goods and services
rise faster than incomes, consumers may need to do something to adjust to those increases. One
common response is that consumers may rely on credit, including credit cards, to smooth
consumption.49 For example, according to survey evidence, roughly a fourth of U.S. adults used
credit cards, loans, or pawnshops to cope with inflation in October 2023. An even higher
percentage relied on these sources in response to inflation among adults who had recently lost
their jobs or who were lower income.50 However, as seen in Figure 3, some of the increase in
credit card debt can be attributed to inflation, meaning the real (inflation-adjusted) increase in
overall debt levels is much smaller. The increases in total credit card debt since the COVID-19
pandemic were driven by lower-income credit card users, and lower-income credit card users are
more likely to hold such debt.51

Public policy solutions or lack thereof may depend on the diagnosis of the causes of credit card
debt. Consumers may be acting rationally when taking out credit card debt, and if so, public
policy responses are not needed to counteract the overall trends presented in “Overall Trends.” If
a policymaker perceives the issues to be primarily related to self-control or financial literacy,
disclosures may help stem some of the information asymmetries. Policymakers concerned about
the behavioral aspects of credit cards or the effects of affordability on credit card debt may want
to impose more regulatory controls to lessen the aggregate effects of such credit card debt or
decrease the compounding nature of such debt. One challenge for policymakers may be that
certain consumers may be acting in the rational model while others are acting in the behavioral
model. This makes creating the optimal regulatory structure difficult for both types of consumers.

        Why Did Credit Card Debt Decline During the Pandemic?
 During the COVID-19 pandemic, transition into both 30+-day delinquency and serious delinquency (90 days or
  more), depicted in Figure 4, as well as stock measures of total credit card debt, depicted in Figure 3, both
  declined. Declines were fairly widespread across geography, race, and income level but may not have been
  uniform across every group.56 This may have been a surprising result, considering the increasing unemployment
  rates and effects on individuals’ core income levels and downstream impacts in previous analogous business cycles
 on credit card debt. The reasons for the decline in credit card debt are multifactorial and offer insights into credit
  card debt more generally for policymakers.
 Government transfers associated with the pandemic assistance programs likely influenced overall credit card
  balances and debt, as individuals used their funds to pay off existing credit card balances during months where
  such assistance arrived.57 Paydowns associated with the second and third economic stimulus payments appeared
 more substantial than the initial stimulus payment.58 Such data seem to match survey responses from the Census
 Bureau indicating that 14%-16% utilized the initial stimulus payment to pay down debt, while 50%-54% utilized the
  subsequent stimulus payments.59 Survey research from the Federal Reserve Bank of New York found that roughly
  a third of total stimulus payments in aggregate was aimed at paying down general debt.60 It is very likely that
 enhanced unemployment benefits, rental assistance, and other forms of support similarly boosted credit card debt
  repayment.
  Individuals who were not eligible to receive pandemic assistance paid down their credit card balances at rates
  equal to or higher than those who received such assistance.61 This means that pandemic assistance was not the
  only driving factor in the declines in credit card debt; other factors are discussed below.
 The Coronavirus Aid, Relief, and Economic Security Act (CARES Act; P.L. 116-136) specifically establishes
 consumers’ forbearance rights for federally insured mortgages (Section 4022) and federal student loans (Section
  3513). Loan forbearance plans are “agreements that allow borrowers to reduce or suspend payments for a short
  period of time, providing extended time for consumers to become current on their payments and repay the
 amounts owed.”62 The law also protected the credit histories of consumers with forbearance agreements
  associated with credit cards or other forms of consumer debt (Section 4021) but did not provide any affirmative
  requirements for financial institutions to offer such agreements to credit card borrowers.63 Certain federal
  regulators issued statements encouraging financial institutions to work out arrangements with consumers under
  financial stress, including offering forbearance.64
 A much higher than normal number of individuals applied for and received credit card forbearance voluntarily
  provided by credit card issuers, relative to before the pandemic.65 Such programs meant interest generally
  continued to accrue but that there was a $0 monthly minimum payment, meaning a late fee waiver. These
 programs were a month or two at a time, with reenrollment at a lender’s discretion. Roughly 4.1% of card
  balances were enrolled in forbearance in May 2020, declining dramatically until summer 2021 (0.2% in August
  2021), with much of it from individuals ineligible for traditional government pandemic assistance.66 Such
  forbearance programs may have assisted individuals from incurring additional late fees associated with their
  accounts absent a minimum payment and enable additional liquidity for either consumption or other existing debt.
 However, the primary effects were on reducing delinquency and improving credit scores, as opposed to
  decreasing aggregate credit card debt directly.67 Further, the fact that such programs were not mandated by
  statute or formal rulemaking, instead being encouraged by regulators, may have limited the effects. Certain survey
  research indicates that among consumers who had difficulty paying their credit cards during the pandemic, only
 20% received a credit card payment deferment.68
 Research found that forbearance from mortgage programs had the effect of freeing up funds that consumers used
  to pay down credit card debt. These funds would normally be devoted to making their mortgage payment.69
  Individuals did utilize such amounts for consumption as well. Similar effects were not present for the pause in
  student loan debt payments, according to research, as individuals instead increased consumption and often used
  this new liquidity and newly improved credit score to increase their credit card debt.70
 The reasons for this difference are interesting to examine from a practical policy perspective. One reason may be
  the design of the programs, the mortgage forbearance program being temporary (with half of borrowers in
  forbearance for three months or less) while the student loan pause lasted for roughly two and a half years.71 The
  student loan pause largely enrolled all borrowers in federal loans, while one had to opt into federal mortgage
  forbearance through a consumer’s mortgage servicer, where 16% of mortgage borrowers opted into
  forbearance.72 Both the program design and the timeline may have led paused student loan borrowers to
 reexamine their spending patterns absent this student loan payment and take on more debt as a result. In
  contrast, the temporary nature of mortgage forbearance might have made it less likely for individuals to change
 consumption habits dramatically, considering this forbearance was temporary, and leading consumers more likely
  to consider redistributing such funds to credit card debt. There may be differences in borrower profile driving

Price Regulation

Banks generate credit card revenue primarily through cardholder interest, annual fees, and
interchange fees. Some policymakers have debated reducing credit card debt through price
regulation, primarily focused on consumer-paid fees and APRs associated with credit cards.
Broadly, such strategies entail setting (“capping”) a price, which will set the maximum payment
amount or rate that a consumer will pay for an individual good or service.79 Price regulation can
take various forms and may include a permanent hard cap, a floating cap adjusted to inflation or
other price, or cap adjustable at the discretion of government regulators. Depending on a few
different factors, such caps may have certain effects on the level of credit offered by credit card
issuers and on aggregate consumer welfare. The level of the cap is important to the effects of the
aggregate price regulation both on consumers and products in the marketplace. Theory predicts
that lower caps relative to current market price will increasingly impact the supply of cards
relative to an unregulated market. The relative weight of the fee or rate being capped as a share of
the total revenue from the good or service remains important in considering the aggregate impact
of the overall price regulation.

Existing Legislation and Policy Options
A number of congressional proposals would cap credit card interest rates.111 For example, in the
119th Congress, S. 381 and H.R. 1944 would cap credit card APRs at 10% until January 2031. S.
2781 and S. 3793 would cap fees and interest or APRs for most forms of consumer credit at 36%,
in line with the Military Lending Act and certain state laws. S. 3721 would enable states to set
maximum APRs for consumer credit that apply to the state where a consumer resides rather than
the location of the financial institution’s headquarters. President Trump and other Administration
officials have called for a limit on credit card interest rates to 10% for one year.112

Outside of such proposals, Congress could consider a few alternatives. A fixed rate, particularly at
a low level, may particularly constrain credit card companies from originating credit during
periods when the federal funds rate is particularly high and thus the cost of obtaining funding for
financial institutions is high. One policy option would be a floating credit card interest rate cap
pegged to the federal funds rate, which might give financial institutions increased flexibility to
adjust credit cards APRs in high rate environments where they can increase APRs at the same
level. For example, as of October 2026, the effective federal funds rate is 3.9%, so pegging the
maximum rate at 15 percentage points plus the federal funds rate would lead to a rate cap of
18.9%, roughly in line with that currently implemented by the NCUA.

An alternative mechanism to take into account broader market conditions is extending the rate
cap implemented for credit unions to financial institutions writ large. As previously discussed, the
NCUA has the ability to increase the interest rate cap if prevailing interest rate levels threaten the
safety and soundness of credit unions. This provision has led to a consistently elevated credit card
cap relative to the original rate as passed by Congress (15%). Alternatively, proponents of caps
might consider removing the NCUA’s discretion, since it has used it to consistently allow higher
rates than the law’s benchmark. While rate regulation has been among the most popular policy
mechanisms to decrease credit card interest rates, other policy considerations may also offer
alternatives while potentially leading to smaller reductions to aggregate credit access. Some of
these alternatives are discussed later in this report.

Financial Literacy and Disclosures

Financial literacy describes the skills, knowledge, and tools that equip people to make individual
financial decisions and take actions to attain their goals.129 The Financial Literacy and Education
Commission (FLEC) was created by the Fair and Accurate Credit Transactions Act of 2003 (P.L.
108-159) and was subsequently updated in 2010 by Dodd-Frank. The FLEC coordinates the
financial literacy and education activities sponsored by various federal agencies. Some
government agencies have certain statutory mandates for financial literacy-related provisions.130
For example, Dodd-Frank created an Office of Financial Education within the CFPB. Statute
states that the office “shall be responsible for developing and implementing initiatives intended to
educate and empower consumers to make better informed financial decisions.”131

Through financial literacy, consumers may learn more constructive financial habits to properly
budget their income, save up their earnings, and better manage their money. Certain prior
research has suggested that individuals with higher financial literacy and knowledge may lead to
lower levels of higher-cost borrowing and credit card debt.132 For example, certain consumers
have been shown to behave irrationally in holding sufficient low-interest savings to pay off high-
interest credit card debt but do not do so. Individuals within this “credit card debt puzzle” are
more likely to have lower financial literacy, leading to increased cycles of indebtedness, relative
to a rational theory of debt and payments.133

There may be limitations on the relative effects of financial education and literacy on consumer
behavior specifically and the effects on credit card debt. Financial literacy may not be as effective
after a consumer has increased their credit card debt to unsustainable levels and may better serve
as a preventive measure before such behavior begins, as an educational tool to inform consumers
on the usefulness and pitfalls associated with credit. Moreover, financial literacy is not a
substitute for a more intractable short-run income-expense difference that may exist for
particularly low-income consumers in the long run, requiring a change in either side of the
household budget. If consumers are irrational in their use of credit cards, rather than merely
uninformed in terms of literacy or understanding of the product, better financial literacy may not
improve their overall credit card debt situation.

Structure of Credit Card Payments

Credit cards are paid monthly, with payments generally due on the same date each month. This
amount owed is known as a statement balance and can vary month to month as expenses and
payments on existing balance fluctuate. The statement balance consists of the charges from the
given month plus any additional charges such as fees and interest from the previous month plus
any still-owed balance minus any payments already levied. Consumers, particularly transactors,
often pay off statement balances using automatic payments, where a credit card directly debits out
of a checking or savings account. This amount can be the statement balance, the minimum
payment, or another amount specified by a consumer. Such a practice may be less common for
liquidity constrained consumers who are potentially concerned about overdraft or other fees
associated with their checking account or juggling multiple payments from rent, other credit
cards, or other installment loans. One difficulty, and advantage, of credit cards is this variable
payment amount. This makes repayment of the credit card more flexible, but the variable
payment amount may make it more difficult for a consumer to predict the aggregate amount owed
each month without paying attention to each transaction individually and in the aggregate.

Congress could consider certain policy responses. As discussed, banks currently appear to
interpret regulatory guidance as setting a floor for minimum payments at 1% of total balance in
addition to fees and interest to avoid negative amortization. This does not appear to be in
response to any direct congressional action, and Congress could reconsider this numerical amount
or require a new rulemaking from bank regulators to review and reissue this guidance weighing
other priorities, such as incentivizing long-term consumer repayment of credit card debt or
providing certain numerical guidance themselves. Other alternatives may include setting a floor
as a percentage of total balance, inclusive of fees and interest. Another less prescriptive option
would be to direct financial regulators or GAO to research how minimum payments affect
borrowers, including whether they anchor certain borrowers to that amount. Such research could
