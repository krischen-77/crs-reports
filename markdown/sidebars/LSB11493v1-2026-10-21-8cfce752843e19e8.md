The Airline Deregulation Act of 1978:
Preemption of State Law


October 21, 2026

The U.S. Department of Transportation (DOT) has implemented a number of measures during the second
Trump Administration to promote economic deregulation of the airline industry. For example, in
November 2025, DOT withdrew an advance notice of proposed rulemaking on airline passenger rights
initiated during the Biden Administration, which would have required airlines to provide cash
compensation, meals, lodging, and other amenities to airline consumers experiencing significant flight
disruptions. In July 2026, DOT issued a notice of proposed rulemaking to amend the agency’s existing
rule on air fare advertising to provide “greater flexibility for regulated entities in how air fare is
displayed.”

DOT’s recent deregulatory actions have drawn attention to the respective roles of the federal government
and state governments in policing airline business practices. When Congress enacted the Airline
Deregulation Act of 1978 (ADA), it included an express preemption provision “[t]o ensure that the States
would not undo federal deregulation with regulation of their own.” That provision, as amended, preempts
state laws “related to” an airline’s “price, route, or service.” Under Supreme Court precedent, the ADA’s
preemption provision applies broadly and can preempt even generally applicable state laws that indirectly
affect airline prices, routes, or services. This Legal Sidebar provides background on the ADA and its
preemption provision, examines the provision’s scope, and discusses considerations for Congress.

Background

Prior to Congress enacting the ADA in 1978, federal law subjected the airline industry to a “system of
detailed economic regulation” in which airlines “operate[d] like a public utility.” Under the Federal
Aviation Act of 1958, the now-abolished Civil Aeronautics Board “closely regulated” air carrier prices,
routes, and services. In enacting the ADA, Congress sought to “plac[e] maximum reliance on competitive
market forces” and encourage “an air transportation system relying on actual and potential competition …
to provide efficiency, innovation, and low prices.” While the ADA scaled back federal control over
airlines’ business practices, DOT still enforces economic regulations governing the airline industry, and
DOT has statutory authority to prohibit airlines from engaging in unfair or deceptive practices and unfair
methods of competition.

Scope of the ADA’s Preemption Provision

The Supremacy Clause of the U.S. Constitution (Article VI, clause 2) provides the basis for the doctrine
of federal preemption, under which “state laws that ‘interfere with, or are contrary to,’ federal law” are
superseded. Federal law can preempt state law in multiple ways. Federal law may impliedly preempt state
law, but Congress also may enact legislation with language expressly preempting state law, as it did in the
ADA. The ADA’s preemption provision is codified as amended at 49 U.S.C. § 41713(b)(1) and provides
as follows:

    [A] State, political subdivision of a State, or political authority of at least 2 States may not enact
    or enforce a law, regulation, or other provision having the force and effect of law related to a
    price, route, or service of an air carrier that may provide air transportation under this subpart.

The Supreme Court has emphasized that, regardless of the type of preemption at issue, Congress’s intent
is the “ultimate touchstone” of a statute’s preemptive effect. In evaluating Congress’s preemptive intent,
courts look primarily to the statutory text itself.

The Court has interpreted the ADA’s “related to” language as having an “expansive” scope. The Court has
held that state laws are “related to” airline prices, routes, or services where the laws have either a
“reference to” or “connection with” the prices, routes, or services. The ADA’s preemption provision thus
not only preempts state laws directly regulating airline prices, routes, or services, but also can preempt
generally applicable state laws that have only an indirect effect on airlines, including common-law rules
that states have not enacted in a statute.

While the Supreme Court has recognized that the phrase “related to” in the ADA “express[es] a broad pre-
emptive purpose,” the Court has also emphasized limits on the phrase’s preemptive scope when used in
the ADA and other federal statutes. The Court has cautioned against strictly literal interpretations of
“related to” preemption provisions. Rather than reading “related to” language “to the furthest stretch of its
indeterminacy,” the Court has looked to Congress’s statutory objectives as a guide to the scope of
preemption under such provisions. The Court has thus explained that preemption under the ADA “occurs
at least where state laws have a ‘significant impact’ related to Congress’ deregulatory and pre-emption-
related objectives,” and that some state laws may affect airline prices, routes, or services “in too tenuous,
remote, or peripheral a manner” to give rise to preemption.

Determining whether the ADA preempts a particular state law can involve difficult interpretive questions
and require fact-intensive, case-by-case analysis. In addition to questions over the scope of the ADA’s
“related to” language, the preemption provision’s use of the term “services” has posed interpretive
challenges for courts, and federal courts of appeals have split over the meaning of the term. Most federal
courts of appeals that have interpreted the meaning of “service” in the ADA have concluded that the term
encompasses not only the provision of transportation, but also other features or amenities that passengers
bargain for or anticipate as part of their transaction with the airline, such as boarding procedures,
provision of food and drink, and baggage handling. In contrast, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth
Circuit (Ninth Circuit) has adopted a narrower definition of “service.” According to the Ninth Circuit,
“the word ‘service’ in … the ADA’s preemption clause … refer[s] to the prices, schedules, origins and
destinations of the point-to-point transportation of passengers, cargo, or mail … [and] was not intended to
include an airline’s provision of in-flight beverages, personal assistance to passengers, the handling of
luggage, and similar amenities.” The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit (Third Circuit) has
favorably described the Ninth Circuit’s narrower interpretation, but lower courts debate whether the Third
Circuit has adopted the Ninth Circuit’s interpretation.

Application of the ADA’s Preemption Provision to
Different Categories of State Laws

Below are selected examples of how courts have applied the ADA’s preemption provision in certain state
law contexts. The examples include each of the Supreme Court’s decisions to date applying the ADA’s
preemption provision (Morales v. Trans World Airlines, Inc.; American Airlines, Inc. v. Wolens; and
Northwest, Inc. v. Ginsberg), as well as selected decisions of federal courts of appeals. Additionally, the
Supreme Court has provided relevant guidance on the ADA’s preemption provision in cases interpreting a
similarly worded provision governing motor carriers in the Federal Aviation Administration Authorization
Act of 1994 (FAAAA). The examples include one of the Court’s FAAAA preemption decisions.

Consumer Protection Law

The Supreme Court has recognized that the ADA can broadly preempt the enforcement of state consumer
protection laws against airlines. In Morales v. Trans World Airlines, Inc., the Court held that the ADA
preempted states from “prohibiting allegedly deceptive airline fare advertisements through enforcement
of their general consumer protection statutes.” A group of airlines had sued state attorneys general to
block them from enforcing guidelines that purported to “explain in detail how existing state laws apply to
air fare advertising and frequent flyer programs.” In the majority opinion authored by Justice Scalia, the
Court rejected the argument that the ADA only preempts states from “actually prescribing” airline prices,
routes, or services, and imposes “no constraints on laws of general applicability” that do not target the
airline industry. In the Court’s view, the state restrictions on airline fare advertisements had a “forbidden
significant effect upon fares” because the restrictions would significantly limit the airlines’ “ability to
market their product,” and thus, as a matter of economics, would have a “significant impact upon the fares
they charge.”

Three dissenting Justices in Morales objected to the Court’s broad interpretation of the ADA’s preemption
provision. Focusing on the ADA’s legislative history, the dissent concluded that Congress intended to
preempt state laws that relate “directly” to airline prices, routes, or services, but not generally applicable
advertising laws that only “indirectly” relate to airline prices.

The Supreme Court also addressed the ADA’s preemption of a consumer protection law in American
Airlines, Inc. v. Wolens. In Wolens, members of an American Airlines’ frequent-flyer program had sued
American because its retroactive changes to the program’s terms allegedly violated an Illinois consumer
fraud statute and constituted a breach of contract. In a majority opinion authored by Justice Ginsberg, the
Court held that the ADA preempted the consumer fraud claims, but not (as discussed below) the contract
claims. The Court explained that the consumer fraud law did not “simply give effect to bargains offered
by the airlines and accepted by airline customers,” but instead “serve[d] as a means to guide and police
the marketing practices of the airlines.” In the Court’s view, the state consumer fraud law demonstrated
the “potential for intrusive regulation of airline business practices inherent in state consumer protection
legislation.” Justice Stevens dissented with respect to preemption of the consumer fraud claim and argued
that the ADA does not preempt generally applicable laws.

Contract Law

In the Wolens case described above, the Supreme Court also held that the ADA did not preempt the
plaintiffs’ state-law breach of contract claims alleging that the airline violated the terms of its agreement
with passengers. The Court reasoned that the terms and conditions the airline offered to passengers were
“privately ordered obligations ‘and thus d[id] not amount to a State’s “enact[ment] or enforce[ment] [of]
any law, … regulation, … or other provision having the force and effect of law” within the meaning of

[the preemption provision].’” The Court explained that, although “the ADA’s preemption prescription bars
state-imposed regulation of air carriers,” it does not protect airlines from lawsuits “seeking recovery
solely for the airline’s alleged breach of its own, self-imposed undertakings.” In a dissenting opinion
joined by Justice Thomas, Justice O’Connor argued that the ADA preempts state contract law “where the
terms of a private contract relate to airline rates and services, and those terms can only be enforced
through state law.”

The Supreme Court addressed preemption of state contract laws again in Northwest, Inc. v. Ginsberg,
where the Court unanimously held that the ADA preempts state common-law contract claims that seek to
“enlarge the contractual obligations that the parties voluntarily adopt[ed].” The plaintiff’s lawsuit against
Northwest had alleged, among other things, that the airline breached an implied covenant of good faith
and fair dealing under Minnesota contract law by revoking his frequent flyer membership “in a way that
contravened his reasonable expectations.” In support of its conclusion that the implied-covenant claim
sought to enforce a state-imposed obligation, the Court noted that Minnesota law did not permit
contracting parties to agree not to be bound by the implied covenant, and that Minnesota law excluded
certain types of contract claims from the implied covenant for policy reasons.

Employment Law

A number of federal appellate courts have concluded that generally applicable state employment laws are
often too tenuously connected to airline prices, routes, and services to fall within the ADA’s preemption
provision. Examples of state employment laws that some courts have held not preempted under the ADA
include racial discrimination laws, wage laws, meal and rest break laws, and whistleblower laws.

Courts have, however, held in some circumstances that the ADA preempted a generally applicable
employment law. For instance, in DiFiore v. American Airlines, Inc., the U.S. Court of Appeals for the
First Circuit held that the ADA preempted a Massachusetts law regulating tips for service employees. The
tips law prohibited employers from taking fees “that a patron or other consumer would reasonably expect
to be given to a ... service employee ... in lieu of, or in addition to, a tip.” American Airlines charged
passengers a $2 fee for each bag checked with the skycaps at Boston’s Logan Airport, and the skycaps
brought a lawsuit alleging that the fee violated the tips law.

In concluding that the ADA preempted the tips law, the court reasoned that preemption under the ADA
distinguishes between laws that regulate “merely how the airline behaves as an employer or proprietor,”
and laws that regulate “how an airline service is performed and how its price is displayed to customers.”
In the court’s view, because complying with the tips law would require the airline to change the way it
provided curbside check-in service or how it advertised the price for that service, the law did not “simply
regulate the employment relationship between the skycaps and the airline.”

Public Health Law

The FAAAA’s preemption provision for motor carriers of property provides that no state may enact or
enforce laws “related to a price, route, or service of any motor carrier … with respect to the transportation
of property.” In Rowe v. New Hampshire Motor Transport Association, the Supreme Court applied its
ADA preemption precedents in holding that a Maine law regulating the delivery of tobacco products to
customers impermissibly “related to” a motor carrier’s “service” and was therefore preempted under the
FAAAA. The challenged law required, among other things, that tobacco retailers use only delivery
services that provided a specified age-verification service.

In concluding that the FAAAA preempted the state law, the Court rejected Maine’s primary argument that
state public health laws are categorically outside the scope of the preemption provision. The Court
concluded that Maine’s law would have a “‘significant’ and adverse ‘impact’” on the FAAAA’s “pre-

Considerations for Congress

In recent years, some advocacy groups and state officials have proposed amending the ADA’s preemption
provision to give states a larger role in policing airlines, particularly with respect to consumer protection
issues. In 2022, for instance, the National Association of Attorneys General sent a letter to Congress
arguing that DOT does not adequately protect airline consumers and that Congress should enact
legislation authorizing states to enforce state and federal consumer protection laws against airlines.

In contrast, Congress has also considered proposals to amend the ADA’s preemption provision to
explicitly preempt state laws related to additional subjects beyond airline prices, routes, and services. For
instance, a withdrawn amendment to the FAA Reauthorization Act of 2018 would have amended the
preemption provision to explicitly preempt state laws related to “the pay or scheduling of the flight crew
of an air carrier.” An airline industry group advocating for broad preemption under the ADA has argued
that “[p]reemption of patchwork state regulation has helped create a cost-effective and efficient
transportation network throughout the United States.”

Congress may choose to monitor how courts apply the ADA’s preemption provision and evaluate whether
the scope of state laws preempted under the ADA aligns with Congress’s determination of the appropriate
balance of federal and state authority over regulating airline business practices. Congress could amend the
ADA’s preemption provision to narrow, expand, or otherwise clarify its scope.
