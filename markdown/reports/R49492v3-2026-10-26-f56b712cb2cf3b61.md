Some Members have shown sustained interest over several Congresses in amending the nation’s
permitting laws to increase energy production and expedite a variety of projects (e.g.,
transportation infrastructure, energy, data center development, ecological restoration, stormwater infrastructure). Some
Members have introduced numerous permitting-reform-related bills, some of which have focused on specific environmental
statutes with review and/or permitting provisions (e.g., the National Environmental Policy Act, Clean Water Act, Endangered
Species Act, National Historic Preservation Act). Others have focused on specific project types (e.g., electricity transmission
lines, natural gas pipelines, geothermal energy development), and some have included broader reforms to permitting and
reviews across project types.

Areas of debate on previous legislative proposals have included whether reforms should target conventional or renewable
forms of energy, whether amending bedrock environmental laws to shorten reviews may weaken environmental protections,
and whether certain proposals may narrow the role of states in the review process and/or reduce opportunities for public input
and judicial review.

BAAJA is the product of negotiations involving Members of the Senate Committees on Environment and Public Works and
Energy and Natural Resources. In a majority press release announcing the introduction of the bill, the Senate Committee on
Energy and Natural Resources asserted that the legislation “will strengthen American energy security by accelerating the
permitting process for critical energy and mineral projects across the United States.” A majority press release from the Senate
Committee on Environment and Public Works stated that the agreement “will deliver cheaper energy, more jobs, and
responsible growth across the United States.”

This report analyzes the provisions in BAAJA specific to the Clean Water Act (CWA, Division A, Title II). Section 1201
would formally require the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) to issue water quality criteria through notice and
comment rulemaking. It would specify the judicial review provision applicable to issuance of water quality criteria and
regulations defining the scope of “waters of the United States.” Section 1202 would amend CWA Section 401 to narrow the
scope of what certifying authorities (typically states) can consider in their water quality certification reviews and the scope of
conditions they could include when granting certifications generally. It would further narrow the scope of certification
reviews and conditions for certain projects, including interstate transmission line and natural gas pipeline projects. It would
also narrow the bases on which certification may be denied and would amend several aspects of Section 401 certification
timelines, processes, and judicial review. Section 1203 would extend the permit term limit for National Pollutant Discharge
Elimination System (NPDES) permits from 5 years to 10 years. Section 1204 would limit the period of time during which
EPA could issue a Section 404(c) “veto” of a Section 404 permit for the discharge of dredged or fill material into waters of
the United States. It would extend the permit term limits for Section 404 general permits from 5 years to 10 years. It would
specify that the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (USACE) consideration of environmental effects for general permits be
limited to water quality requirements, which is newly defined in the bill, and that any adverse effects of discharges of dredge
or fill material into less than 2 acres of waters of the United States are considered minimal. It would require USACE to
maintain certain nationwide permits and would raise the threshold of allowable impacts for coverage under these permits.
Section 1205 would impose a 150-day statute of limitations for lawsuits challenging Section 404 permits and certain other
actions taken pursuant to Section 404. It would limit the availability of judicial review to parties that commented on an issue
related to the lawsuit during the public comment period. It would limit the ability of courts to vacate permits or agency
actions found to be unlawful and would require courts to set and enforce specific deadlines for agencies to act on remand.

Overview

The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026 (BAAJA; S. 5653) was introduced
on September 30, 2026. The bill as introduced contains seven titles within two divisions, which
aim to reform certain federal environmental review and permitting processes to expedite a wide
range of project types, with an emphasis on energy-related projects.

BAAJA is organized according to two divisions:

    1.  Division A—Committee on Environment and Public Works—has four titles: the
        National Environmental Policy Act of 1969 (NEPA), the Clean Water Act
      (CWA), the Endangered Species Act of 1973 (ESA), and Project Certainty.
    2.  Division B—Committee on Energy and Natural Resources—has three titles:
        Electric Transmission, Energy Permitting, and Historic Preservation.

This report examines the sections in BAAJA specific to the CWA (Division A, Title II):
    •   Section 1201. Water Quality Criteria
    •   Section 1202. Clean Water Act permitting and State certification
    •   Section 1203. Permit term for NPDES permits1
    •   Section 1205. Judicial review timeline clarity

The report provides background on the permitting and review provisions BAAJA amends,
compares each CWA-related provision in BAAJA to existing law and regulations, and provides an
analysis of selected changes in the bill.

Clean Water Act Background

To help achieve the CWA’s objective and goals, the statute prohibits the discharge of pollutants
from any point source (e.g., a discrete conveyance such as a pipe) to waters of the United States
(WOTUS) without a permit. The CWA establishes two permitting programs that may be used to
authorize certain discharges to WOTUS. These are the Section 402 National Pollutant Discharge
Elimination System (NPDES) permitting program and the Section 404 permitting program for
dredged or fill material.2 Separate from, but related to permitting, CWA Section 401 requires a
type of approval, called a water quality certification, for any federal permit or license that may
result in a discharge to WOTUS.3 Division A, Title II, of BAAJA would make changes to these
three programs as well as to the provisions governing judicial review of various actions taken
under the CWA.

NPDES permits are issued by 47 states and the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) for
discharges of pollutants from point sources, such as municipal and industrial sources.4 These
sources include wastewater treatment facilities, municipal stormwater collection systems, and a

Section 404 permits are issued primarily by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (USACE) and two
states (Michigan and New Jersey) for discharges of dredged or fill material.6 Activities that may
require a Section 404 permit include pipeline projects, water resource projects, mining projects,
transmission line projects, or other development projects. Similar to NPDES permits, there are
two main types of Section 404 permits: individual permits and general permits.7 USACE or states
issue individual permits for individual projects or applicants, typically when the project’s
discharge may cause more than minimal individual or cumulative impacts.8 USACE (and
authorized states) may issue general permits for proposed activities that are similar in nature and
will result in only minimal individual and cumulative adverse environmental effects.9 These
permits, authorized under CWA Section 404(e), are issued on a nationwide, regional, or state
basis for particular categories of activities.10 The CWA limits general permit terms to no more
than five years.11

Nationwide permits (NWPs) are general permits that authorize categories of similar activities on a
nationwide basis.12 USACE issues NWPs (along with general conditions and definitions for the
NWPs) through rulemaking, typically every five years. In January 2026, USACE reissued 56
existing NWPs (with some modifications) and issued one new NWP.13 These NWPs went into
effect on March 15, 2026. Examples of activities covered under NWPs, if they meet terms and
conditions of the permit, are oil and natural gas pipeline activities, linear transportation projects,
utility line activities, residential developments, commercial and institutional developments,
agricultural activities, mining activities, and wetland and stream restoration activities.14

The CWA also establishes distinct responsibilities for EPA in implementing Section 404. In
addition, EPA has authority under Section 404(c) to prohibit, deny, restrict, or withdraw the use of
a specified area as a disposal site for dredged or fill material whenever the Administrator
determines that the discharge will have an unacceptable adverse effect on municipal water
supplies, shellfish beds and fishery areas, wildlife, or recreational areas.15

Separate from, but related to permitting, CWA Section 401 requires a type of approval, called a
water quality certification, for any federal permit or license that may result in a discharge to
WOTUS.16 A water quality certification, issued by the state (or other certifying authority)17 in
which the discharge originates, attests that the discharge will comply with applicable provisions
of certain enumerated sections of the CWA (301, 302, 303, 306, and 307) and may include
consideration of other water quality requirements.18 Examples of activities that may require a
CWA Section 401 water quality certification include activities requiring a USACE-issued CWA
Section 404 permit, projects requiring USACE Rivers and Harbors Act permits, hydropower
projects licensed by the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC), and certificates of
public convenience and necessity issued by FERC. A certifying authority may grant, grant with
conditions, deny, or waive certification of proposed federal licenses or permits.19

Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act (BAAJA): Clean Water Act Provisions



Summary of Selected Changes Proposed in BAAJA

As introduced, BAAJA’s CWA-related provisions propose numerous changes to CWA permitting
and water quality certification requirements. Other provisions in the bill, such as Section 1401,
may also affect EPA’s and USACE’s ability to modify or revoke CWA permits. This section
focuses on changes to the Section 401 water quality certification process, NPDES permit term
limits, Section 404 permitting, and judicial review.

Section 401 Water Quality Certification Process (BAAJA Section
1201)

Although CWA Section 401 water quality certifications are not permits, federally permitted or
licensed projects that are subject to the certification requirement may not proceed unless
certification is granted or waived. As a result, the role of water quality certifications in and their
effect on issuance of federal permits and licenses have attracted attention in permitting reform
debates. Some areas of policy interest have included the scope of states’ certification reviews, the
scope of conditions states can impose when granting a certification, and time frames for issuing
certifications.20

Regarding the scope of certification reviews and conditions, one area of debate is whether a
certifying authority’s review and conditions can consider the impacts of the “activity” (i.e., the
impacts from the permitted activity as a whole, including the point source discharge as well as
nonpoint source impacts from the activity) or may only more narrowly consider the impacts from
the point source discharge. Other areas of debate include which water quality requirements may
be considered and which waters may be considered.

Regarding time frames for issuing certifications, Section 401 requires that certifying authorities
act on a certification request within a reasonable period of time (which shall not exceed one year)
after receipt of such request. If a certifying authority does not act on a certification request within
that time frame, the statute provides that the certification requirements are waived, and the
certification is not required for the federal licensing or permitting agency to issue the license or
permit. Areas of debate have included what constitutes a reasonable period of time and who
determines it.

Another area of debate is whether water quality certification should include different procedures
and requirements based on the type of project at issue. BAAJA would specify rules that apply
generally for Section 401 certifications as well as special rules that apply for certain projects—
interstate transmission line projects and interstate natural gas pipeline projects.21

compliance with water quality requirements, which the bill defines to mean (A) the applicable
provisions of Sections 301, 302, 303, 306, and 307; and (B) any federal and state or tribal laws
(including regulations) implementing Sections 301, 302, 303, 306, and 307 that are applicable to

Certification Time Frames

BAAJA would change the certifying authority’s role in determining the reasonable period of
time. Section 401(a)(1) of the CWA provides that if a certifying authority fails or refuses to act on
a request for certification within a reasonable period of time (not to exceed one year), the
certification requirements are deemed waived with respect to the underlying permit or license
application.30 Under current regulations, the federal permitting or licensing agency and the
certifying authority may jointly agree to the reasonable period of time for the certifying authority
to act on a request (provided it does not exceed one year from the date the certification was
received) and establishes a default of six months if they cannot agree.31 BAAJA specifies that the
federal licensing or permitting agency, in consultation with the certifying authority, would
determine the reasonable period of time (not to exceed one year from the date of receipt of the
certification application).32 BAAJA’s special rules for interstate transmission line and natural gas
pipeline projects would also shorten the statutory maximum for the reasonable period of time
from one year to nine months.33

certification actions. BAAJA would specify that the only actions to be taken in response to a
certification application are to grant the application, grant the application with conditions, deny
the application, or waive certification requirements.34

waive” certification requirements at any time during the reasonable period of time. With respect
to certification denials, BAAJA would narrow the bases on which certifications could be denied.

Section 404 Permitting

Section 1204 of BAAJA proposes numerous changes to Section 404 permitting processes and
requirements. Section 1204(a) would narrow the timing within which the EPA Administrator
could exercise 404(c) authority. The CWA currently authorizes the EPA Administrator to exercise
404(c) authority (often referred to as “veto authority”) whenever he determines, after notice and
opportunity for public hearings, that the discharge of such materials into such area will have an
unacceptable adverse effect on municipal water supplies, shellfish beds and fishery areas
(including spawning and breeding areas), wildlife, or recreational areas.36 BAAJA would limit the
time period in which the EPA Administrator may issue a 404(c) determination to the time period
that begins on the date on which an applicant submits a complete application and ends on the date
on which the Secretary (USACE) issues the permit.37

BAAJA Section 1204(b) would also make numerous changes to general permits issued under
Section 404, including changes to nationwide permits. Similar to NPDES permits, BAAJA would
extend the permit term limit for general permits issued under Section 404 from 5 years to 10
years.38 BAAJA also specifies what USACE can consider in determining the environmental
effects of an activity for purposes of issuing general permits, requires that USACE maintain
certain NWPs, and specifies reissuance procedures specific to those NWPs. Collectively, these
changes would result in more projects qualifying for coverage under general permits.

Determination of Environmental Effects

Under existing law, USACE may issue general permits for proposed activities that are similar in
nature and will result in only minimal individual and cumulative adverse environmental effects.39
BAAJA would specify that in determining the environmental effects of an activity, for purposes
of determining whether a general permit can be issued, USACE shall consider only the effects on
compliance with water quality requirements, as defined in the bill (under new Section 401(h)) of
any discharge of dredged or fill material resulting from the activity. The implications of the
limitation to water quality requirements is unclear. While the provision appears to aim to narrow
what is considered for Section 404 general permits, it does not strike the current requirement in
CWA Section 404(e) to evaluate the activities in accordance with the CWA’s Section 404(b)(1)
guidelines, which include broader considerations.40

BAAJA would also specify that USACE is to consider any effects of a discharge of dredged or fill
material into less than 2 acres of WOTUS to be a minimal adverse environmental effect.41 Under
current law and regulations, USACE determines what acreages are considered to be minimal
adverse environmental effects.42 While there is no current acreage limit that applies to all general
permits, acreage limits established under existing NWPs, as discussed in more detail below, range
from ⅒ of an acre to 10 acres, with ½ acre as the most common threshold.43

BAAJA’s provision deeming the effects of a discharge of dredged or fill material into less than 2
acres of WOTUS to be a minimal adverse environmental effect may have additional implications
for USACE’s ability to modify or revoke a general permit, including NWPs. The CWA specifies
that general permits may be revoked or modified by the Secretary of the Army, acting through the
Chief of Engineers (USACE) if, after opportunity for public hearing, the Secretary determines
that the activities authorized by such general permit have an adverse impact on the environment

acre of nontidal WOTUS or ⅓ acre of tidal WOTUS for each single and complete project.

The proposed changes in BAAJA are likely to increase the number of activities that can obtain
coverage under NWPs, as well as under other types of USACE general permits. Given that
issuance time frames for all general permits, including NWPs, are shorter than time frames for
individual permits, this also could result in shorter permitting time frames for more activities.50

Judicial Review

BAAJA’s amendments to the CWA would alter various aspects of judicial review of actions taken
pursuant to the statute. The CWA’s judicial review provision, Section 509(b), applies to seven
enumerated categories of actions and specifies a 120-day statute of limitations and direct review
in the federal appellate courts.51 For actions not covered by that provision, the Administrative
Procedure Act’s (APA’s) judicial review framework governs, providing a six-year statute of
limitations and review in the federal district courts.52 Actions taken by state agencies pursuant to
state law (e.g., state permitting requirements or water quality certification procedures) are
typically subject to state-specific procedures for judicial review.

Section 1201(b) of BAAJA would amend the CWA’s judicial review provision to extend to
development of water quality criteria and regulations defining WOTUS, thus streamlining the
judicial review process for those actions and subjecting them to a shorter statute of limitations.53

Section 1202 of BAAJA would affect various aspects of judicial review of actions related to
water quality certification. The CWA’s judicial review provision does not cover actions taken
under Section 401.54 Under current law, the forum and applicable standards for judicial review of
certification decisions are not uniform across all such decisions. A state’s decision on a
certification application or adherence to state-law certification requirements is generally
reviewable in state court, while questions regarding the state’s adherence to requirements
imposed directly by Section 401 are reviewable in federal court.55 When a state acts as a
certifying authority, state law may govern the burden of proof or standard of review in judicial
challenges to actions on certification applications. The judicial review provisions in Section 1202
of BAAJA would impose more uniform procedures and standards of review for some aspects of
Section 401 litigation.

Specifically, BAAJA would provide a mechanism for certification applicants to seek review of a
certifying authority’s alleged failure to follow the bill’s newly specified procedural requirements.
It would also specify the burden of proof in judicial review of certification denials or certification
conditions.56 BAAJA would provide that in challenges to certification denials, the certifying
authority would bear the burden of demonstrating by clear and convincing evidence that no
modification to or reasonable condition on the certification would make it possible to grant the
certification without violating water quality requirements.57 In challenges to certification
conditions, the certifying authority would bear the burden of demonstrating by clear and
convincing evidence that the challenged conditions are necessary to ensure compliance with
water quality requirements, the least burdensome to ensure compliance with water quality
requirements, and consistent with the requirements of the underlying federal license or permit.58
In addition, BAAJA would provide that waiver of certification requirements is not subject to
judicial review.59
