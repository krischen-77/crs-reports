Summary

The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026 (BAAJA; [S. 5653](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119/s/5653)) was introduced on September 30, 2026. The bill as introduced contains seven titles within two divisions, the first of which—Division A—aims to reform certain federal environmental review and permitting processes (e.g., those contained within the National Environmental Policy Act [NEPA], Clean Water Act, Endangered Species Act) and provide new provisions associated with project certainty and judicial review. Division B contains reforms to the National Historic Preservation Act and has provisions focused on specific project types (e.g., electricity transmission lines, natural gas pipelines, geothermal energy development), including some broader reforms to permitting processes and reviews across project types. This report provides background on the permitting and environmental review processes. It also analyzes BAAJA provisions specific to Division A, Title I, which primarily include proposed amendments to NEPA (Subtitles A [§§1101-1110] and B [§1121]) and provisions broadly related to federal permitting improvements (Subtitle C [§1131-1133]) as compared to existing NEPA requirements.

Section 1101 of BAAJA would specify that NEPA prescribes a procedure to inform the public about environmental effects of federal actions, describes the result of the NEPA process as an informational document, and does not mandate a particular outcome. Section 1102 would reorganize NEPA's definitions, remove two existing definitions, modify several existing definitions, add 15 new definitions, and make conforming amendments. For example, it would expand the list of exclusions in the definition of "major federal action," remove the definition of "participating Federal agencies," and add definitions for terms such as "authorization" and "make public." Section 1103 would require federal agencies to update their agency-specific NEPA procedures and direct the Council on Environmental Quality (CEQ) to promote standardized implementation of NEPA. Section 1104 would require federal agencies to implement NEPA consistent with minimum functional requirements and data and technology standards (as specified in BAAJA Section 1121). Section 1105 would amend the process for determining whether and what level of environmental review applies to a proposed action.

Section 1106 would, among other changes, expand requirements governing the roles and responsibilities of lead, joint lead, and cooperating agencies for federal decisions "relating to an authorization"; add interagency scheduling, coordination, and deadline requirements; eliminate consideration of certain alternatives; specify aspects of CEQ's role in resolving disputes associated with participating agencies and deadlines; place limitations on when an agency is required to consider new scientific or technical research; provide administrative consequences if an agency misses a deadline for an environmental review or related authorization; expand judicial review for missed deadlines; and specify annual reporting requirements for agencies and CEQ. Section 1107 would extend the timeline for reliance on programmatic environmental documents. Section 1108 would require CEQ to maintain a central public record of all categorical exclusions (CEs) and specify the criteria under which agencies could use a listed CE maintained by another agency. Section 1109 would establish application completeness and level of review determination deadlines and require project sponsors to submit specified permitting, stakeholder engagement, and other information in a permitting-related application. Section 1110 would affect the timing, venue, available remedies, and other considerations associated with judicial review of NEPA claims and potentially related claims, including a 150-day statute of limitations to file a claim and a general prohibition on any judicial remedy imposing vacatur or injunctive relief if based on a NEPA violation, among other requirements.

Subtitle B (Section 1121) would direct CEQ to lead development of electronic tools for environmental reviews and authorizations and create an interconnected federal permitting data system with a common and publicly accessible authorization portal. Section 1121 would also increase authorized annual CEQ appropriations from $1 million to $5 million. Subtitle C (Sections 1131-1133) would require CEQ to maintain an online database to provide information on and track the status of environmental reviews and related authorizations for specified environmental documents.

Overview

The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026 (BAAJA; [S. 5653](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119/s/5653)) was introduced on September 30, 2026. The bill as introduced contains seven titles within two divisions, which aim to reform certain federal environmental review and permitting processes to expedite a wide range of project types, with an emphasis on energy-related projects.

BAAJA is organized according to two divisions:

* Division A—Committee on Environment and Public Works—has four titles: the National Environmental Policy Act of 1969 (NEPA), the Clean Water Act (CWA), the Endangered Species Act of 1973 (ESA), and Project Certainty.
* Division B—Committee on Energy and Natural Resources—has three titles: Electric Transmission, Energy Permitting, and Historic Preservation.

This report examines the subtitles and sections in BAAJA specific to NEPA (Division A, Title I):

• Subtitle A—Declaration of Environmental Policy

• Section 1101. Purposes; procedure; savings provision

• Section 1102. Definitions

• Section 1103. Implementing procedures

• Section 1104. Cooperation of agencies; reports; availability of information; recommendations; international and national coordination of efforts

• Section 1105. Procedure for determination of level of review

• Section 1106. Timely and unified Federal reviews

• Section 1107. Programmatic environmental document

• Section 1108. Use of categorical exclusions by other agencies

• Section 1109. Applications for authorizations

• Section 1110. Judicial review

• Subtitle B—Council on Environmental Quality

• Section 1121. Council on Environmental Quality

• Subtitle C—Federal Permitting Improvement

• Section 1131. Definitions

• Section 1132. Environmental Review and Permitting Tracker

• Section 1133. Permitting Improvement Fund

The report provides background on the permitting and review provisions BAAJA would amend and compares each NEPA-related provision in BAAJA to existing NEPA requirements.

Background on Environmental Reviews and Federal Permitting

Certain activities that support the nation's economy and infrastructure, including energy production, transportation, and natural resource development, require one or more federal authorizations.[1](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49510.html#fn1 "The statutory and administrative steps through which federal agencies review proposed projects or activities and issue any required federal authorizations are generally referred to as federal permitting or the federal permitting process . The federal permitting process can involve multiple agencies , laws , and regulations . For a list of c ommon f ederal environmental reviews, authorizations, licenses, permits, approvals, findings, determinations, and other administrative decisions...") Many federal permitting processes (e.g., issuing permits, licensing, or granting rights-of-way across federal lands) require compliance with the National Environmental Policy Act of 1969, as amended (NEPA; 42 U.S.C. §§4321 et seq.). NEPA establishes a national policy with respect to environmental quality and a process for integrating environmental considerations into federal decisionmaking (i.e., through an *environmental review*). Although NEPA often informs federal action—and thus decisions relating to certain authorizations—it does not itself authorize or prohibit projects; rather, it operates alongside the substantive statutes that govern individual federal authorizations.

Congress made substantial amendments to NEPA in the Fiscal Responsibility Act of 2023 (FRA; [P.L. 118-5](https://www.congress.gov/law/118/pub/5)).[2](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49510.html#fn2 "Fiscal Responsibility Act (FRA; P.L. 118-5 ).") Among other changes, the FRA amended NEPA to address circumstances in which the statute applies, the procedure for determining the level of review, page and time limits, and the use of programmatic environmental documents.[3](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49510.html#fn3 "42 U.S.C. §4336e(11) defines a programmatic environmental document as \"an [environmental impact statement] or [environmental assessment] analyzing all or some of the environmental effects of a policy, program, plan, or group of related actions.\"") Congress amended NEPA again in the FY2025 budget reconciliation act ([P.L. 119-21](http://www.congress.gov/law/119/pub/21)) to allow project sponsors to pay a fee for expedited environmental reviews.[4](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49510.html#fn4 "Section 60026 of P.L. 119-21 added Section 112 to the National Environmental Policy Act ( NEPA ) authorizing project sponsors to pay a fee for environmental reviews that cut NEPA's statutory deadlines in half, reducing the deadline of an environmental impact statement (EIS) from two years to one year and an environmental assessment (EA) from one year to 180 days. For additional information on NEPA's levels of review, see CRS In Focus IF12560, National Environmental Policy Act: An Ov...") Division A Title I of BAAJA would affect environmental reviews and certain procedural aspects of federal permitting. NEPA's *environmental reviews* serve as inputs to federal decisionmaking on permitting and other federal activities. Statutory requirements for permitting processes other than environmental reviews—such as deadlines and coordination between agencies—are not found in NEPA but rather in other laws. BAAJA would amend NEPA to connect the environmental review process to additional aspects of federal permitting and authorizations—for example, by setting a unified schedule for the timing of certain decisions.

While NEPA's environmental review requirements apply broadly across the federal government, the specific federal permitting requirements and the agencies involved for any particular project depend on factors such as the nature of the proposed action, applicable jurisdictions, and anticipated environmental impacts. Though beyond the scope of this report, state and local permits (e.g., zoning approvals, land use permits, and state-specific environmental certifications) are often required in addition to the approvals or requirements imposed at the federal level.

Unless Congress exempts an action from environmental review, NEPA requires agencies, prior to making final decisions, to assess reasonably foreseeable environmental effects, identify "irreversible and irretrievable" resource commitments, and consider a reasonable range of alternatives for "*major Federal* *actions* significantly affecting the quality of the human environment."[8](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49510.html#fn8 "42 U.S.C. §4332 (2) (C) (emph a sis added) .") A federal agency's issuance of a permit or other authorization is often considered a major federal actionunder NEPA.[9](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49510.html#fn9 "Section 4336e(10) defines major federal action as an action that an agency \"determines is subject to substantial Federal control and responsibility,\" subject to certain exclusions. For example, NEPA excludes actions with no or minimal federal funding and those with no or minimal federal involvement where the agency cannot control the outcome of the project; see 42 U.S.C. §4336e(10)(B) . Additionally, NEPA does not apply to actions that are exempted by statute or involve nondiscretiona...") During an environmental review, agencies typically consider and analyze social, ecological, and health impacts. NEPA does not require agencies to select the most environmentally protective outcome or any other specific alternative option. Rather, for major federal actions likely to have significant impacts, NEPA requires agencies to conduct thorough analyses before making final decisions.

How a federal agency demonstrates compliance with NEPA depends on the level of the proposed action's impacts. If the proposed action has "a reasonably foreseeable significant effect on the quality of the human environment," an agency prepares an environmental impact statement (EIS).[10](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49510.html#fn10 "42 U.S.C. §4336(b)(1).") If the proposed action "does not have a reasonably foreseeable significant effect on the quality of the human environment, or if the significance of such effect is unknown," an agency typically prepares an environmental assessment (EA).[11](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49510.html#fn11 "42 U.S.C. §4336(b)(2).") An agency is not required to prepare an EIS or EA for a proposed agency action if the action is covered by one of the agency's own categorical exclusions (CEs) or if another agency's CE is applied to the action consistent with 42 U.S.C. §4336c.[12](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49510.html#fn12 "42 U.S.C. §4336(a)(2). Categorical exclusions (CEs) are not exemptions from the NEPA process; rather, they are typically used for actions that an agency does repeatedly and expects will routinely not result in significant impacts. Further, 42 U.S.C. §4336e(1) defines a categorical exclusion as a category of actions that a federal agency has determined normally does not significantly affect the quality of the human environment. Congress also may choose to enact CEs legislatively; see CRS R...")

NEPA establishes a procedural framework for evaluating the environmental impacts of a given project and for informing federal agency decisionmaking; it does not itself confer authority to approve or deny a project or replace the need for specific permits or other authorizations mandated by regulatory statutes. In contrast, federal permitting statutes grant agencies substantive decisionmaking authority and define the criteria under which an authorization may be issued. In practice, agencies often integrate NEPA analyses with permitting requirements.[13](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49510.html#fn13 "42 U.S.C. §§4336a(2)(D) and 4336a(b) specify that lead agencies shall coordinate any environmental review, permit, or authorization required to carry out the proposed agency action and to the extent practicable, all agencies shall evaluate those actions in a single environmental document. This means that compliance requirements of any additional environmental laws (e.g., the Endangered Species Act [ESA]) should be identified (but not necessarily completed) during the NEPA process. If full comp...") NEPA documents may inform permitting decisions; completion of NEPA does not guarantee issuance of a permit, and a permit may be denied after an environmental document is finalized.

In NEPA cases brought under the APA, courts typically require that the agency was on notice of the concern if there was an opportunity to comment.  To have a case heard in court, plaintiffs must meet minimum *standing* requirements including demonstrating an injury in fact that is fairly traceable to the challenged action and that can be remedied by a court (see Lujan v. | [9](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49510.html#ifn9). | Section 4336e(10) defines *major federal action* as an action that an agency "determines is subject to substantial Federal control and responsibility," subject to certain exclusions. For example, NEPA excludes actions with no or minimal federal funding and those with no or minimal federal involvement where the agency cannot control the outcome of the project; see 42 U.S.C. §4336e(10)(B). Additionally, NEPA does not apply to actions that are exempted by statute or involve nondiscretionary agency functions, or where the preparation of an environmental document would conflict with another provision of federal law; see 42 U.S.C. §4336(a). Congress also may designate statutory exemptions for certain agency actions, removing the requirement for environmental review or compliance with NEPA altogether. For example, in Section 316 of [P.L. 93-288](https://www.congress.gov/law/93/pub/288) (42 U.S.C. §5159), Congress exempted certain natural disaster and emergency response actions from NEPA requirements by declaring that such actions were not "major federal actions significantly affecting the quality of the human environment." |

Categorical exclusions (CEs) are not exemptions from the NEPA process; rather, they are typically used for actions that an agency does repeatedly and expects will routinely not result in significant impacts. Further, 42 U.S.C. §4336e(1) defines a *categorical exclusion* as a category of actions that a federal agency has determined normally does not significantly affect the quality of the human environment. Congress also may choose to enact CEs legislatively; see CRS Report R48595, [*Legislative Categorical Exclusions Under the National Environmental Policy Act*](http://www.crs.gov/Reports/R48595). |
| [13](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R49510.html#ifn13). | 42 U.S.C. §§4336a(2)(D) and 4336a(b) specify that lead agencies shall coordinate any environmental review, permit, or authorization required to carry out the proposed agency action and to the extent practicable, all agencies shall evaluate those actions in a single environmental document. This means that compliance requirements of any additional environmental laws (e.g., the Endangered Species Act [ESA]) should be identified (but not necessarily completed) during the NEPA process. If full compliance is not possible by the time the final NEPA document is prepared, the document should reflect consultation with the appropriate agencies and provide reasonable assurance that requirements will be met. |
