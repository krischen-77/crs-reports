Summary

The federal fleet of oceanographic research vessels serves as a primary tool for advancing knowledge of the ocean system. The federal oceanographic fleet is primarily composed of oceanographic research vessels that are owned and operated or leased by federal departments and agencies. Federally funded projects have included the mapping of the seafloor, which aids in the delineation of the outer limits of U.S. maritime boundaries; the identification and study of marine resources for protection, management, or exploitation; ocean exploration; U.S. Arctic and Antarctic research; and U.S. ocean-based research broadly, including the installation, removal, and maintenance of U.S. ocean observation networks. Some of these activities also contribute to various national security objectives. At issue for Congress is understanding the current capacity of the federal oceanographic fleet and its future capability to support ocean research and related national security priorities in a timely and cost-effective manner.

In March 2016, the Interagency Working Group on Facilities and Infrastructure (IWG-FI) published a federal oceanographic research vessel status report entitled Federal Fleet Status Report: Current Capacity and Near-Term Priorities. In this report, the IWG-FI inventoried the oceanographic research vessels that composed the federal fleet at the end of FY2015. The 2016 federal fleet status report also provided an outlook of the fleet's capacity through FY2030 and discussed the potential impacts associated with changes to capacity. The IWG-FI, a non-congressionally mandated working group that is cochaired by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) and the Department of Navy, has not published a federal fleet status report since 2016.

The 2016 federal fleet status report included vessels owned and operated or leased by NOAA, the Department of the Navy, the National Science Foundation, and the U.S. Coast Guard. Since the publication of the 2016 fleet status report, several oceanographic research vessels have retired from the fleet, entered into the fleet, or required maintenance to extend their service lives. The IWG-FI began preparing a new updated federal fleet status report in 2023. In 2024, NOAA acknowledged that an update to the 2016 federal fleet status report was being prepared, but the agency provided no timeline for its publication. To date, the 2016 report remains the most comprehensive publicly available assessment of the federal oceanographic fleet.

Congressional decisionmaking on the federal oceanographic fleet can substantially affect federal agencies' abilities to fulfill their missions (e.g., science and research, education, and navigation safety) and the fleet's ability to address certain national security concerns. Recurring status reports about the federal oceanographic fleet could inform congressional deliberation over and decisions related to the fleet. Congress could pursue several options for mandating reporting on the federal fleet status; in doing so, it could specify the report's contents. For example, Congress could direct the report to inventory the fleet at the time of publication; provide additional details about agencies' future plans, including funding needs to maintain, expand, or reduce the fleet's size to meet evolving congressional priorities; or both. Alternatively, Congress could choose to maintain the status quo and defer to the executive branch on whether, and if so when and how, to update the federal fleet status report.

Given that oceanographic research vessels are a shared federal asset, improved collaboration among federal agencies might allow the government to reduce the number of vessels in the fleet and thus reduce operational costs. Congress has directed federal agencies to increase their use of uncrewed marine systems (UMSs) as a cost-cutting measure. While deploying a UMS may cost less than deploying an oceanographic research vessel, some UMSs would need to be deployed from a vessel.

Congress also may be interested in understanding how the size, capabilities, and utilization of the U.S. oceanographic research fleet compare to the fleets of foreign governments (e.g., the People's Republic of China [PRC] and/or Russia). Some Members of Congress are concerned about the PRC's expanding global deployment of its state-owned fleet of oceanographic research vessels. Several stakeholders have expressed concerns about how data collected by PRC oceanographic research vessels may present national security concerns, as these data have numerous applications, including for military use. Congress could examine whether the current U.S. oceanographic research fleet is capable of collecting these types of data and whether the United States should consider oceanographic research vessels for national security objectives, in addition to research activities. Such an examination may elucidate congressional priorities for the renewal of the federal oceanographic research fleet.

Introduction

The federal fleet of oceanographic research vessels (hereinafter federal oceanographic fleet) serves as a primary tool for advancing knowledge of the global ocean system and the Great Lakes.1 Some federal departments and agencies have referred to the federal oceanographic fleet as "critical national infrastructure," as it supports federal research and operational marine missions (see the text box below).2 For example, data collected by the fleet help address evolving national ocean policy priorities, such as mapping the seabed and identifying marine resources for protection, management, or exploitation.3 In general, the fleet supports federal agency operations, surveys, and research in U.S. ocean waters, the Great Lakes, and the high seas (i.e., international waters).

Congress has defined an oceanographic research vessel in statute as

a vessel that the Secretary [of the department in which the Coast Guard is operating] finds is being employed only in instruction in oceanography or limnology, or both, or only in oceanographic or limnological research, including studies about the sea such as seismic, gravity meter, and magnetic exploration and other marine geophysical or geological surveys, atmospheric research, and biological research.4

Furthermore, Congress determined that oceanographic research vessels are not to be considered passenger vessels, meaning that they carry scientists, not passengers, and are not to be associated with trade or commerce.5 For example, oceanographic research vessels carry science equipment, not cargo, and fishing activities on board are for research purposes, not for commercial sale.

The Interagency Working Group on Facilities and Infrastructure (IWG-FI) has advised the National Science and Technology Council's Subcommittee on Ocean Science and Technology on policies, procedures, and plans relating to oceanographic facility use, upgrades, and investments.6 IWG-FI is cochaired by representatives from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) and the Department of the Navy. The IWG-FI has published inventories of the federal oceanographic fleet and provided information regarding the fleet's aging dynamics.

In 2016, the IWG-FI published its most recent federal fleet status report, Federal Fleet Status Report: Current Capacity and Near-Term Priorities (hereinafter the 2016 federal fleet status report). The report projected a decline in the number of vessels in the federal oceanographic fleet. In the decade since its publication, several oceanographic research vessels have retired from the fleet, entered into the fleet, or required maintenance to extend their service lives (i.e., service-life extensions).7 According to the National Oceanographic Partnership Program, the IWG-FI began preparing a new, updated federal fleet status report in 2023.8 In October 2024, NOAA confirmed that work on an update to the 2016 federal fleet status report was ongoing but provided no timeline for its expected publication.9 As of the date of this CRS report, an updated federal fleet status report has not been published and the 2016 report remains the most comprehensive publicly available assessment of the federal oceanographic fleet.

This CRS report summarizes the 2016 federal fleet status report and discusses the potential value of a recurring report. For example, recurring status reports about the federal oceanographic fleet could serve as a standardized source of information for congressional decisionmaking, including helping policymakers match at-sea capabilities with agency mission requirements, decide whether to maintain or replace current capabilities, and manage the fleet's operational and maintenance costs. The Appendix includes a list of frequently used abbreviations.

Contributions of Oceanographic Research Vessels

The Interagency Working Group on Facilities and Infrastructure, formerly the Interagency Working Group on Facilities, identified several ways in which the federal fleet of oceanographic research vessels has contributed to U.S. ocean policy priorities.

  • Conduct basic research on the ocean, including its physical, chemical, geological, and biological characteristics, processes, and interactions.
  • Support tsunami research and deployment of sensors for disaster warnings.
  • Support biomedical research in the marine realm for new pharmaceuticals and therapies.
  • Provide oceanographic information in support of national defense and homeland security.
  • Assess the status of living marine resources for resource and conservation managers and policymakers.
  • Map, sample, and monitor ocean conditions and features that influence weather and climate.
  • Map, sample, and monitor the ocean floor in geologically active areas to better understand mineral deposition, life in extreme habitats, natural hazards, and plate tectonics.
  • Provide navigational information for the safe and effective movement of commerce and transportation within and between U.S. ports.
  • Study Earth's geologic and climatic history as recorded in corals, ocean ﬂoor sediments, and polar ice.
  • Discover and protect archeological resources.
  • Assess nonliving marine resources and renewable energy for management and development.
  • Enhance ocean-focused educational programs and inspire teachers as well as the public.
  • Deploy new instruments and technology for ocean observations, such as remotely operated vehicles, autonomous underwater vehicles, drifters, buoys, and proﬁling ﬂoats.

Sources: Interagency Working Group on Facilities and Infrastructure, Federal Oceanographic Fleet Status Report, 2013, p. 5; and Interagency Working Group on Facilities, Federal Oceanographic Fleet Status Report, July 2007, p. 5.

Background on Federal Fleet of Oceanographic Research Vessels

The federal oceanographic fleet is composed of vessels that are owned and operated or leased by federal departments and agencies. The fleet is primarily composed of oceanographic research vessels. Other types of vessels that are included in the fleet, such as U.S. Coast Guard (USCG) polar icebreakers, support oceanographic research objectives. Some agency-owned vessels in the federal oceanographic fleet are operated by U.S. universities and laboratories under the University-National Oceanographic Laboratory System (UNOLS); this subset of vessels is collectively known as the U.S. Academic Research Fleet (ARF).10 The ARF also includes vessels owned and operated by U.S. universities and laboratories.11 The ARF is a federal asset of "community-accessible" vessels made available for qualified private investigators and all government agencies that need to conduct oceanographic research to support their missions.12

Vessels in the federal oceanographic fleet are typically greater than 130 feet in length and span three classes of ships.

  • 1. Global Class ships are the largest and most capable ships in the federal oceanographic fleet, as they can work worldwide with large scientific parties and have the longest endurance (i.e., amount of time a vessel can operate at sea).
  • 2. Ocean (Intermediate) Class ships, slightly smaller than Global Class ships, typically work within an ocean basin and have slightly shorter endurance compared with Global Class ships.
  • 3. Regional Class ships, smaller than Ocean Class ships, are optimized for operation in coastal waters, bays, and estuaries.13

Other federal agencies classify their vessels in different ways. For example, NOAA uses NOAA Vessel (N/V) Class A through N/V Class D to classify its fleet based on the vessels' primary missions.14 The Navy classifies its oceanographic research vessels as Auxiliary General Oceanographic Research (AGOR) ships. N/V Class A ships and AGOR ships are equivalent to Global Class ships, as they are capable of oceanographic science and data collection in coastal and deep ocean areas.15

In addition to these classes, oceanographic research vessels can be divided into research vessels and survey vessels. In general, research vessels carry a broad array of scientific instrumentation and have laboratories equipped with analytical equipment and computers that allow for preliminary data analysis and sample storage. Survey vessels generally acquire a wide range of data (including oceanographic, hydrographic, fisheries‐stock assessment, and ecosystem data) in direct support of resource management and monitoring programs. Most survey ships are designed to meet specific mission requirements, such as NOAA's Fisheries Survey Vessels. Vessels in the federal oceanographic fleet are generally capable of providing at-sea experiences for students, teachers, and the public.16

Overview of the 2016 Federal Fleet Status Report

The 2016 federal fleet status report provided an update to the 2013 federal fleet status report.17 To date, the 2016 report provides the most recent comprehensive public assessment of the federal oceanographic fleet.

The IWG-FI provided an inventory of the oceanographic research vessels in the federal fleet at the end of FY2015 in the 2016 federal fleet status report. The IWG-FI included the NOAA research and survey fleet, some USCG polar icebreakers, vessels leased by the National Science Foundation (NSF), and the ARF—which includes vessels owned by NSF and the Department of the Navy's Office of Naval Research (ONR).18 UNOLS provides a forum for the research and education community and the federal government to work cooperatively on oceanographic research, and schedules the research projects to take place on most NSF ships and some Navy, NOAA, and USCG ships in the federal oceanographic fleet.19 While the 2013 federal fleet status report included military Auxiliary General Surveying (AGS) ships as well as vessels owned and operated by the Environmental Protection Agency, the 2016 report excluded them.20

The 2016 federal fleet status report inventoried the capacity of the federal oceanographic fleet at the close of FY2015 and provided information about capacity gaps.21 According to the IWG-FI, capacity refers to the fleet's ability to support federally funded projects in a "timely and cost-effective manner."22 The IWG-FI determines capacity by considering the following aspects of the fleet:

  • total operating budget;
  • total number of ships (or number of ships in a particular class of ships);
  • each ship's design, size, range, and endurance;
  • total number of available science berths;
  • ships' mission configurations; and
  • the federal oceanographic fleet's geographic distribution.

The 2016 federal fleet status report also projected the fleet's future capacity outlook. Specifically, the report identified that at the end of FY2015 the fleet had 35 ships and projected that with congressional appropriations the fleet size would be "22 ships by FY 2030 compared to 18 ships without investment."23 The IWG-FI also analyzed how capacity gaps could impact specific U.S. ocean regions and activities (e.g., research, agency mission support, and education).

The 2016 federal fleet status report presented information about the fleet's oceanographic research and survey activities.24 Between FY2010 and FY2014, NOAA, NSF, ONR, and USCG vessels within the federal oceanographic fleet dedicated more than 80% of their days at sea (or operational days) to resource management and scientific research.25 The remainder of the days at sea contributed to activities related to mission support, such as Antarctic station support (for NSF), ship maintenance (for all four agencies), and education and outreach (for NSF).

Federal Departments and Agencies with Oceanographic Research Vessels

According to the 2016 federal fleet status report, the federal oceanographic fleet is composed of vessels owned and operated or leased by NOAA, NSF, the Department of the Navy, and USCG. Of the 35 vessels listed in the report, 16 were owned by NOAA, 6 were owned by ONR, 5 were owned by NSF, 2 were owned by USCG, and the 6 remaining were owned by nonfederal entities (as part of the ARF or leased by NSF).26

Ships operated by federal departments and agencies are designed to meet specific statutory requirements.27 Some federal departments and agencies may use oceanographic research vessels owned by other federal agencies to fulfill aspects of their missions. For example, the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) has used federal oceanographic fleet ship time to support the calibration of space-based sensors and the validation of space-based data products. Between 2003 and 2013, NASA used approximately six months of ship time per year for its ocean research activities.28

The sections below discuss the four federal agencies that own and operate or lease ships, as identified in the 2016 federal fleet status report, and any changes to the fleet since the end of FY2015. Changes to the federal oceanographic fleet are discussed in the context of agency missions, proposed changes to agency-specific fleets, and selected congressional actions that might impact the current fleet.

National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration

Congress has directed the Secretary of Commerce, acting through the Assistant Administrator of NOAA for Marine and Aviation Operations, to operate and maintain a fleet of vessels that would meet the mission and functions of NOAA, and authorized appropriations for such purposes.29 In this context, NOAA's mission includes safe navigation, weather and climate services, hazard detection and warning, and ocean exploration, as well as surveying living marine resources to inform their protection and management. NOAA owns a variety of vessel types, ranging from large oceanographic research vessels to smaller ships, to accomplish these activities.30

As of FY2015, NOAA owned 16 research vessels. In 2019, NOAA decommissioned Research Vessel (R/V) Hi'ialakai, an Ocean Class ship, due to structural issues, including extensive corrosion, pipe failures, and propulsion problems.31 As of the date of this report, the agency states that it "operates a fleet of 15 research and survey vessels and is in the process of building more."32 The average age of these 15 vessels is older than 30 years. NOAA anticipates that six of the vessels will likely reach the end of their service lives by 2030.

NOAA periodically publishes its own fleet status report, most recently in 2016.33 In 2025, Congress directed NOAA to develop and submit to certain congressional committees a "replacement and modernization plan for the NOAA fleet not later than 180 days after December 18, 2025, and every 2 years thereafter."34 As of the date of this CRS report, congressional committees have not received NOAA's fleet replacement and modernization plan, which is to include

(1) the number of vessels in operation in the NOAA fleet as of the date of submission of the Plan, a description of the status of those vessels, and a statement of the planned and anticipated service life of those vessels;

(2) a plan with respect to operation, maintenance, and replacement of vessels described in paragraph (1), including the schedule for maintenance or replacement and anticipated funding requirements;

(3) the number of vessels proposed to be constructed by NOAA;

(4) the number of vessels proposed to be acquired, leased, or chartered by NOAA;

(5) the number of vessels, or days at sea, that can be obtained by using the vessels of the UNOLS;

(6) the number of vessels that will be made available to NOAA by the Secretary of the Navy, the Director of the National Science Foundation, or any other Federal official, and the terms and conditions for the availability of those vessels;

(7) the proposed acquisition of modern scientific instrumentation for the NOAA fleet, including acoustic systems, data transmission positioning and communication systems, physical, chemical, and meteorological oceanographic systems, and data acquisition and processing systems;

(8) a plan for using small vessels, uncrewed systems, and partnerships to augment the requirements of NOAA for days at sea;

(9) the number of officers of the NOAA commissioned officer corps and professional wage mariners needed to operate and maintain the NOAA fleet, including the vessels identified under paragraph (3); and

(10) current and potential challenges with meeting the requirements under paragraph (9) and proposed solutions to those challenges.35

NOAA provided some information about its current vessel recapitalization and construction plan in its FY2027 budget request. For example, in the request, NOAA stated that its Office of Marine and Aviation Operations is "working to implement" a recommendation made by the Office of Inspector General in 2021 to develop and regularly update a long-range vessel acquisition plan.36 NOAA reported that its "ships are undergoing a generational overhaul, extending the life of existing vessels and infusing them with new technology. At the same time, investments in vessel recapitalization are delivering new ships designed to work in concert with uncrewed maritime systems."37 NOAA anticipates receiving two new charting and mapping vessels in 2027 and 2028.38 NOAA's budget request also stated that it "intends to reduce the number of ship classes in the future. Each class will meet a core mission requirement with secondary missions."39 The agency also plans to standardize core equipment across its fleet, which it stated "will allow NOAA to meet multiple missions and is critical for efficient maintenance, upgrades, and optimal crewing models and to reduce operation and maintenance costs."40

National Science Foundation

NSF provides support for oceanographic research, including through its support of UNOLS and the ARF.41 The federal oceanographic fleet, which includes the ARF, enables data collection and research by academia and the public, in addition to supporting federal research and operational marine missions. NSF owns R/V Sikuliaq, an ARF vessel that has been operated by the University of Alaska Fairbanks since it was built in 2014.42 As of January 2026, NSF owns "three new Regional Class Research Vessels"; the agency expects these vessels to begin operations between 2027 and 2029.43 These three new vessels are to become part of the ARF.44 NSF's Ship Operations Program provides funding for the operation and maintenance of ARF vessels.45

The 2016 federal fleet status report listed NSF as owning four additional oceanographic research vessels, but the agency has since retired these vessels or transferred their ownership to academic institutions.46 According to NSF, budgetary constraints contributed to some transfer-of-ownership decisions. For example, a 2015 National Research Council report recommended that NSF trim its ocean infrastructure in favor of more research support.47 In 2016 NSF stated in response that the operational model for R/V Marcus G. Langseth was "unsustainable"48 and subsequently transferred the ship to Columbia University's Lamont-Doherty Earth Observatory.49 R/V Marcus G. Langseth continues to operate in the ARF.50

NSF has also leased vessels. For example, the 2016 federal fleet status report included two research vessels then leased by NSF—Antarctic Research and Support Vessel Laurence M. Gould and Research Vessel Icebreaker Nathaniel B. Palmer—to support U.S. Antarctic research.51 Due to budgetary constraints, NSF terminated the leases of these two vessels in 2024 and 2025.52 For FY2027, NSF has requested $900 million as new mandatory spending to be used for the construction of an Antarctic icebreaking research vessel.53 To support research vessel operations around the polar regions, among other purposes, some Senators in the 119th Congress introduced legislation that would authorize the NSF's Office of Polar Programs (S. 5165).

U.S. Coast Guard

Among its seven primary duties, Congress has directed the USCG to "engage in oceanographic research of the high seas and in waters subject to the jurisdiction of the United States."54 Congress has further instructed the USCG to "conduct such oceanographic research, use such equipment or instruments, and collect and analyze such oceanographic data, in cooperation with other agencies of the Government, or not, as may be in the national interest."55 USCG polar icebreaker operations fulfill this mandate by supporting NSF research activities in both polar regions.56 For example, the 2016 federal fleet report included both the Coast Guard Cutter (CGC) Polar Star, a heavy polar icebreaker, and CGC Healy, a medium polar icebreaker.57 The Polar Star supports U.S. Antarctic research through Operation Deep Freeze—an annual mission to resupply NSF McMurdo Station, the largest year-round station on Antarctica and the logistical hub for the U.S. Antarctic Program.58 In this context, the Polar Star is not dedicated to scientific research but rather supports Antarctic research vessels by opening paths for them through ice to NSF McMurdo Station. As another example, the Healy spends most of its time in the Arctic supporting NSF research activities and performing other operations.59 The USCG does not consider the Polar Star or the Healy to be oceanographic research vessels, as, under statute, an oceanographic research vessel is to be employed only for oceanographic or limnological research or instructional purposes.60

The future outlook for both Polar Star and Healy remains unclear. The Polar Star is more than 50 years old.61 The Healy suffered a "significant engineering casualty" in July 2026,62 and was reportedly unable to complete its planned 2026 high-latitude summer mission.63

The USCG operates one additional medium polar icebreaker, CGC Storis, which entered into USCG service in 2025.64 The Storis primarily operates in the Arctic, and scientific research is not listed among its multiple missions.65 A House-passed version of a Coast Guard Reauthorization Act of 2025 (H.R. 4275) would require the Commandant of the Coast Guard to enter into a memorandum of agreement with the Under Secretary of Commerce for Oceans and Atmosphere to govern and use the Storis for oceanographic research purposes. It would further direct that the Commandant may enter into agreements with federal, state, and local entities; institutions of higher education; or research institutes to use the Storis for research activities.66 It remains unclear whether Storis would be considered part of the federal oceanographic fleet with or without the enactment of H.R. 4275 or similar legislation.

Congress has provided funding to the USCG for the construction and procurement of new heavy and medium icebreakers, called Polar Security Cutters and Arctic Security Cutters (ASCs), respectively.67 Congress specified that at least one ASC have "scientific research capacity comparable to [CGC] Healy ... for the purposes of hydrographic, bathymetric, oceanographic, weather, atmospheric, climate, fisheries, marine mammals, genetic and other data related to the Arctic, and other research as the Under Secretary [of Commerce for Oceans and Atmosphere] determines appropriate."68 USCG has not indicated whether or how the new vessels may contribute to or support oceanographic research and be considered part of the federal oceanographic fleet.

U.S. Department of the Navy

The Department of the Navy, and its various commands and offices, produces meteorological and oceanographic knowledge to advance the U.S. military mission and provide secondary benefits to the United States. The Navy classifies its auxiliary ships (i.e., naval ships designed to operate in the open ocean in a variety of sea states to provide indirect support to combatant forces or services to shore-based establishments and infrastructure) that are specific to oceanographic surveys or research in the following ways:

AGOR, an Auxiliary General Oceanographic Research ship operated by a non-Navy entity,

T-AGOR, an Auxiliary General Oceanographic Research ship operated by Navy Military Sealift Command (MSC), and

T-AGS, an Auxiliary General Surveying ship operated by MSC.69

As noted earlier, an oceanographic research vessel is defined in statute as only for oceanographic or limnological research or instructional purposes.70 In general, an AGOR ship is an oceanographic research vessel. In contrast, T-AGOR ships do not exclusively engage in oceanographic research or instruction, nor do they possess a USCG oceanographic research vessel determination, as they are operated under Navy authority.

The Navy's Naval Oceanographic Office (NAVOCEANO) collects and analyzes oceanographic data to support national security.71 NAVOCEANO operates a fleet of T-AGS ships to support national security objectives and may operate its vessels in support of other federal agencies' survey missions.72 As previously discussed, while T-AGS ships were included in the 2013 federal fleet status report, they were excluded from the 2016 federal fleet status report. The Navy anticipates the delivery of a new T-AGS ship (T-AGS 67) in December 2026; it is unknown whether T-AGS ships will be included in future federal fleet status reports.73

Within the Navy, ONR aims to provide science- and technology-based solutions for current and future naval challenges. ONR owns six ARF vessels and, together with the operating institutions under UNOLS, coordinates their research missions and ship schedules.74 Because other entities operate these ONR-owned vessels, they are considered AGOR vessels. Six AGOR vessels were included in the 2016 federal fleet status report, three of which are Global Class ships that may need to be replaced in the coming years (Atlantis, Roger Revelle, and Thomas G. Thompson). The Navy's plans for replacing the three AGOR vessels remain unclear.

Under direction from Congress, the Secretary of the Navy submits an annual naval vessel construction plan for combatant and support vessels, as well as auxiliary vessels, that provides an outlook over the next 30 fiscal years (commonly referred to as the Navy 30-year shipbuilding plan).75 This plan excludes AGOR ships.

In 2025, Congress instructed the Navy that none of the funds authorized to be appropriated by the FY2026 National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA) could be used "to retire or decommission, prepare to retire or decommission, or place in storage, any oceanographic research vessel of the Navy unless the Secretary of the Navy has identified and acquired a suitable replacement vessel for conducting the research."76 The Senate-introduced version of an NDAA for FY2027 (S. 4784) would provide similar direction for funds authorized to be appropriated should the bill be enacted.77 S. 4784 also would direct the Secretary of the Navy to submit to certain congressional committees a strategy and report specific to the design and construction of new AGOR ships.78 The strategy is to detail "how the Department of the Navy will complete expedited design and construction of new Global-class auxiliary general oceanographic research (AGOR) ships ... after funding is provided for such new ships, including a cost estimate and an estimated delivery timeline." The report is to include information related to the replacement of certain AGOR ships, including limitations for operating in the Arctic, how the ships would improve the Navy's operation and warfighting capacity in the Arctic, the feasibility of converting current civilian or military operational ships into oceanographic research vessels, and identification of funding for the annual operation and recapitalization of the Navy's oceanographic research fleet.

Including AGOR ships and other Navy auxiliary ships in the annual Navy 30-year shipbuilding plan or in a congressionally mandated recurring federal oceanographic fleet status report would likely have provided context for some of the information requested in S. 4784.

Issues for Congress

Congress has a continuing interest in understanding the current capacity of the federal oceanographic fleet and its future capability to support ocean research and related national security priorities in a timely and cost-effective manner. Previously published federal fleet status reports have provided some of this information. An oceanographic research vessel can cost hundreds of millions of dollars.79 These vessels may be considered generational investments for the federal government as well as the U.S. oceanographic research community.80 Congress may have questions related to the current and future capacity of the federal oceanographic fleet, such as the following:

  • What number of oceanographic research vessels is needed for the fleet to meet congressional priorities, and would this require increased, sustained, or reduced federal investment?
  • How can the federal oceanographic fleet advance U.S. science leadership and help address potential national security concerns in line with congressional priorities?
  • How might the increased deployment of new marine technology, such as uncrewed marine systems (UMSs), affect the number of oceanographic research vessels in the federal fleet?

Mandating a Recurring Federal Oceanographic Research Fleet Status Report

Congress has previously identified the need for a "formal mechanism to coordinate existing partnerships and establish new partnerships for the sharing of resources, intellectual talent, and facilities in the ocean sciences and education, so that optimal use can be made of this most important natural resource for the well-being of all Americans."81 To formalize a recurring federal oceanographic fleet status report, Congress could codify the IWG-FI and mandate that the interagency working group publicly publish or submit to Congress a federal fleet status report with some specified periodicity. Congress also could direct the report to inventory the fleet at the time of publication; provide additional details about agencies' future plans, including funding needs to maintain, expand, or reduce the fleet's size to meet evolving congressional priorities; or both. A recurring status report could be used to provide regular updates on the federal oceanographic fleet, allowing for potential trends in the fleet to be identified. With this information, Congress may choose to address developing trends. Furthermore, Congress may want to better understand why the federal fleet status report that was under preparation in 2023 and 2024 has not been released. Congress also may choose to maintain the status quo of deferring to the executive branch on whether, and if so when and how, to update federal fleet status reports.

Alternatively, Congress could choose to direct a specific federal agency (or combination of agencies) to lead this effort on behalf of federal agencies with an oceanographic and limnological research nexus. Some federal agencies already produce agency-specific shipbuilding plans or fleet status reports. As previously discussed, Congress has directed the Navy to submit a 30-year shipbuilding plan annually and NOAA to submit a replacement and modernization fleet plan every two years. The Navy's 30-year shipbuilding plan omits its auxiliary general ships (including T-AGS, T-AGORS, and AGOR ships). Congress could amend Title 10, Section 231 of the U.S. Code to specify that the Navy's auxiliary ships be included in this plan or pass new legislation directing the Navy to submit a separate plan specific to its oceanographic research vessels.

Given that both agency-specific and government-wide fleet status reports have been released, each approach may present advantages. On one hand, agency-specific fleet reports could be more efficient to produce (e.g., fewer layers of bureaucratic review across agencies). In addition, agencies could provide greater specifics, which may inform the respective oversight and appropriations committees. On the other hand, one government-wide fleet status report could provide a more uniform and holistic view of this shared resource. According to the IWG-FI, the government-wide analysis included in a federal fleet status report "ensures that oceanographic agencies have the information necessary to make informed, coordinated, and cost‐effective Fleet investments that maximize the entire Fleet's ability to support high‐priority research and surveys."82

Right-Sizing the Federal Oceanographic Research Fleet

Congress may also be interested in right-sizing the federal oceanographic research fleet, in regard to costs or national priorities. According to the IWG-FI, right-sizing means identifying the "proper size of the Federal Fleet to meet the Nation's mission requirements."83 In addition to ensuring the fleet covers the geographic ocean regions and ocean research objectives identified by Congress and the executive branch, right-sizing might also help to address rising costs associated with the current fleet or potential national security concerns.

Right-Sizing to Reduce Costs

Previous federal fleet status reports noted that inflation-adjusted costs to operate and maintain oceanographic research vessels have risen over time.84 This increase was partly attributed to older ships in the fleet requiring more frequent and extensive repairs and increasing operational costs (e.g., higher fuel costs and security at dock facilities).85 The IWG-FI has stated that right-sizing the federal oceanographic fleet could be one option to help address rising costs.86 For FY2027, NOAA plans to reduce the number of vessels in its fleet and require standardized core equipment across the fleet to "allow all NOAA vessels to provide multi-mission capabilities."87 Reducing the number of vessels in the federal oceanographic fleet while providing the remaining vessels with multi-mission capabilities may be one approach to reduce operational costs. Congress may consider directing other agencies to do the same.

Improved collaboration and sharing of oceanographic research vessels among certain federal agencies might be another option to reduce operational costs. Some Members of Congress have expressed interest in maximizing federal funding by reducing potentially duplicative federal programs and assets. While individual department and agencies own, and in some cases also operate, oceanographic research vessels, federal agency collaboration on the fleet's use and operation could maximize the effectiveness of limited at-sea resources, according to the IWG-FI.88 For example, according to NOAA, the agency uses UNOLS, Navy, USCG, commercial and international vessels, and hydrographic survey data purchases to cost effectively manage its at-sea data needs.89 As another example, NSF anticipates that operational support for its future Antarctic icebreaking research vessel "will be shared with partner agencies," including ONR, the Bureau of Ocean Energy Management,90 and NOAA.91 Shifting toward greater ship time-sharing between federal agencies, instead of single-agency ownership and operation, might influence how Congress funds federal oceanographic research programs. For example, one potential concern is that Congress may choose to fund one agency at the expense of another. In the absence of information that could be provided by recurring federal fleet status reports, it may be difficult for Congress to evaluate the benefits and drawbacks of single-agency ownership versus greater ship time-sharing.

Right-Sizing to Address National Security Concerns

In contrast to right-sizing to reduce costs, some observers and stakeholders might argue that Congress should increase appropriations for the federal oceanographic fleet to fund service-life extensions of existing vessels and procure new vessels for national security purposes. For example, some Members of Congress have expressed interest in the People's Republic of China's (PRC's, or China's) expanding global deployment of its state-owned fleet of oceanographic research vessels—from waters close to its coast in the Western Pacific and beyond, including the Arctic Ocean.92 Other Members have introduced legislation in the 119th Congress that would require the Secretary of State to submit a report to certain congressional committees about PRC and Russian use of research vessels to surveil or sabotage critical undersea infrastructure (see Section 105 of H.R. 8069/S. 3249).

Under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, scientists and vessels participating in marine scientific research are afforded certain rights, such as the right to conduct marine scientific research in the high seas and in the waters of a coastal nation with that nation's prior consent.93 Some stakeholders contend that the expanded range of PRC research ships' voyages includes areas of potential interest to China's military, such as areas near Guam, a U.S. territory that hosts U.S. military forces.94 The USCG also has "observed heightened activity" of PRC research vessels in U.S. Arctic waters, and in July 2026 stated that it "remains vigilant to ensure [vessel] activities comply with established international law."95 China's expanded research fleet could enhance its exploration for seabed mineral resources or fishing operations, among other purposes.

China's fleet comprises more than 40 oceanographic research vessels, representing the world's largest research vessel fleet.96 China's oceanographic research fleet, according to one observer, has capabilities that are "equivalent" to the U.S. ARF and NOAA's fleet.97 PRC oceanographic research vessels are equipped with sonar for collecting bathymetric data (i.e., data used for seafloor mapping) and are capable of deploying UMSs.98 Some Members of Congress, U.S. naval observers, and other stakeholders speculate that oceanographic data, including seabed mapping data, collected by these vessels for civilian purposes may also be used for military purposes and may present national security concerns for some countries.99 Seafloor mapping activities could enhance China's undersea and seabed warfare capabilities by supporting, among other things,

  • operations by China's submarines and UMSs,
  • operations by China to counter U.S. or other militaries' submarines and UMSs,
  • operations by China to defend its undersea communications cables, and
  • operations by China to tap into or cut others' undersea communications cables.

One observer contends that "despite being unarmed, [PRC oceanographic research] vessels and their crew are capable of carrying out a range of missions that subtract from U.S. power and influence—from eroding America's longstanding advantages in undersea warfare to advancing the PRC's preferred vision of international maritime order."100

In the 119th Congress, the Senate Committee on Foreign Relations advanced legislation that would aim to limit PRC military and oceanographic research vessels from operating in or near U.S. Arctic waters (S. 4708). The bill cites concerns related to the PRC's increased "use of dual-use military-scientific vessels ... while remaining opaque about its intentions." S. 4708 would prohibit the Secretary of State from approving an application for a marine research project in U.S. waters for any foreign vessels owned by or associated with a foreign adversary "that threatens the security of the United States," including the PRC.101 According to one observer, S. 4708 recasts ocean knowledge "as a strategic asset, and its foreign production as a security threat."102

Congress may consider the appropriate level of funding and support for the federal oceanographic fleet given the PRC's current comparative advantage. According to the USCG, "the Coast Guard is America's only surface presence in the Arctic—a growing zone of strategic global competition";103 some U.S. oceanographic research vessels conduct research expeditions in the Arctic.104 Executive Order (EO) 14269 of April 9, 2025, "Restoring America's Maritime Dominance," is intended, in part, to help the United States better compete with the PRC in ocean research. In its FY2027 budget request, NOAA requested an increased funding level to accelerate the acquisition of a vessel to support EO 14296.105 Some private companies and observers, however, contend that leasing a vessel can be more cost-effective for the federal government, compared with purchasing a vessel.106 Increasing the number of vessels in the federal oceanographic fleet to address national security concerns, such as competing with China in the ocean-research space, would require Congress to increase appropriations and support for federal investment in oceanographic vessels.

Alternatives to Research Vessels

In the decade since the 2016 federal fleet status report, certain federal agencies have increasingly deployed marine technology, including UMSs. For example, NOAA has deployed UMSs for "the collection and utilization of critical, high accuracy, and time-sensitive data."107 According to NOAA, the benefits of UMSs include lower costs to own and operate, and reduced safety risk in remote and extreme environments.108 Congress also has encouraged NOAA to expand UMS operations across the agency and to coordinate research and acquisition of UMSs with the Navy and other federal agencies, industry, and academia.109 UNOLS contends that "despite rapid advances in satellites, robotics, and autonomous platforms, research vessels remain indispensable," given they provide for the direct sampling of the ocean and seafloor; the deployment, recovery, and servicing of marine technologies (e.g., buoys); and other services.110 To illustrate this, in May 2026, NOAA announced a $21.6 million award for the purchase of UMSs to be deployed from new NOAA charting and mapping vessels.111 As another example, that same month, NSF announced a plan to remove a network of ocean observation arrays, collectively referred to as the Ocean Observatories Initiative, "subject to ship scheduling and other operational constraints."112 NSF has since paused its plans to remove these arrays in response to congressional and other stakeholder concerns.113 Congress may seek to better understand the relative costs and benefits of advanced marine technologies, including UMSs, versus research and survey vessels for fulfilling national maritime priorities.

Congress has previously directed NOAA to provide information about the agency's use of "chartering agreements, uncrewed assets capable of operating independent of vessels, and commercial data purchase agreements" when its oceanographic research vessels are out of commission.114 Congress could consider directing other agencies to do the same. It could also direct the IWG-FI to broaden such analysis—in a federal fleet status report or elsewhere—to include information about how certain federal agencies are using UMSs and commercial data buys in conjunction with or independent of federally owned or leased oceanographic research vessels (independent of when research vessels are out of commission). Such an analysis could inform congressional decisions related to the federal oceanographic fleet, including whether the fleet should maintain or decrease its current size in light of the future demand for and use of UMSs.
