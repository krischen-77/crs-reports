Background and Policy
                                                                                                           Elizabeth A. Myers
An Employee Stock Ownership Plan (ESOP) is a type of defined contribution (DC) retirement       Analyst in Income Security
plan designed to invest primarily in employer securities. In DC plans, workers are provided
individual accounts funded by their own contributions, contributions from their employers, or
both. Contributions and any investment earnings accrue to a participant’s account, which can
provide a source of income in retirement.

The most common type of DC plan is the 401(k) plan, which permits employees to defer compensation into their own
accounts. In contrast, ESOPs are primarily or solely funded by employers: employees typically do not contribute to ESOPs to
purchase shares of employer securities for their accounts. ESOPs, like nearly all tax-qualified private-sector DC plans, are
governed by the Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974 (ERISA, P.L. 93-406) and provisions in the Internal
Revenue Code (IRC).

To establish ESOPs, companies set up ESOP trusts, which acquire shares of employer securities sold by shareholders, by the
company, or on the open market. In some cases, companies or ESOP trusts use loans to finance the purchase of shares. The
value of shares acquired by the ESOP trust, which may increase if the value of the company sponsoring the ESOP increases,
is allocated to employees’ individual accounts as they work for the sponsoring company. Upon separating from employment
or retiring, employees receive the cash value of their shares, or the shares themselves, which can be used as part of their
retirement income. Participants are taxed on the value of their account when distributed, as is the case with other tax-deferred
retirement plans.

Company owners commonly use ESOPs to transfer partial or full ownership of their businesses. Owners have several ways to
sell their companies, such as initial public offerings (IPOs), direct sales to individuals, or sales to private equity firms.
However, some owners find ESOPs attractive because they are a way to reward employees, preserve jobs, and promote the
continuity of the business. In addition, ESOPs provide selling shareholders with a readily available market to purchase shares
(and tax advantages for certain sellers). As the Baby Boomer generation reaches retirement age in the United States, ESOPs
may become increasingly relevant as a business transition tool.

ESOPs can be stand-alone plans or combined with other retirement plans. The most common type of hybrid plan is the
KSOP, which combines an ESOP with 401(k) features. In 2023, about 6,500 ESOPs (which includes KSOPs) covered
roughly 15 million participants. While most ESOPs are stand-alone plans sponsored by privately held businesses and have
fewer than 250 participants, most participants are in KSOPs sponsored by publicly traded corporations. A number of these
corporations created ESOPs decades ago in response to favorable tax changes and, in some cases, as a means of preventing
corporate takeovers that were prevalent during the 1980s. Publicly traded companies might offer KSOPs to their employees
as a way to provide company stock investment exposure. Others offer stand-alone ESOPs that are not combined with a
401(k) plan.

Congress has expressed bipartisan interest in facilitating employee ownership of firms, particularly through the promotion
and expansion of ESOPs. In July 2025, the Senate Committee on Health, Education, Labor, and Pensions (HELP) held a
hearing entitled “Empowering Workers by Expanding Employee Ownership,” and in January 2024, the Senate Committee on
Small Business and Entrepreneurship held a hearing entitled “Succession Planning: Opportunities to Build Wealth and Keep
Jobs in Local Communities.” In the 117th Congress, the SECURE 2.0 Act of 2022 (P.L. 117-328) included multiple
provisions related to ESOPs, such as expanding a tax deferral benefit to shareholders selling S corporation stock to ESOPs
and directing the Department of Labor (DOL) to create an Employee Ownership Initiative.

This report provides an overview of ESOPs and how they function; selected ERISA and IRC requirements regarding
participation, contributions, investments, and more; data on ESOP prevalence and participation; a discussion of stakeholder
tax advantages, benefits, risks, and other considerations; and relevant policy issues. It also provides a legislative history and a
summary of proposed legislation in the 119th Congress that would modify ESOP provisions and encourage employee
ownership through ESOPs.

Overview of ESOPs

An Employee Stock Ownership Plan (ESOP) is a type of defined contribution (DC) retirement
plan that is designed to invest primarily in shares of stock issued by the sponsoring employer,
called qualifying employer securities. In DC plans, workers are provided individual accounts
funded by their own contributions, contributions from their employers, or both. Contributions and
any investment earnings accrue to a participant’s account, which can provide a source of income
in retirement. The 401(k) plan is the most common type of DC plan. However, unlike 401(k)
plans, which permit employees to defer compensation to fund their own accounts, ESOPs are
primarily or solely funded by employers—employees typically do not contribute to ESOPs to
purchase shares of employer securities for their accounts.1 ESOPs, like all tax-qualified private-
sector DC plans, are governed by the Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974
(ERISA, P.L. 93-406) and provisions in the Internal Revenue Code (IRC).

To establish ESOPs, companies set up ESOP trusts, which acquire shares of employer securities
sold by shareholders, by the company, or on the open market. In some cases, companies or ESOP
trusts use loans to finance the purchase of shares. The value of such shares acquired by the ESOP
trust, which may increase if the value of the company sponsoring the ESOP increases, is allocated
to employees’ individual accounts as they work for the sponsoring company. Upon separating
from employment or retiring, employees receive the cash value of their shares, or the shares
themselves, which can be used as part of their retirement income. Participants are taxed on the
value of their account when distributed, as they are with other tax-deferred retirement plans.

ESOPs are commonly used to transfer business ownership. An owner of a private company might
sell part or all of his or her company to the ESOP, where it becomes owned by employees, as an
alternative to selling the company to an outside buyer (e.g., another individual or a private equity
firm), a management buyout, or going public. As the United States undergoes a demographic
trend in which the Baby Boomer generation—comprising those born between 1946 and 1964—
reaches retirement age (a trend sometimes referred to as the “silver tsunami”), ESOPs may
become increasingly relevant as a business transition tool. Many older individuals own
businesses: The Census Bureau’s 2019 Annual Business Survey found that 51% of business
owners were aged 55 and older in 2018.2 Some stakeholders have indicated that many older
Americans who own businesses lack exit planning strategies.3 ESOPs represent one option for
certain business owners looking to transition out of their businesses.4

Who Can Sponsor an ESOP?

In the United States, major business types include sole proprietorships, partnerships, C
corporations, and S corporations.9 ESOPs are available only to C and S corporations because they
are the corporate forms that issue stock.10

C corporations, also known as ordinary corporations, are named for Subchapter C of the IRC,
which details their tax treatment. A C corporation is considered to be an entity that is separate
from its owners (shareholders) for legal purposes. C corporations are taxed once at the corporate
level and again at the individual shareholder level. The corporation pays tax on profits;
shareholders pay tax on dividends. C corporations can issue multiple classes of stocks. Some C
corporations are publicly traded, though most are privately held.11

S corporations are named for Subchapter S of the IRC, which details their tax treatment. S
corporations are pass-through entities (i.e., they generally do not pay corporate-level income tax
and operating income and loss are computed at the corporate level and passed through to the
shareholders), can only issue one class of stock, and must have no more than 100 shareholders,
and those shareholders must be either eligible individuals or one of a limited number of eligible
entities.12 S corporation earnings are allocated among shareholders, who pay tax on the earnings
at their own individual tax rates in the year earned, regardless of whether the income is
distributed to shareholders. S corporations are often “closely held” businesses, in which the
business is owned by a limited number of owners.13 S corporation stock is not publicly traded.

Most ESOPs are sponsored by privately held companies, and most privately held companies that
sponsor ESOPs are S corporations. According to the National Center for Employee Ownership
(NCEO), a nonprofit organization that supports employee ownership initiatives, about 93% of the

How Does an ESOP Work?

First, a company creates an ESOP trust. An ESOP must be structured as a stock bonus plan or a
combination stock bonus/money purchase plan.17 The ESOP trust acquires company stock—with
or without the use of a loan from a lender—and allocates the value of company stock shares to
participants’ individual accounts over time.18 The shares of stock may increase in value as the
value of the company increases.19 When employees separate from the employer, they typically
receive the cash value of the company shares allocated to their accounts (though some plans
might distribute the shares themselves). The following sections provide more detail on how
companies set up and fund ESOPs.

Establishing an ESOP Trust

First, a company establishes an ESOP trust. The ESOP trust must be maintained pursuant to a
written document. The trust primarily holds shares of company stock but can also hold cash or
other investments. Once the ESOP trust holds shares of employer stock, the ESOP is considered a
legal shareholder of the corporation.20

The ESOP trust is governed by a trustee, appointed by the sponsoring company’s board of
directors, who manages the trust for the exclusive benefit of plan participants. An ESOP trustee
can be an individual or an entity (such as a bank or trust company) and can be internal or
independent. An internal ESOP trustee is a company employee who might be a member of a
management team, on the company board, or an ESOP participant.21 An independent trustee is not
affiliated with the company. ESOP trustees typically serve as the plan’s fiduciary, though any
individual who exercises discretion over plan decisions is considered a fiduciary under ERISA.22

corporation. A 30% initial purchase of employer stock is common, as this is the percentage at
which shareholders selling closely held C corporation stock receive special tax treatment (see
“Tax Advantages for Selling Shareholders”). Some companies might stay 30% ESOP-owned for
periods of time, while others might gradually increase the percentage owned by the ESOP by
selling additional shares to the ESOP over time.

The ESOP trust holds legal ownership of all company shares. As employees work for the
company sponsoring the ESOP, the value of shares is allocated to their individual accounts in the
ESOP trust based on a formula that considers, for example, relative pay. The participants in the
plans are the beneficial owners of their allocated stock shares and are treated as if they own the
assets, though the ESOP trustee is the legal owner of all shares held in the ESOP.

Leveraged ESOPs

Many companies borrow money to fund their ESOPs. In general, tax-qualified retirement plans
are not permitted to borrow money from certain related parties.29 However, ESOPs have an
exemption that permits them to borrow money from certain related parties to purchase company
stock if specific conditions are met (e.g., that the loan is primarily for the benefit of plan
participants and that the collateral consists only of qualifying employer securities).30

An ESOP trust can borrow money from the company, a selling shareholder, or a third-party lender
(such as a bank or a private equity group). In practice, third-party lenders generally do not loan
directly to ESOP trusts. Instead, they loan money to the company sponsoring the ESOP (this loan
is referred to as the outside loan). The company then uses that money to make a loan to the ESOP
(this loan is referred to as the inside loan). Outside loan repayment schedules are negotiated
between the third-party lender and the company. The inside loan term is between the company
and the ESOP and might be structured to have a longer repayment schedule.

Once the ESOP trust holds the loan proceeds (either because the ESOP took out a loan or the
company sponsoring the ESOP took out a loan and then loaned money to the ESOP), it purchases
shares of company stock from the company, from shareholders, or, for publicly traded shares, on
the open market.31 Loans used to purchase employer securities are referred to as acquisition

making tax-deductible contributions to the ESOP to repay the loan principal (and any interest
accrued on the loan).36 This can be a tax-advantaged strategy compared with a company taking
out a traditional loan from a bank to finance business activities.

Nonleveraged ESOPs

Nonleveraged ESOPs, or the companies sponsoring them, do not borrow money from certain
related parties to acquire employer securities for the ESOP.37 Nonleveraged ESOPs acquire
company stock in the following ways:
    •  A company contributes shares directly or issues new shares to the ESOP.
    •  The company makes cash contributions to the ESOP trust.38 The ESOP uses the
       cash to purchase shares from the existing owner(s) or shareholders. The selling
        shareholder receives cash from the ESOP in exchange for the company shares.39

Employers make fixed contributions or discretionary contributions to the ESOP trust, typically on
an annual basis. Employers might choose to make discretionary contributions based on the
company’s performance in a year, though IRS requires that employers maintain “substantial and
recurring” contributions to maintain the plan’s tax-qualified status.40

Other Investments in an ESOP Trust

In addition to employer stock shares, ESOP trusts might have other holdings, such as cash,
mutual funds, or exchange-traded funds (ETFs). ESOPs holding C corporation stock may receive
dividends on such stock shares. ESOPs holding S corporation stock may receive distributions on
S corporation shares (which are similar to dividends on C corporation shares) into the trust.

Dividends in C Corporations. C corporations may pay dividends on company stock shares.
Dividends are a portion of a company’s profit that is paid to shareholders. Dividends can be paid
in stock, in cash, or a mixture of stock and cash.

but instead as earnings in the ESOP. Like other earnings on ESOP shares, dividends reinvested in
the ESOP are taxed at normal income tax rates when participants take withdrawals.

participants’ accounts) or unallocated shares (i.e., those held in a suspense account and not yet
allocated to participants’ accounts).

distributions on S corporation shares. S corporations that pay distributions must do so on a pro
rata basis among all shareholders (which, in an S corporation with an ESOP, includes the ESOP
trust and any other shareholders).45 S corporation distributions paid on stock held in an ESOP are
not deductible to the corporation.46

S corporation distributions can be used to pay ESOP administrative expenses, purchase additional
shares from selling shareholders, buy back shares from separating employees, or repay an ESOP
loan.47 Like dividends on C corporation stock used to repay loans, distributions on S corporation
stock used to repay loans can come from allocated shares or unallocated shares.48

Selected ESOP Requirements

As private-sector employee benefit plans, ESOPs are subject to ERISA’s provisions in Title 29 of
the U.S. Code.64 In addition, ESOPs must meet many of the same IRC requirements that apply to
other tax-qualified plans (such as 401(k) plans) to maintain their tax-qualified status. Failure to
meet the IRC’s requirements can result in plan disqualification or excise taxes; failure to meet
ERISA’s requirements can result in civil penalties and fiduciary liability.

One of ERISA’s standards requires that, in general, employee benefit plan fiduciaries must
diversify the investments of a plan “so as to minimize the risk of large losses, unless under the
circumstances it is clearly prudent not to do so.”65 Because ESOPs are designed to be invested
primarily in employer stock, this diversification requirement does not apply.66

Neither statute nor regulations specify the percentage of plan assets that must be invested in
qualifying employer securities to be considered “primarily” invested in qualifying employer
securities. DOL Advisory Opinion 1983-06A stated that “an employee benefit plan that is
required under the plan documents to have more than 50 percent of its assets invested in
qualifying employer securities” would not “contravene” ERISA’s requirement that the ESOP be
designed to invest primarily in qualifying employer securities.67 An IRS document designed for
IRS determination specialists to understand ESOPs for the purposes of issuing favorable
determination letters noted that “the term generally requires at least 50% of the ESOP assets to be
invested in employer securities” but also that “in actuality however, facts and circumstances
would have to be taken into account, such as the investment performance of the securities.”68

may not exceed the limit.

fully vested in the plan.

Nondiscrimination Requirements

ESOPs, like other tax-qualified retirement plans, must provide that plan contributions and
benefits do not discriminate in favor of HCEs.100 To do so, plans must satisfy annual
nondiscrimination tests, which are intended to ensure that nonelective contributions, elective
deferrals, and matching contributions are not concentrated among HCEs. 101

Not all of these tests will apply to all ESOPs. For example, stand-alone ESOPs sponsored by
companies that do not operate separate DC plans would be subject only to the tests measuring
nonelective contributions.

In addition, ESOPs generally cannot be aggregated with other plans for the purposes of
nondiscrimination testing, and plans with ESOP and non-ESOP components (e.g., KSOPs) must
test them separately.102

Testing Nonelective Employer Contributions

Plans must meet requirements to ensure that nonelective contributions are distributed among a
broad range of participants. Nonelective contributions are contributions that do not depend on an
employee making contributions to his or her own account (i.e., nonmatching contributions). In an
ESOP that does not provide matching contributions, all employer contributions are nonelective
contributions.

The IRS provides a safe harbor that, if adopted, permits plans to avoid further nondiscrimination
testing.103 To meet this safe harbor, an ESOP must allocate contributions using a uniform
allocation formula based on (1) the same percentage of compensation for the plan year, (2) the
same dollar amount, or (3) the same dollar amount for each unit of service (e.g., hour or day

satisfy the requirement by satisfying a separate test called the general test, which tests whether the
benefits provided to HCEs and NHCEs are nondiscriminatory. The general test compares
allocations based on “rate groups” of employees and is typically more complicated than using a
safe harbor allocation formula.

Top-Heavy Requirements

A DC plan is top-heavy if key employees (i.e., certain officers and business owners) have
aggregate account balances that exceed 60% of all employees’ balances.112 ESOPs that meet the
definition of a top-heavy plan must comply with special rules to ensure that rank-and-file
employees are also benefiting from the plan.113

If a plan is deemed to be top-heavy, the employer must make contributions equal to at least 3% of
compensation for each non-key employee. Employer matching contributions, if provided, are
considered for the purposes of this contribution.

In cases where the highest percentage at which employer contributions are made for a key
employee is less than 3%, the employer contribution made for non-key employees may not
exceed that percentage. For example, if the highest percentage at which employer contributions
are made for a key employee is 2%, then the employer would not have to make contributions on
behalf of non-key employees in excess of 2%.

Anti-Abuse Requirements for S Corporation ESOPs

ESOPs were originally limited to C corporations. After Congress permitted S corporations to
sponsor ESOPs in the late 1990s, several S corporation ESOPs were developed that benefited
only a few highly compensated employees. The Economic Growth and Tax Reconciliation Act of
2001 (EGTRRA; P.L. 107-16) required that ESOPs holding S corporation stock must comply
with anti-abuse rules.114 These rules are intended to prevent S corporation ESOPs from being
used as tax shelters and to ensure that a broad range of rank-and-file employees benefit from the
plan.115

In general, these rules require that during years in which certain individuals own 50% or more of
the employer stock in the S corporation ESOP, the employer cannot allocate additional employer

disqualified person during a non-allocation year. A disqualified person includes
    •  an individual who owns, or whose family members own, at least 20% of the total
      S corporation shares held by the ESOP,117 or
    •   an individual who owns at least 10% of the total S corporation shares held by the
     ESOP and who is not described above.

Voting Rights
ESOP participants have the right to vote the shares held in their accounts.119 Technically, the
ESOP participants’ voting rights are “pass-through,” and the ESOP trustee votes the ESOP shares
based on how the participants direct the trustee to vote.

In companies sponsoring ESOPs with registration-type classes of securities (e.g., common stock
and preferred stock required to be registered with the Securities and Exchange Commission
[SEC]), ESOP participants must have the right to direct the ESOP on how to vote their shares for
all matters that are subject to a shareholder vote.120

Most companies sponsoring ESOPs do not have registration-type classes of securities. In this
case, ESOP participants may be limited to voting on major corporate events (e.g., mergers,
recapitalizations, liquidations). The NCEO found that these issues typically do not come up in
most ESOP companies.121

Leveraged ESOPs have both allocated shares (allocated to participants’ accounts) and unallocated
shares (which are held in a suspense account within the ESOP trust). Plans may allow participants
to vote the shares allocated to their accounts or provide for a one-vote-per-participant
arrangement. 122 In plans with unallocated shares, trustees may vote such shares without regard to
participants’ directions. Publicly traded companies with ESOPs commonly have “mirror voting,”
in which participants are permitted to vote unallocated shares in proportion to allocated shares.

Plans may be more generous in conferring voting rights to participants. For example, closely held
corporations may permit participants to vote on additional issues not specified in law or
regulations.

Diversification Requirements

ESOPs are designed to be primarily invested in employer stock. Primarily investing in one
company’s stock carries inherent risk, as an ESOP participant who works for a company that goes
bankrupt could lose a portion or all of his or her retirement savings and job at the same time.

To address this risk, a provision in the Tax Reform Act of 1986 (P.L. 99-514) requires that certain
ESOPs permit individuals who reach age 55 with 10 years of participation in the plan to diversify
a portion of their account.123 This diversification requirement applies for six years (the first year
occurring at age 55 with 10 years of service). During the 90-day period following the end of each
of the six years, a participant may diversify up to a cumulative total of 25% of the account,
reduced by the portion of the account diversified in prior years.124 Following the sixth year, the
participant may diversify up to a cumulative total of 50% of the account balance.

These rules apply to ESOPs sponsored by privately held companies. They also apply to ESOPs
sponsored by publicly traded companies if the ESOP (1) is stand-alone, (2) does not permit
employee contributions or provide matching contributions (based on the amount that an
individual contributes to a separate plan sponsored by the employer), and (3) is a separate plan
from any other DB plan or DC plan maintained by the same employer.

Plans must provide at least three investment options for participants diversifying their accounts.
Instead of diversifying their investments, these participants may choose to receive an in-service
distribution of the portion of their account eligible for diversification. (Plans are generally limited
in providing in-service distributions; ESOPs have an exception in this case.)

Some employers that sponsor 401(k) plans in addition to ESOPs permit employees who are
eligible to diversify their accounts to transfer diversification-eligible amounts to the company’s
401(k) plan instead of diversifying within the ESOP.125 This can be advantageous for ESOP
sponsors because it provides a way to ensure that the ESOP remains primarily invested in
employer stock.

When Benefits Begin

As a type of DC plan, ESOPs are subject to distribution requirements that apply to DC plans
generally. They are also subject to a provision in the U.S. Code that applies exclusively to ESOPs
and is intended to allow ESOPs to speed up benefit distributions compared with other DC plans.
Together, these provisions govern when ESOPs are permitted, or required, to distribute benefits to
participants.

ESOPs must allow participants (with the consent of their spouses, if applicable) to choose to
receive their ESOP benefits no later than (1) one year following the end of the plan year in which
the participant dies, separates from service due to disability, or reaches the ESOP’s normal
retirement age, or (2) the sixth plan year that occurs after the plan year in which the participant
separated from employment for any other reason (e.g., switching to a new job).128 For example,
for a plan that follows the calendar year, a participant who retires at age 62 in 2026 could begin
receiving their benefits no later than December 31, 2027. A participant who separates from
service at age 30 (for reasons other than death or disability) in 2026 could begin receiving
benefits no later than December 31, 2032 (i.e., the sixth plan year after the plan year in which the
participant separated).129 These rules typically result in ESOPs commencing benefit payments
earlier than DC plans would generally begin doing so (as described in the next two
paragraphs).130

of the plan year in which (1) the participant reaches age 65 or the normal retirement age of the
plan, (2) the 10th anniversary of the participant’s participation in the plan occurs, or (3) the
participant separates from employment.131

Tax Advantages for Selling Shareholders

Some company owners sell a portion, or the entirety, of their company to an ESOP as a
succession planning tool or to diversify their own assets. An ESOP provides a readily available
market to purchase shares from an owner.

A selling shareholder (e.g., a company owner) of closely held C corporation stock receives tax
advantages if that stock is sold to an ESOP: Taxation of capital gains that occurs when a
shareholder sells stock to an ESOP that would normally be due at the time of sale can be deferred
when meeting certain requirements.153 This is referred to as a Section 1042 transaction.154
Requirements include, for example, that the seller files written statements of election and
purchase with the IRS, that the sale proceeds be reinvested into qualified replacement property
(QRP) (e.g., stocks and bonds of other U.S. corporations), and that the ESOP owns at least 30%
of the company stock following the sale. Capital gains taxes may be due when the shareholder
later sells the QRP.

To be eligible for a Section 1042 transaction, the securities that are sold by the shareholder to the
ESOP cannot have been readily tradable on an established securities market (i.e., publicly traded)
for at least one year prior to the sale, and such securities must have been held by the selling
shareholder for at least three years prior to the sale to the ESOP.155

As an example, consider a shareholder who owns 100 shares of closely held C corporation stock
with a basis of $200,000. This shareholder sells his or her shares of company stock to an ESOP
for $1,000,000, representing a gain of $800,000. Normally, the shareholder would be taxed on
this $800,000 of capital gains in the year of the sale to the ESOP. However, since the shareholder
is eligible for and chooses Section 1042 treatment, he or she can reinvest the $1,000,000 into
QRP. The QRP’s basis is $200,000, rather than $1,000,000, because it was reduced by the
$800,000 of gain deferral for selling to an ESOP. When the shareholder later sells the QRP, the
gain is calculated based on the $200,000 basis, so if the investment grows to $2,000,000, the
shareholder would then owe taxes on $1,800,000 in capital gains.

Shareholders selling stock to an ESOP must sell at the stock’s fair market value. Other methods of
selling company ownership could result in higher payouts for the selling stockholder because they
can be negotiated (e.g., selling to another individual or to a private equity firm).

Some owners selling closely held C corporation stock to ESOPs might consider the inheritance
benefit of the transaction. If the selling shareholder dies while holding the QRP, the QRP receives
a “step-up” in basis to fair market value, which allows heirs to inherit the QRP and pay little to no
capital gains tax on any appreciation that occurred.

Starting in 2028, selling shareholders of S corporation stock will also begin to receive tax
advantages for selling their shares to an ESOP. Section 114 of SECURE 2.0 (P.L. 117-328)
expanded the Section 1042 transaction provision—with a 10% limit—to shareholders that sell S
corporation stock to ESOPs, effective for sales to ESOPs after 2027.

allow it to purchase company stock shares, and using the cash received from the ESOP for its
business goals. To pay back the ESOP’s debt to the company, the company makes tax-deductible
contributions to the ESOP.

because the ESOP does not pay tax itself. At the shareholder level and over a short period, this
functions as a tax exemption: While most shareholders report S corporation income on their
individual income tax return and pay taxes, the ESOP does not. This can offer advantages,
particularly for S corporations that are 100% owned by an ESOP. In that case, the S corporation
does not need to make annual distributions to cover its owners’ tax responsibilities, since its only
owner (the ESOP) does not pay tax. However, the income of an S corporation owned by an ESOP
is not exempt from taxation indefinitely. Later (potentially up to the length of a full career), the S
corporation’s income will be taxed on the employee-owner’s individual income tax return when it
is distributed as retirement income.168

Tax Advantages for ESOP Participants

Participants who accrue benefits in an ESOP can defer taxation until such benefits are distributed,
rather than including the benefits in their taxable income in each year that they are earned. This
can represent a significant tax benefit, as any appreciation on stock shares allocated to a
participant’s account or other investment earnings will accrue on a tax-deferred basis.

Individuals include distributions from ESOPs in their taxable income, which are taxed at normal
income tax rates. However, some ESOP participants might be eligible for an additional tax
benefit: NUA treatment of employer stock appreciation, in which distributions are taxed at capital
gains tax rates, which are typically lower than normal income tax rates (see “Tax Treatment of
Distributions”).

Costs and Planning Requirements for Companies

Certain companies might be better suited than others for ESOPs. One ESOP firm noted that
companies with stable cash flow, reliable profitability, at least 20-25 or more employees, and a
culture that promotes accountability and engagement are typical candidates for an ESOP.173

Companies considering ESOPs often conduct feasibility studies to determine whether an ESOP
aligns with the company’s goals. Feasibility studies assess, among other things, how different
plan designs might affect cash flow, tax savings, financing, employee benefits, the repurchase
obligation, shareholder impacts, and coordination with other employer-sponsored benefit plans.174

Aside from feasibility studies, other ESOP costs include those related to setup and
implementation, as well as annual (or more frequent) appraisals. The NCEO stated that setup
costs ranged from $150,000 to $500,000 in 2026 but noted that “selling a business through any
method is almost always both complex and costly.”175 A company with 100 or fewer employees
that starts an ESOP may be eligible for a federal tax credit to offset a portion of retirement plan
startup costs.176

In addition, some stakeholders identified litigation risk as a consideration for companies
sponsoring ESOPs.177 For example, an ESOP trustee who pays more than fair market value for
company stock shares to be acquired by the ESOP could be sued by DOL or plan participants for
violating fiduciary duties (see the “Valuation of Employer Securities” section).

The Repurchase Obligation

Companies with ESOPs must ensure that they have sufficient cash flow to finance the repurchase
obligation, which occurs when companies buy back stock shares at fair market value from
separating employees. Successful companies might have large increases in the value of their stock
shares, and companies must consider this when planning to buy back employees’ shares in the
future. Because the repurchase obligation can represent a challenging issue, many ESOPs conduct
sustainability studies, which, among other things, assess the company’s future cash flow
requirements and determine a management succession plan.

In addition, lenders to companies with ESOPs often require periodic repurchase obligation
studies, which provide the lender with estimates of the liabilities that the company owes when
buying back departing employees’ stock shares.178

Selected Policy Issues

ESOPs are complicated structures that intersect with a variety of policy issues. Among these
issues, valuation, facilitating ESOP sponsorship, and contribution limits have recently received
congressional attention.

Valuation of Employer Securities

An ESOP trustee is a fiduciary who is, among other duties, responsible for the valuation of
employer securities when sold to an ESOP and subsequently on a regular, typically annual, basis.
Regular valuations of employer securities held by an ESOP ensure that participants can receive
periodic statements of their account balances, companies can plan for their repurchase
obligations, and companies or ESOPs can make cash distributions.

An ESOP trustee can be an individual or an entity (such as a bank or trust company) and can be
internal or independent. An internal ESOP trustee is a company employee who might be a
member of a management team, on the company board, or an ESOP participant.194 An
independent trustee is not affiliated with the company.

ESOP trustees must ensure that the valuation of employer securities purchased by an ESOP is

Conflicts of interest between various parties, such as the trustee and the company sponsoring the
ESOP, might create incentives that could lead to incorrect valuations. An overvaluation or
undervaluation of employer securities could harm ESOP participants. For example, an ESOP that
pays more than fair market value for employer securities would benefit the selling shareholder at
the expense of plan participants. When participants separate from the company sponsoring the
ESOP, the company (or the ESOP) pays the participant the fair market value of their shares
(based on the most recent valuation). If the most recent valuation had undervalued the securities,
the separating participant’s retirement assets would be undervalued, which could threaten his or
her financial security in retirement.

ERISA and the IRC have requirements intended to mitigate or avoid conflicts of interest. DOL,
the IRS, and the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation (PBGC) administer various parts of
ERISA. DOL administers Title I, which contains rules for reporting and disclosure, vesting,
participation, funding, fiduciary conduct, and civil enforcement. The IRS administers Title II of
ERISA, which makes conforming amendments to the IRC for tax-qualified plans.197 In addition,
the IRC has a number of separate requirements for plans to maintain their tax-qualified status
(e.g., adhering to contribution limits). ESOP fiduciaries must comply with both ERISA and IRC
requirements to avoid penalties, excise taxes, fiduciary liability, and plan disqualification. While
most ESOPs adhere to both sets of requirements, it is possible for a plan to run afoul of one but
not the other.198

Defining Adequate Consideration

ERISA’s definition of the term adequate consideration depends on whether the employer
securities being acquired or sold have a generally recognized market.203

For employer securities with a generally recognized market (e.g., publicly traded securities),
ERISA defines adequate consideration as the price prevailing on a national securities exchange
registered with the Securities and Exchange Commission (e.g., the New York Stock Exchange,
Nasdaq).204

defines adequate consideration as “the fair market value of the asset as determined in good faith
by the trustee or named fiduciary pursuant to the terms of the plan and in accordance with
regulations promulgated by the Secretary.”205

ESOP Valuation as Investment Advice under ERISA

An individual who is a fiduciary under Title I of ERISA is required, among other duties, to
“discharge his duties with respect to a plan solely in the interest of the participants and
beneficiaries.” ERISA identifies four standards of conduct: (1) a duty of loyalty, (2) a duty of
prudence, (3) a duty to diversify investments, and (4) a duty to follow plan documents to the
extent that they comply with ERISA.230 ERISA established situations in which a person qualifies
as a fiduciary.231 One of these situations is when an individual renders investment advice for a fee
or other compensation.

Following the enactment of ERISA, DOL issued regulations in 1975 that defined investment
advice using a five-part test. Individuals who provide recommendations that meet the definition
of investment advice to pension plans (and their participants) covered by pensions law are held to
ERISA’s fiduciary standard. The fiduciary standard is a higher standard of conduct than that for
individuals who provide recommendations that do not meet the definition.232 Individuals who are
held to the fiduciary standard are required to act solely in the interests of plan participants and
beneficiaries.

Legislative History of the ESOP

Louis Kelso, a political economist and lawyer, created the first ESOP in 1956 to enable the
employees of a privately held newspaper business to purchase the business from its retiring
owners.249 After exploring different ways for the employees to buy the business, Kelso noted that
the company’s retirement plan held funds sufficient to make a 30% down payment on the
company. Additionally, if the retirement plan were permitted to borrow, it could take out a loan
and pay the loan back through employer contributions. However, because tax-qualified plans are
not permitted to borrow from related parties under prohibited transaction rules, Kelso obtained
IRS approval for an exemption to the prohibited transaction.250

The first mention of ESOPs in federal law occurred in 1973, when the Regional Rail
Reorganization Act of 1973 (P.L. 93-236) created the Consolidated Rail Corporation (Conrail).251
As part of the legislation, Conrail’s system plan was required to consider how an ESOP could be
used to meet certain capitalization and other goals. During a 1973 hearing about the Northeastern
Railroad Transportation Crisis, Kelso, speaking as a witness, argued in favor of employee
ownership of the rail corporation.252 As part of a subsequent law, the United States Railway
Association Amendments Act of 1978 (P.L. 95-565), Conrail was required to set up an ESOP that
owned 15% of Conrail stock; the federal government owned the remaining 85% of Conrail stock.
Conrail later went public in 1987 when the federal government sold its 85% ownership stake,
representing the largest initial public offering (IPO) in the history of the U.S. at the time.253

Reportedly, during a meeting between Louis Kelso and Senator Russell Long, then-Chairman of
the Senate Finance Committee, Kelso convinced Senator Long to authorize ESOPs as a type of
DC retirement plan in ERISA (P.L. 93-406).254 The NCEO noted that “Long, inspired by this idea,
began a crusade to provide ESOPs with tax incentives and shepherded 17 pieces of legislation
through Congress in 12 years.”255

Legislation in the 1970s Following ERISA

Following the enactment of ERISA, subsequent legislation in the 1970s aimed to encourage the
formation of ESOPs by providing tax benefits or preferential treatment to corporations with
ESOPs and clarifying ESOP requirements.

The following sections describe legislation specifically affecting ESOPS, rather than those
affecting DC plans generally (e.g., those modifying DC plan contribution limits). Not every
provision in each law that modified requirements for ESOPs is discussed. For example, laws that
included provisions that solely clarified requirements for ESOPs are not included in the
discussion below.

The Trade Act of 1974 (P.L. 93-618), which was a wide-ranging legislative package addressing
international trade authority, included a provision authorizing the Secretary of Commerce to make
loans to trade-impacted applicants that acquired, constructed, or modernized plant facilities or
engaged in other purposes that would create new, long-term employment opportunities.
Corporations that applied for such loans received preferential treatment if they established an
ESOP, provided that the value of the stock purchased for the ESOP was equal to at least 25% of
the loan principal.256

As part of the Tax Reduction Act of 1975 (P.L. 94-12), which included tax cuts for individuals
and businesses that were intended to boost growth, Congress authorized Tax Reduction Act Stock
Ownership Plans (TRASOPs). TRASOPs were a type of ESOP in which the employer received a
1% tax credit for capital investments provided that the company contributed an equivalent
amount of stock to the ESOP.257

The Tax Reform Act of 1976 (P.L. 94-455) was a legislative package that the JCT described as
serving six major purposes: improving the equity of the tax system, simplifying the tax system,
extending the fiscal stimulus provided by the Tax Reduction Act of 1975 (P.L. 94-12),
encouraging capital formation, improving tax law administration, and revising estate and gift
taxes.258 Provisions affecting ESOPs were included in the section of the bill aimed at encouraging
capital formation. For example, P.L. 94-455 extended and expanded the 1% tax credit for
TRASOPs. The law includes a statement addressing congressional intent regarding ESOPs:

       The Congress, in a series of laws … and this Act has made clear its interest in encouraging
       employee stock ownership plans as a bold and innovative method of strengthening the free
         private enterprise systems which will solve the dual problems of securing capital funds for
        necessary  capital growth and of bringing about stock ownership by  all corporate
        employees.

The JCT noted that the provision was “considered desirable in order to broaden employee
ownership in business and thereby increase their interest in improving productivity.”259

Legislation in the 1990s and Early 2000s

Legislation in the 1990s permitted S corporations to sponsor ESOPs, added diversification
requirements for DC plans with publicly traded stock, and, in several cases, curbed perceived
abuse or reduced or eliminated generous tax incentives.

The Small Business Job Protection Act of 1996 (P.L. 104-188) permitted S corporations to
sponsor ESOPs beginning in 1998. Congressional motivation for this provision was to encourage
employee ownership of closely held businesses.271 It applied UBIT to an ESOP’s share of S
corporation earnings.272

P.L. 104-188 repealed the provision allowing qualified financial institutions to exclude 50% of
the interest on loans to ESOPs (which had been previously limited by P.L. 101-239).

The Taxpayer Relief Act of 1997 (P.L. 105-34) repealed UBIT for ESOPs, which resulted in
income attributable to S corporation stock being tax free while it is held in an ESOP. Congress
was concerned about possible double taxation of S corporation ESOPs, since all S corporation
income was subject to taxation at the shareholder level, in addition to the UBIT tax that was
applied to an ESOP’s share of S corporation earnings under P.L. 104-188.273 It also permitted S
corporation ESOPs make cash distributions to participants (rather than stock distributions).

Sense of Congress that costs related to ESOPs should not be disallowed for Department of
