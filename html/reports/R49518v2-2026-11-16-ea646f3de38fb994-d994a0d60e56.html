Summary

The United States and the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia signed agreements in July 2026 to advance their cooperation in nuclear energy and to establish enhanced international inspections and monitoring of Saudi nuclear material to prevent diversion from peaceful uses. As of November 2026, Congress is reviewing these agreements; U.S. law requires the President to submit to Congress agreements for significant international nuclear commerce that include nonproliferation obligations intended to ensure nuclear transfers will not be used to produce nuclear weapons. The Trump Administration has called for the pursuit of at least 20 such agreements with new countries to support U.S. nuclear exports under Executive Order 14299.

As part of broader efforts to diversify its economy and sources of energy, the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia is implementing a national program to build civilian nuclear power reactors and potentially develop the capacity for producing nuclear fuel using domestic uranium supplies. The kingdom is a party to the Nuclear Nonproliferation Treaty (NPT), which prohibits Saudi Arabia from having nuclear weapons and guarantees access to peaceful nuclear technology. The United States, the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA), and other countries have provided technical and advisory assistance concerning the Saudi nuclear program.

On August 24, 2026, President Donald Trump submitted to Congress a U.S.-Saudi agreement for cooperation on peaceful uses of nuclear energy (hereinafter "123 agreement"), pursuant to Section 123 of the Atomic Energy Act (AEA) of 1954, as amended, together with a Bilateral Safeguards Agreement (BSA). The Administration also submitted two side letters to the agreement to Congress; the letters have not been publicly released. The BSA, which is a feature unique to the U.S.-Saudi Arabia 123 agreement, would involve IAEA monitoring and would need to be approved by the IAEA Board of Governors before the 123 agreement is implemented. The BSA has provisions similar to, but less extensive than, what would be included in an Additional Protocol to a comprehensive safeguards agreement with the IAEA. In the 123 agreement, Saudi Arabia commits to using "qualified suppliers" for its nuclear energy program, which are companies licensed or approved by the United States.

Under the AEA, Congress reviews Section 123 agreements for two periods totaling 90 days of continuous session. At the beginning of the second of these review periods, which lasts for 60 days of continuous session, joint resolutions of disapproval are to be automatically introduced in each house. After the second review period, the President can take the steps necessary for the agreement's entry into force unless Congress adopts a joint resolution of disapproval that becomes law. Under the AEA, the review periods commence from the date of the agreement's transmittal to Congress. Based on the U.S.-Saudi agreement's receipt in Congress, the review periods for the U.S.-Saudi 123 nuclear agreement may conclude in December 2026.

Congress closely considers instances of nuclear cooperation that may enable uranium enrichment for fuel production, which is allowed under the NPT but can pose nuclear weapons proliferation risks. The proposed 123 agreement says the United States and Saudi Arabia "may explore options to support the long-term commercial feasibility of civil uranium enrichment capacity" in their respective countries and will initiate a two-year Joint Enrichment and Conversion Study. The agreement does not authorize transfer of enrichment technology. The agreement requires that the BSA must enter into force prior to the United States' "providing consent for enrichment or support for any enrichment facility" in Saudi Arabia. President Trump stated in a July 2026 social media post that, "There will be no enrichment of material."

Through 2024, successive U.S. Administrations, including the first Trump Administration, had linked a potential 123 agreement with Saudi Arabia to the kingdom's acceptance of enhanced international safeguards on its nuclear program and its forgoing acquisition of the most proliferation-sensitive nuclear facilities—those for enriching uranium or reprocessing spent nuclear fuel to obtain plutonium. These terms—which U.S. officials and international observers at times have referred to as "the gold standard"—were incorporated into the 2009 U.S. 123 agreement with Saudi Arabia's neighbor, the United Arab Emirates (UAE). An Agreed Minute (2009) to the U.S.-UAE 123 agreement states that its terms "shall be no less favorable in scope and effect" than U.S. agreements with other countries in the Middle East and that the United States will, at the UAE's request, consult "regarding the possibility of amending" the agreement with equally favorable terms. The U.S.-UAE agreement does not include qualified supplier provisions or a bilateral safeguards agreement.

Members of Congress have adopted varying positions on U.S.-Saudi nuclear cooperation. Some Members have argued that no agreement should go into effect until Saudi Arabia commits to forgo uranium enrichment and spent nuclear fuel reprocessing and to conclude an Additional Protocol to its comprehensive safeguards agreement with the IAEA. In 2025, the Trump Administration waived a provision of law that limited consideration of a 123 agreement because Saudi Arabia has not concluded an Additional Protocol. Other Members have welcomed the 123 agreement as a commercial opportunity for U.S. firms and as a means of displacing potential Chinese or Russian vendors from participation in Saudi Arabia's nuclear energy program. In the 119th Congress, some Members have introduced joint resolutions of disapproval for the proposed U.S.-Saudi 123 agreement (H.J.Res. 217 and S.J.Res. 221).

If the 123 agreement is not disapproved during the congressional review period, it may enter into force through an exchange of diplomatic notes between the two countries. President Trump stated on July 21, 2026, that implementation of the agreement would be contingent on Saudi Arabia signing the Abraham Accords, which involve the recognition of Israel. Saudi Arabia's Crown Prince said in 2025 that the kingdom wants to be a part of the Accords, but official Saudi statements condition recognition of Israel on the establishment of an independent Palestinian state with East Jerusalem as its capital. The government of Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu has opposed the establishment of a Palestinian state, and leading Israeli opposition figure Gadi Eisenkot has pledged that no Palestinian state will arise under a government he forms. The U.S.-Saudi 123 agreement does not mention Israel or the Abraham Accords, and in a Senate Foreign Relations Committee hearing in November 2026, the U.S. Ambassador-designate for Saudi Arabia said the matter is subject to continuing negotiations.

For more information see CRS Report RS22937, Nuclear Cooperation with Other Countries: A Primer, and archived CRS Report R40344, The United Arab Emirates Nuclear Program and Proposed U.S. Nuclear Cooperation.

This report surveys Saudi Arabia's nuclear energy program and policies, provides an overview of proposed U.S.-Saudi Arabia nuclear cooperation agreements' provisions, assesses the possible nuclear nonproliferation implications of the proposed agreements, examines bilateral and regional factors that may shape the agreements' consideration, and outlines relevant congressional precedents and procedures.

Saudi Arabia: Background and U.S Relations

Saudi Arabia is a monarchy governed by the Al Saud family. King Salman bin Abd al Aziz Al Saud (born 1935) has ruled since 2015. His son, Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman (born 1985, aka MBS), serves as Prime Minister and acts as the kingdom's principal decisionmaker. The king's age and health challenges suggest that a leadership transition could occur at any time. Since the 1940s, the United States and Saudi Arabia have maintained close relations anchored in security and energy partnerships and tested at times by disagreements over oil production, the Israeli-Palestinian conflict, terrorism, human rights, and regional security. U.S. forces are deployed in Saudi Arabia to aid in Saudi missile and air defenses and to provide training to Saudi security forces under long running cooperative programs.

Under the Crown Prince's leadership since 2017, Saudi Arabia has pursued an economic and social transformation agenda at home and a multifaceted foreign policy abroad involving complex relationships with the United States, U.S. rivals, other G-20 economies, and the developing world. The kingdom's Vision 2030 initiatives outline ambitious goals for reducing the kingdom's fiscal dependence on oil revenue, developing a diverse and globally connected domestic economy, and creating opportunity for Saudi citizens and foreign investors in sectors such as tourism, global logistics, mining, manufacturing, and artificial intelligence.

In 2025, President Trump and Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman exchanged visits, and announced progress on bilateral agreements on mining, defense, artificial intelligence, and civil nuclear energy cooperation. The leaders also discussed plans for what the White House has estimated may be "nearly $1 trillion"1 in trade and mutual investment, including purchases of U.S. goods and services. President Trump designated Saudi Arabia as a Major Non-NATO Ally in January 2026 and has notified Congress of more than $35 billion in proposed foreign military sales to the kingdom since December 2025, including the proposed sale of F-35 fighter aircraft.

As of 2026, the kingdom has made measurable progress on several of its Vision 2030 objectives, but conflict in the Persian Gulf and Red Sea regions is challenging Saudi plans and preferences. The U.S. and Israeli conflict with Iran since February 2026 and the subsequent resurgence of war between Saudi Arabia and the Yemen-based Ansarallah/Houthi movement have disrupted Saudi oil exports and economic activity and threaten Saudi infrastructure. Attacks attributed to Iran and Iran-backed groups in Iraq and Yemen have damaged energy sites and other government and civilian targets in Saudi Arabia and other Arab Gulf states.

Iranian attacks resulted in the death and injury of U.S. military forces deployed in Saudi Arabia and damaged U.S. military aircraft there. U.S. and Saudi forces have jointly struck targets associated with Iran-backed groups in Iraq. In September 2026, Secretary of State Marco Rubio said that "we have a very strong defense agreement with Saudi Arabia and we will live up to our commitments in that agreement."2

The possible entry into force of the 123 agreement and bilateral safeguards agreement and any future reactor sales or uranium enrichment cooperation would constitute a tangible deepening of bilateral relations. The underlying agreement may reflect both sides' mutual confidence and current leaders' intention that the governments should maintain close ties over the agreement's 30-year duration.

Saudi Arabia's Nuclear Energy Program

Rationale

Saudi Arabia holds nearly 17% of the world's proven reserves of crude oil, has the world's fourth-largest reserves of natural gas, and is the second-largest consumer of energy on a per capita basis in the Middle East.3 In 2025, oil and natural gas generated about 32% and 64% of the kingdom's electricity, respectively.4

In 2010, a Saudi royal decree called for the development of nuclear and renewable energy for use in electricity production and water desalination, as well as a means to diversify the country's energy supply. As part of this effort, the 2017 Saudi National Project for Atomic Energy (NPAE) includes plans to build large and small nuclear reactors. The NPAE is part of the Saudi government's broader Vision 2030 initiative to diversify the kingdom's economy, which includes efforts to expand the use of non-fossil-fuel-based energy to preserve oil and natural gas resources for revenue-generating exports. Vision 2030 includes a National Renewable Energy Program (NREP) to expand the generation of electricity from renewable sources such as wind and solar to supply 50% of the kingdom's electricity by 2030.5 In addition to the development of large and small reactors, Saudi Arabia is building a low power research reactor facility, and Saudi officials have stated their intent to mine domestic uranium resources and develop a capacity to enrich uranium and fabricate fuel for nuclear power plants.

Saudi Arabia's 2022 National Policy for the Atomic Energy Program states that the program is intended to help the country "move from an oil-dependent economy towards a more diversified and productive economy," meet "water desalination and other needs," and contribute to an alternative to the kingdom's "steady increase in the consumption of fossil fuel based energy."6 An additional priority of the nuclear power program, according to the Saudi energy minister, is "knowledge and technology transfer" such that the program "supports the development of national human and technical capabilities" in Saudi Arabia.7

Detailed information about Saudi authorities' projections for electricity demand growth, nuclear capital construction cost assumptions, and their evolving cost analyses for nuclear and other generation sources is not publicly available. As of 2026, Saudi economic transformation plans seek to position the kingdom as a hub for regional and international data centers and artificial intelligence technology, which may contribute to higher domestic electricity generation needs than previously assumed when nuclear power was first contemplated.

Program Plans and Progress

Power plants, research, and uranium exploration and extraction constitute the three components of the planned Saudi national nuclear program. The kingdom has worked to advance each component since 2017.

Power Generation

Saudi Arabia plans to develop and operate both large and small nuclear power plants to generate electricity. The government's longer term nuclear power capacity targets have evolved over time. In 2011, the King Abdullah City for Atomic and Renewable Energy (KA CARE) set a goal of developing more than 17 gigawatt electric (GWe) of nuclear power capacity by 2032, to include a proposal for 16 eventual power plants.8 However, the kingdom's nuclear and overall renewable energy targets were later amended formally through the development of Vision 2030, the NREP, and the approval of the 2017 National Project for Atomic Energy.

Preparations since 2017 have focused on plans to construct two 1-to-1.6 GWe nuclear reactors at Khor Duwayhin (alt. Duweihin, see Figure 1), a coastal area on the Persian Gulf between the kingdom's borders with Qatar and the United Arab Emirates (UAE).9 The kingdom initiated a bidding tender process for the construction of the two planned reactors, with Russia's Rosatom, France's Électricité de France (EDF), China National Nuclear Corporation (CNNC), South Korea's Korea Electric Power Corporation (KEPCO), and Westinghouse having all provided information for consideration. The Trump Administration states that the Saudi Government has repeatedly extended the timeline for completing the tender process to allow for the United States and Saudi Arabia to enter into a nuclear cooperation agreement and "has encouraged Westinghouse participation, while remaining committed to its completion and to selecting a vendor to build the Kingdom's first nuclear power plant."10

Separately, through a partnership with South Korea, the kingdom seeks to develop lower power, site-specific nuclear power plants using small modular advanced reactor technology (SMART) systems. Additionally, according to the unclassified Nuclear Proliferation Assessment Statement (NPAS) submitted by the Administration to Congress as required by the Atomic Energy Act,11

The Department of State's Foundational Infrastructure for Responsible Use of Small Modular Reactor Technology (FIRST) program works with Saudi Arabia through the GCC [Gulf Cooperation Council] Secretariat on a cost-shared basis to support the country's readiness to deploy U.S. vendors' SMR [small modular reactor] and other advanced nuclear technology under the highest, U.S.-aligned nuclear safety, security, and nonproliferation standards.

Research

Saudi Arabia has completed construction of a low power research reactor at the King Abd al Aziz City for Science and Technology (KACST) in Riyadh.12 The reactor is being built in partnership with Argentina's Investigacion Applicada (INVAP), and "will be used for nuclear science-related research and training."13 Additionally, KACST hosts an Atomic Energy Research Institute (AERI) that conducts research on "industrial applications of radiation and radioactive isotopes, nuclear power and reactors, nuclear materials, and radiation protection."14 KACST also oversees the kingdom's Nuclear Research Institute, which consists of a Nuclear Regulatory Unit and "national centers for accelerators, applied-radiation, irradiation, and nuclear technologies."15 The Saudi government has identified the development of indigenous research and applications expertise as a priority.16

Uranium Resources and Nuclear Fuel Development17

Mining is one of the main strategic pillars of the Vision 2030 project, and Saudi leaders are working to establish the kingdom as a hub for mineral extraction, processing, and supply chain integration. Saudi Arabia has engaged in extensive mineral exploration since about 2017, including with assistance from the government of the People's Republic of China. Over the medium term, Saudi Arabia seeks to develop its domestic uranium resources for use as nuclear reactor fuel. According to the NPAS, the kingdom "will depend on the international market for nuclear fuel services for the near-term."18

The Saudi Uranium Exploration Project has included joint uranium resource mapping efforts between the Saudi Geological Survey and China's CNNC, as well as a separate initiative with Jordan. To date, three main uranium deposit sites have been identified in northern and western Saudi Arabia—Qurriyah/Ghurayyah, Jabal Sayid, and the Al Jalamid phosphates zone in the Thaniyat Turayf basin adjacent to Jordan and Iraq.

According to a Nuclear Energy Agency and IAEA worldwide assessment of uranium resources, Saudi Arabia had about 64,100 tonnes of identified recoverable uranium resources (tU)19 in the polymetallic Th-U-Nb-Ta and rare earth element (REE)-bearing Qurriyah/Ghurayyah deposit and the Jabal Sayid U-Th prospect20 as of January 1, 2025.21 As of January 1, 2025, Saudi Arabia's total identified unconventional resources were 99,984 tU, including other deposits, such as the Thaniyat Turayf phosphorite prospect.22 Saudi Arabia plans to continue mineral exploration.

To date, the identified recoverable uranium resources are of low to very low grade,23 and may be included within deposits that also are enriched in other critical minerals.24 Saudi Arabia's identified recoverable uranium resources are only recoverable as a by-product or co-product (i.e., derived from a mine that produces other mineral commodities and may produce uranium as an additional product).25 The recoverable uranium resources identified in Saudi Arabia are considered among the more difficult and more expensive to extract with current methods and technologies compared with other types of deposits, based on what is known about their geologic characteristics.26

In May 2025, U.S.-based entities entered into a bilateral agreement with Saudi Arabia-based entities to support exploration and processing of potential REE deposits in the country, including the Jabal Sayid U-Th prospect.27 Such work may also advance uranium resource exploration and lead to more information about uranium resources in Saudi Arabia.

Regulatory Regime and Key Entities, Officials, and Partners

Saudi authorities have worked to develop nuclear energy laws and regulations with the support of the IAEA and the involvement of international consultants. IAEA officials completed a nuclear infrastructure review in Saudi Arabia in 2018 and issued a final report in January 2019.28 IAEA officials completed an Integrated Regulatory Review Service (IRRS) mission in October 2023.29

The Saudi cabinet approved a National Policy for the Atomic Energy Program in 2018 and supporting programs for occupational health and safety in 2020 and radioactive waste management, in 2021, respectively. The Saudi cabinet also has approved supporting laws governing nuclear and radiological controls, related civil liability, and providing for the establishment of the national Nuclear and Radiological Regulatory Commission (NRRC). Prince Abdulaziz bin Salman bin Abdulaziz, the king's son and half-brother to Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, serves as NRRC chairman and also as Minister of Energy and Chairman of the Board of King Abdullah City for Atomic and Renewable Energy (KA CARE).

The government established the Saudi Nuclear Energy Holding Company (SNEHC) in 2020 to develop and operate planned nuclear facilities, and, in 2022, established the subsidiary Duwayhin Nuclear Energy Company (DNEC) to own and operate the planned nuclear power plants at Khor Duwayhin. The kingdom also has engaged international firms in site selection, project management, legal, financial, and technical consultancy contracts related to the nuclear energy program.

Saudi Arabia and IAEA Safeguards

In 1988, Saudi Arabia acceded to the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons (NPT), Article II of which prohibits nonnuclear-weapon states-parties (NNWS) from acquiring nuclear weapons.30 Article III of the treaty requires NNWS to conclude Comprehensive Safeguards Agreements (CSAs) with the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA). (See Appendix B for more information.) Such agreements require governments to declare their entire inventory of certain nuclear materials, as well as related facilities; the agency subsequently applies safeguards "on all nuclear material in all nuclear activities" in the state.31 CSAs enable the IAEA to detect the diversion of nuclear material from peaceful purposes to nuclear weapons uses, as well as to detect undeclared nuclear activities and material.32 Safeguards include agency onsite inspections and continuous monitoring of declared nuclear facilities. Although all nuclear material in an NNWS is subject to IAEA safeguards, CSAs, as a practical matter, limit the agency's monitoring and inspections authority to declared facilities.

Saudi Arabia's CSA entered into force in 2009.33 Until 2024, the kingdom's agreement had a small quantities protocol (SQP) that held "in abeyance" most CSA procedures given the limited nuclear activities that were taking place in Saudi Arabia.34 Effective in 2025, the kingdom rescinded that SQP and the CSA is now fully operable.35

U.S. negotiators have engaged Saudi Arabia about the prospect of Saudi Arabia concluding an Additional Protocol (AP) to its CSA over successive Administrations.36 (See Appendix B.) To date, Saudi Arabia has not concluded such a protocol and, according to former longtime State Department official Robert Einhorn, "adamantly refused" to adopt an additional protocol arguing that potential "inspections at culturally sensitive locations, such as holy sites and palaces, would be unnecessarily intrusive."37 Saudi Arabia restricts access to areas near the city of Mecca and areas in the city of Medina in its role as custodian of the Islamic faith's principal holy sites.38 Some palaces may function as sensitive national security facilities for members of the royal family and government officials.

Rather than continuing to insist on an AP to Saudi Arabia's CSA as a condition for a U.S.-Saudi 123 Agreement, the Trump Administration negotiated a Bilateral Safeguards Agreement (BSA) as a supplement to the kingdom's CSA. An senior Administration official has stated that the BSA "will provide the Agency [IAEA] with more tools in the toolbox than it currently has to enact safeguards upon the kingdom."39 An analysis by the Arms Control Association contends that, while the BSA goes beyond AP requirements in some areas, it "does not ensure IAEA access to sites that are not part of Saudi Arabia's declared nuclear program."40

Proposed U.S.-Saudi Nuclear Cooperation

Since at least the early 2000s, the United States has discussed peaceful nuclear cooperation with Saudi Arabia. For example, in 2008, the two governments signed a nonbinding memorandum of understanding (MOU) that stated the governments' intent to cooperate on nuclear activities in the fields of medicine, industry, and electricity production, and noted Saudi Arabia's intent "to rely on existing international markets for nuclear fuel services as an alternative to the pursuit of enrichment and reprocessing."41

The United States has also negotiated with Saudi Arabia since the Obama Administration over the terms of a peaceful nuclear cooperation agreement pursuant to Section 123 of the Atomic Energy Act (AEA) of 1954, as amended (P.L. 83-703; 42 U.S.C. §§2153 et seq.). Such agreements, known as "123 agreements," generally authorize U.S. nuclear cooperation with other governments, but do not permit specific nuclear transfers. (See CRS Report RS22937, Nuclear Cooperation with Other Countries: A Primer, by Paul K. Kerr et al. and Appendix A.)

In 2012 and 2018, respectively, the Obama and first Trump Administrations engaged in discussions with Saudi counterparts on a possible U.S.-Saudi 123 agreement. From 2017 to 2019, the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) granted seven "Part 810" authorizations (per 10 C.F.R. §810) for U.S. companies to engage in civil nuclear discussions, including marketing, with Saudi Arabia in response to the kingdom's 2017 request for information.42 Such authorizations do not require a 123 agreement.43

The potential acquisition by a non-nuclear weapons state of facilities for enriching uranium enrichment and reprocessing spent nuclear reactor fuel has long been a source of U.S. government concern because such facilities can produce fissile material for nuclear weapons. Obtaining such material—highly enriched uranium (HEU) and/or plutonium—is widely regarded as the most difficult task in building nuclear weapons.

  • Uranium enrichment is a process that increases the concentration of the uranium-235 (U-235) isotope. Low-enriched uranium (below 20% enrichment in U-235) can be used in nuclear power reactors; HEU can be used as fuel in certain reactors but is also one of the two types of fissile material used in nuclear weapons.
  • Reprocessing spent nuclear reactor fuel separates plutonium, which is the other type of fissile material used in nuclear weapons, from the spent fuel's other elements. Plutonium can also be used as fuel in certain types of nuclear reactors.

The Government Accountability Office reported in 2020 that the United States and Saudi Arabia had "not made significant progress toward a nuclear cooperation agreement because of persistent differences … over nonproliferation conditions," including restrictions on enrichment and reprocessing, as well as Saudi reluctance to adopt an Additional Protocol to its CSA (see below).44 Those differences reportedly persisted during the Biden Administration, with U.S. and Saudi officials revisiting the prospect for civilian nuclear cooperation in the context of U.S. efforts to promote diplomatic normalization between Saudi Arabia and Israel.45 In 2022, U.S. and Saudi officials signed an MOU on the exchange of technical information and cooperation in nuclear safety matters. According to unnamed U.S. and Israeli officials cited in a September 2023 press report, the United States was then considering a U.S.-run uranium enrichment operation in Saudi Arabia.46

2026 Nuclear Cooperation Agreement47

Section 123a, paragraphs (1) through (9) of the Atomic Energy Act (AEA) of 1954, as amended, lists nine criteria that an agreement with a non-nuclear weapon state must meet to go into effect without a presidential exemption.48 The agreement under consideration includes these elements, which require:

  • safeguards on transferred nuclear material and equipment continue in perpetuity;
  • the IAEA applies comprehensive safeguards in NNWS;
  • nothing transferred is used for any nuclear explosive device or for any other military purpose;
  • the United States has the right to demand the return of transferred nuclear materials and equipment, as well as any special nuclear material produced through their use, if the cooperating state detonates a nuclear explosive device or terminates or abrogates an IAEA safeguards agreement;
  • the recipient state will not retransfer material or restricted data without U.S. consent, which can be in the form of subsequent arrangements. Such arrangements are required for additional forms of nuclear cooperation pursuant to an existing 123 agreement;49
  • the recipient state will maintain physical security on transferred nuclear material;
  • the recipient state will obtain approval before enriching or reprocessing transferred nuclear material or nuclear material produced with materials or facilities transferred pursuant to the agreement;
  • the recipient state will obtain U.S. advance approval for storage of transferred plutonium and HEU; and
  • any material or facilities produced or constructed through use of sensitive nuclear technology transferred under the cooperation agreement will be subject to all of the above requirements.

During Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman Al Saud's visit to the United States in November 2025, the United States and Saudi Arabia signed a "Joint Declaration on the Completion of Negotiations on Civil Nuclear Energy Cooperation."50 According to the White House, the declaration was to develop "the legal foundation for a decades-long, multi-billion-dollar nuclear energy partnership with the Kingdom; confirms that the United States and American companies will be the Kingdom's civil nuclear cooperation partners of choice; and ensures that all cooperation will be conducted in a manner consistent with strong nonproliferation standards."51

Later in November 2025, the Administration issued a waiver necessary for a U.S.-Saudi 123 agreement. Specifically, the Administration waived a provision of law prohibiting the executive branch from submitting a Nuclear Proliferation Assessment Statement (NPAS) for a proposed 123 agreement with a country, such as Saudi Arabia, which has not brought into force an Additional Protocol to its IAEA safeguards agreement (see below).52

On July 22, 2026, U.S. Secretary of Energy Chris Wright and Saudi Minister of Energy Prince Abd al Aziz bin Salman Al Saud signed a 123 agreement, together with a draft Bilateral Safeguards Agreement. On August 24, 2026, the Administration submitted to Congress the 123 agreement text, the required NPAS, the BSA, and a presidential determination that the proposed agreement "will promote, and will not constitute an unreasonable risk to, the common defense and security."53 The 123 agreement, which has a 30-year duration, is to enter into force after the governments exchange diplomatic notes informing each other that each government has completed its "domestic requirements" for the agreement's entry into force. The agreement states: "This Agreement may be amended upon mutual written agreement of the Parties." The nature of any mutually agreed amendments would determine the necessity of a new agreement pursuant to the AEA.

According to the 123 agreement, the United States and Saudi Arabia "intend to cooperate in the safe and secure use of nuclear energy for peaceful purposes."54 The text specifies that the two governments have agreed to develop Saudi "infrastructure and capacity … for peaceful uses of nuclear energy in a manner that takes full advantage of existing" Saudi resources, according to the agreement's text, which adds that Saudi Arabia "agrees to partnering with Qualified Suppliers for nuclear power reactors, nuclear reactor fuel, and all other aspects of the nuclear fuel cycle" in the country.

The agreement defines "qualified suppliers" as entities to which the United States and Saudi Arabia have "agreed in writing." Such suppliers may also be entities that have received or applied for licenses or authorizations from the Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) or Department of Energy for a nuclear facility (Article 1(T)). The NPAS described the requirement that Saudi Arabia partner with qualified suppliers as "an unprecedented commercial provision unique to the proposed Agreement."55

The text of the 123 agreement does not use the words "exclusive" or "exclusivity"; however, an Arms Control Association analysis said, "Reportedly, one of the confidential side letters includes additional details on an exclusivity arrangement with U.S. suppliers."56 The analysis concluded that the qualified supplier requirements are "clearly intended to favor U.S. companies." In October 2026 public remarks on the 123 agreement and in response to a question about potential Saudi cooperation with other countries on nuclear activities that would not involve materials or technology covered by the 123 agreement, National Nuclear Security Administration (NNSA) Deputy Administrator for Defense Nuclear Nonproliferation Matthew Napoli said,

The deal is … an exclusivity issue with the United States. So you will not be seeing … a Chinese reactor being built in Saudi Arabia. Or Chinese activities being built in Saudi Arabia. It's a bilateral agreement between the United States and it's exclusive with the United States. So under that premise there shouldn't be any other activities taking place within that country. And if they're doing something outside their Comprehensive Safeguards Agreement, the Agency [IAEA] should be able to have access to that and make sure they're not violating the terms of their … Comprehensive Safeguards Agreement with the Agency or their Bilateral Safeguards Agreement with the United States.… This agreement will provide the Agency with more tools in the toolbox than it currently has to enact safeguards upon the kingdom.57

Joint Enrichment and Conversion Study58

Stating that the United States and Saudi Arabia "may explore options to support the long-term commercial feasibility of civil uranium enrichment capacity," the 123 agreement specifically contemplates the U.S. transfer of sensitive nuclear technology to Saudi Arabia to support a "Joint Enrichment and Conversion Study."59 The study is to begin upon the agreement's entry into force and be completed within two years; within the agreement, the two governments "confirmed their shared understanding" that Saudi Arabia is not permitted to use any such transferred technology "for other purposes."

As part of the joint study, the governments are to "consult with a view to identifying appropriate options for" enriching uranium subject to the 123 agreement, as well as "for further development or demonstration" in the United States "of relevant technologies that could be deployed" in Saudi Arabia. These consultations are to "take into account … the specific characteristics of the technologies involved in such options," as well as "the global commercial market." The consultations are also to consider "the economic viability of" and "potential modalities for" a Saudi enrichment facility.

After the study's completion, the governments are to "consult regarding considerations for the transfer of sensitive nuclear facilities or sensitive nuclear technologies." However, the BSA must be in force prior to U.S. provision of "consent for enrichment or support for any enrichment facility" in Saudi Arabia. In addition, the transfer of "sensitive nuclear facilities and major critical components thereof" requires U.S. consent. The 123 agreement also requires U.S. consent in order for Saudi Arabia to engage in certain future enrichment activities. For example, Saudi Arabia could enrich either uranium transferred pursuant to the agreement or uranium "used in or produced through the use of equipment transferred" pursuant to the same agreement. But these activities would require U.S consent under the terms of the agreement.

Similarly, Saudi Arabia must also obtain U.S. consent, as well as complete a "Joint High-Assay Low-Enriched Uranium Enrichment Study," before modifying or operating an enrichment facility to produce enriched uranium containing more than 5% U-235. This provision covers enrichment facilities which use "equipment or technology transferred pursuant to" the 123 agreement. Any such enrichment agreement would permit Saudi production of only enriched uranium containing less than 20% U-235.

Bilateral Safeguards Agreement60

The package submitted to Congress by President Trump includes a Bilateral Safeguards Agreement, which the two governments signed this past July.61 The United States is not to "transfer … any nuclear material, equipment, or components pursuant to" the agreement until Saudi Arabia brings the BSA, which is a feature unique to the 123 agreement, into force and the IAEA Board of Governors has authorized the agency's Director General to "implement safeguards and verification measures identified in and related to" the 123 agreement. According to its text, the BSA is not to "affect" the IAEA's "rights and obligations" pursuant to Saudi Arabia's CSA. The United States and Saudi Arabia are to implement the BSA "consistent with" the IAEA's authorities under the agency's statute.

The BSA would require Saudi Arabia to conduct all uranium conversion, uranium enrichment, fuel fabrication, reprocessing, and "any associated research and development activity" at a "covered site." The BSA defines a covered site as "a geographic area delimited by Saudi Arabia … related to the scope" of such nuclear activities when undertaken pursuant to the 123 agreement. The Saudi government is to determine covered sites "following consultations" with the United States.

The BSA also contains provisions that supplement Saudi Arabia's CSA obligations to provide information about and access to the government's nuclear program. These provisions are more than is required by the government's CSA alone, but less than an Additional Protocol to the CSA would require (see Appendix B). For example, within 180 days of the BSA's entry into force, Saudi Arabia is to provide the IAEA with several categories of information concerning the government's nuclear program. These categories include

  • a "list of covered sites";
  • a "description of each building on each covered site that is under development, supplied, built, or operated in Saudi Arabia" under the 123 agreement;
  • a "description of nuclear fuel cycle-related research and development activities" on each covered site that do not include nuclear material; and
  • a list of "nuclear-related equipment and non-nuclear material" imported by Saudi Arabia pursuant to the 123 agreement.

Saudi Arabia is to provide the agency with "short notice access … to any place on a covered site to verify, or to resolve a question related to, the correctness and completeness" of the above-described Saudi-provided information. Should providing this access fail to resolve such a question, the United States and Saudi Arabia would "immediately consult" with the IAEA on "appropriate procedures and means to resolve the question … without delay."

As discussed above, a senior U.S. official has stated that the "agreement will provide the Agency with more tools in the toolbox than it currently has to enact safeguards upon the kingdom."62

Congressional Review

This section describes AEA congressional review procedures for a nuclear cooperation agreement, such as the agreement with Saudi Arabia, which the President has not exempted from any requirements of Section 123(a).63 Congress has the opportunity to review such an agreement during two consecutive time periods totaling 90 days of continuous session. After the review period, the President is permitted take the steps necessary for the agreement's entry into force.

The AEA requires the President to submit the text of the proposed nuclear cooperation agreement, along with the unclassified NPAS, to the House Foreign Affairs Committee and the Senate Foreign Relations Committee. The President must also authorize "the execution of the proposed agreement for cooperation" and make "a determination in writing that the performance of the proposed agreement will promote, and will not constitute an unreasonable risk to, the common defense and security." The President is then to consult with the committees "for a period of not less than 30 days of continuous session."

After this period of consultation, the President is to submit the agreement to Congress, along with the above-described presidential approval and determination, as well as the classified NPAS.64 This action begins the second review period, which lasts for 60 days of continuous session. At the beginning of this 60-day period, joint resolutions of disapproval are to be automatically introduced in each house. During this period, the committees are to hold hearings on the proposed agreement and "submit a report to their respective bodies recommending whether it should be approved or disapproved." If either committee has not reported the requisite joint resolution of disapproval by the end of 45 days, the committee is automatically discharged from further consideration of the measure. After the joint resolution is reported or the committee is discharged from considering the resolution further, Congress is to consider it under expedited procedures, as established by Section 130i of the AEA.

The agreement can become effective after the 60-day period unless, during that time, Congress adopts a joint resolution disapproving the agreement and the resolution becomes law (after being signed by the President or a veto is overridden). In practice generally, the President has submitted the agreement to Congress, along with the unclassified NPAS, its classified annex, and his approval and determination, at the beginning of the full 90-day period. Congress has considered the 60-day period as following immediately upon the expiration of the 30-day period.

Section 1264 of the National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2020 (P.L. 116-92, §1264; 42 U.S.C. §2153 note) prohibits the President from providing to Congress an NPAS for a 123 agreement with a country lacking an additional protocol.65 The President can take action to waive this prohibition, with any waiver taking effect "90 days after the date on which the President submits" to the Senate Foreign Relations Committee, the House Committee on Foreign Affairs, and the House and Senate Armed Services Committees "a report describing the manner in which such agreement would advance the national security and defense interests of the United States and not contribute to the proliferation of nuclear weapons." In the case of the proposed U.S.-Saudi agreement, President Trump submitted such a report to Congress in late November 2025.

On August 24, 2026, President Donald Trump submitted to Congress a U.S.-Saudi AEA Section 123 agreement, together with a Bilateral Safeguards Agreement. The Administration also submitted two side letters to the agreement to Congress; the letters have not been publicly released.

Based on the U.S.-Saudi agreement's receipt in Congress, the review periods for the U.S.-Saudi 123 nuclear agreement may conclude in December 2026. The Senate Parliamentarian provides authoritative guidance on the procedures used to review AEA Section 123 agreements, including on what day the 90-day congressional review period began. If the 123 agreement is not disapproved after the congressional review period, it may enter into force through an exchange of diplomatic notes between the two countries.

The Biden Administration discussed the prospect for civilian nuclear cooperation with Saudi Arabia in the context of U.S. efforts to promote diplomatic normalization between Saudi Arabia and Israel.66 Those discussions contributed to U.S.-Saudi mutual understandings on civil nuclear cooperation but did not result in a final agreement. President Trump has continued to promote Saudi-Israeli normalization, but reached agreement with Saudi Arabia on civil nuclear cooperation separately.

Saudi Arabia's Crown Prince said in 2025 that the kingdom wants to be a part of the Accords, but official Saudi statements continue to condition recognition of Israel on the establishment of an independent Palestinian state with East Jerusalem as its capital.68

The government of Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu has opposed the establishment of a Palestinian state, and leading Israeli opposition figure Gadi Eisenkot has pledged that no Palestinian state will arise under a government he forms.69

In a Senate Foreign Relations Committee confirmation hearing in November 2026, the U.S. Ambassador-designate to Saudi Arabia said the matter is subject to continuing negotiations.70

Issues for Congress

Nonproliferation

The NPAS notes that "Saudi Arabia signed and ratified the NPT as a non-nuclear weapon State Party in 1988 and has had a Comprehensive Safeguards Agreement (CSA) in force with the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) since 2009."71 While the NPT obligates non-nuclear weapon states not to acquire nuclear weapons, it also guarantees access to the peaceful use of nuclear technology under safeguards. The NPAS states that Saudi Arabia "lacks comprehensive export control and dual-use regulations." The agreement's letter of transmittal to Congress says that that "An addendum to the NPAS containing a comprehensive analysis of the export control system of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia with respect to nuclear-related matters, including interactions with countries of proliferation concern and the actual or suspected nuclear, dual-use, or missile-related transfers to such countries" was to be submitted to Congress separately by the Director of National Intelligence.

While Saudi policy maintains that Saudi nuclear energy pursuits are limited to peaceful purposes, Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman said in 2018 and 2023 that if Iran were to obtain a nuclear weapon, the kingdom would have to follow suit.72 The NPAS describes these statements as "concerning," but states that "the Kingdom has repeatedly reaffirmed its commitment to the peaceful uses of nuclear energy, the nuclear nonproliferation regime, and the NPT." The NPAS further argues, "The safeguards and other nonproliferation provisions contained in the proposed Agreement and the BSA would provide enhanced assurances that nuclear material, equipment, components, and technology supplied pursuant to the proposed Agreement will not contribute to nuclear weapons proliferation."

As with other Section 123 agreements, Article 11 of this agreement states that if either party does not comply with the agreement's nonproliferation provisions or violates their IAEA safeguards agreement, the other party has the right to cease further cooperation, terminate the agreement, and require the return of any material, nuclear material, equipment or components transferred or "any special fissionable material produced through the use of such items."

Some observers have highlighted the reported role that Saudi Arabia played from the 1970s onward in providing economic support to Pakistan that may have enabled its efforts to develop nuclear technology.73 Saudi Arabia and Pakistan signed a mutual defense agreement in 2025, and, in 2026, Saudi Arabia, Turkey, and Pakistan signed a trilateral mutual defense agreement. An unnamed Saudi official described the Saudi-Pakistan agreement as "a comprehensive defensive agreement that encompasses all military means."74 The agreements do not specifically address nuclear cooperation.

Some former U.S. officials have speculated that Saudi Arabia could seek access to nuclear technology or weaponry from Pakistan through undisclosed bilateral arrangements.75 Saudi officials have denied that such arrangements exist or are viable.76 One former Obama Administration official has said that during "many years of working in the U.S. government, I never saw any information or intelligence that confirmed the existence" of an agreement for Pakistan to supply Saudi Arabia with nuclear weapons or use nuclear weapons to protect Saudi Arabia.77

Some press articles have included reports that Saudi Arabia is building undeclared nuclear facilities. In 2020, Saudi authorities denied claims attributed to "Western officials" that Saudi Arabia had accepted China's help to build a facility for milling uranium ore.78 According to the NPAS, Saudi Arabia "declined to comment publicly on separate press reports alleging an 'undeclared nuclear site'" in the country.79

Some observers of the agreement have raised concerns that its terms may set precedents that other states may refer to in negotiation with the United States and lead to a weakening of U.S. nonproliferation policy.80 For example, some states may seek alternative safeguards arrangements rather than adhering to the IAEA's model Additional Protocol. These observers have argued that the BSA does not address undeclared sites in the country, though such sites would need to be declared pursuant to Saudi Arabia's CSA. Others have expressed concern over the enrichment study provisions, U.S. openness to possible follow-on agreed transfers, and whether those terms may lead other states to acquire enrichment capabilities and encourage other states to transfer enrichment technology.81

Export Controls and Supply Constraints

According to the NPAS, Saudi Arabia does not possess "any significant nuclear fuel cycle capabilities and will depend on the international market for nuclear fuel services for the near-term."82 Nuclear-related exports to Saudi Arabia, therefore, would likely be subject to various international requirements governing such exports. For example, Saudi Arabia's CSA requires IAEA safeguards on all legitimate transfers of nuclear technology; no country with comprehensive safeguards in place has used declared nuclear facilities to produce fissile material for weapons.83 Moreover, major suppliers of uranium and fuel cycle technology have in force IAEA safeguards agreements that obligate those suppliers to report nuclear-related exports to the IAEA.84 Some analysts have argued that "qualified suppliers" provisions of the proposed agreement as well as the associated consent rights would ensure nonproliferation commitments over the long term and would exclude suppliers that are "less committed to nonproliferation" as long as Saudi Arabia complies with the agreement.85 Others have argued that the lack of an Additional Protocol is "in violation of Nuclear Suppliers Group standards that the United States has championed."86

As noted above, the multilateral Nuclear Suppliers Group (NSG), in which the United States participates, prohibits transfers of enrichment and reprocessing technology unless the recipient government has brought into force an Additional Protocol or, "pending this, is implementing appropriate safeguards agreements in cooperation with the IAEA … as approved by the IAEA Board of Governors."87 In the absence of IAEA approval of appropriate safeguards under the proposed U.S.-Saudi Bilateral Safeguards Agreement or a similar agreement with another supplier, the NSG member states could enforce guidelines and voluntary restrictions on transfers of nuclear technology that would otherwise constrain Saudi Arabia's ability to obtain or develop enrichment and reprocessing facilities.88 Nuclear weapon states are subject to additional constraints by the NPT, Article I of which prohibits such states from assisting "in any way" any non-nuclear weapon state "to manufacture or otherwise acquire nuclear weapons."89

Security and Governance

Ongoing conflict in the Middle East region has demonstrated the vulnerability of Saudi critical infrastructure to attack by missiles and drones. The U.S. travel advisory for Saudi Arabia notes that "Iran and Iran-backed Houthi rebels have launched missile and drone attacks into Saudi Arabia" and states that terrorist attacks "can occur with little or no warning" against targets that may include government facilities and areas frequented by U.S. citizens.90 In May 2026, an attack on the United Arab Emirates reportedly damaged electrical equipment at the UAE's Barakah nuclear power plant; U.S. and South Korean-origin UAE missile defense systems contributed to the UAE's defenses.91

Saudi Arabia is a monarchy without a legislative body or other constitutionally established entities with budgetary or oversight authorities. National security and economic decisionmaking in Saudi Arabia are functions of the kingdom's executive leadership, as manifested in the cabinet and the Crown Prince in his roles as Prime Minister and Chairman of the Council of Political and Security Affairs, the Council of Economic and Development Affairs, and the Public Investment Fund, the kingdom's sovereign wealth fund. Policy decisions and the issuance of legal and regulatory mechanisms to implement them are functions of the Saudi executive. As noted above, succession in Saudi Arabia could occur at any time given the king's advanced age and health challenges. Members of Congress have taken divergent approaches to bilateral cooperation with Saudi Arabia, with some prioritizing sustaining cooperation and others taking a more conditional approach reflecting concerns about national security, human rights, or regional developments.

On a bilateral basis, the implementation of a civil nuclear cooperation agreement and the involvement of U.S. personnel in bilateral safeguards activities could extend the duration and expand the extent of U.S. official presence in Saudi Arabia, with attendant security and cost implications. As of October 2026, the U.S. State Department advised U.S. citizens to "Reconsider Travel" to Saudi Arabia due to "risk of Iranian drone and missile targeting of American interests, armed conflict, terrorism, exit bans, and local laws regarding social media activity."

Possible Regional and Diplomatic Implications

The development of civilian nuclear energy infrastructure has been a subject of broad interest in the Middle East region for decades, and U.S. decisionmakers have considered bilateral relationships, nonproliferation goals, and global strategic competition as factors in developing U.S. policy and priorities. In the wider Middle East and North Africa region, the United States has concluded 123 agreements with Morocco, Turkey, and the UAE.

It is possible that future discussions with regional partners over nuclear cooperation will be influenced by the content of the proposed Saudi agreement and that U.S. interlocutors may seek similar terms that they may deem favorable, including with regard to consideration of potential nuclear fuel development and the development and acceptance of bilateral rather than multilateral safeguards arrangements. The 2009 U.S.-UAE nuclear agreement provides the United States the right to terminate nuclear cooperation if the UAE "possesses sensitive nuclear facilities within its territory or otherwise engages in activities within its territory relating to enrichment of uranium or reprocessing of nuclear fuel." A bilateral Agreed Minute supplementing the 2009 agreement states that its terms "shall be no less favorable in scope and effect" than U.S. agreements with other countries in the Middle East and that the United States will, at the UAE's request, consult "regarding the possibility of amending" the agreement with equally favorable terms. The U.S.-UAE agreement and Agreed Minute do not have qualified supplier or U.S.-approved supplier provisions.

U.S. agreements with Saudi Arabia and other regional partners may influence the course of future discussions with Iran over its nuclear activities. Iranian officials, including officials of any successor government to the current Islamic Republic, could cite U.S. acceptance of uranium enrichment for fuel production by Iran's regional neighbors as a reason that Iran should not face efforts to force it to forgo such technologies and initiatives itself.

Outlook and Options

With the proposed nuclear agreement, bilateral safeguards agreement, and accompanying side letters before Congress for review, Members may consider the proposals for cooperation in light of U.S. national security interests, commercial opportunities, nonproliferation policies, and interbranch precedents. As proposed, the agreement could provide a basis for decades of close U.S.-Saudi nuclear cooperation, including with regard to reactor sales and operation, nuclear fuel production, training, and industrial applications. Absent congressional adoption of a joint resolution of disapproval, the executive branch could exchange diplomatic notes with Saudi Arabia, after which the agreement could enter into force. Near term steps in implementation could include an exchange of diplomatic notes and initial steps to support implementation of the bilateral safeguards agreement and joint study.

Should Congress choose to condition or reject the agreement, the executive branch and the Saudi government could take a number of different approaches, depending on the nature and content of legislative action. Under conditions in which Congress were to require certification of specific steps by Saudi Arabia—such as Saudi adoption of an Additional Protocol to its CSA—prior to the implementation of parts of the proposed agreement, the Saudi government could choose either to take the mandated steps or forgo the prospect of deeper nuclear cooperation with the United States. Rejection could lead Saudi Arabia to pursue closer cooperation with other suppliers and could have implications for other aspects of U.S.-Saudi relations. To the extent that other global exporters are willing and able to offer the kingdom satisfactory terms for cooperation and comparable technological cooperation, Saudi leaders may find non-U.S. alternatives compelling.

Under future circumstances in which Saudi Arabia's nuclear program were to become a subject of acute proliferation concern—whether under the proposed agreement or in its absence—the United States and other global actors could face challenges in using coercive economic policies to induce Saudi compliance with nonproliferation related requests. The United States and other actors have sought to use economic means, including sanctions, to respond to proliferation concerns in other contexts, including with regard to Iran. Isolating Saudi Arabia economically in the event that its nuclear program became a matter of proliferation concern could prove difficult for concerned parties, given the kingdom's central role in the world's oil market, its vast wealth, and its global investment posture. Similarly, the potential for the interruption of U.S.-Saudi security cooperation, including through training programs and the sale of U.S. defense articles and services, presumably would be weighed seriously by U.S. and Saudi officials when discussing potential issues of proliferation concern. Congress has conditioned aspects of U.S.-Saudi defense cooperation in the past, but has not acted to durably sever security ties. It is possible, though not certain, that the conclusion and implementation of the proposed agreement and bilateral safeguards measures would afford the United States increased insight into nuclear related developments in the kingdom that could limit the potential for proliferation concerns to emerge.

Additional Legislative Models and Oversight Options

Congress has several means with which to conduct oversight of the U.S.-Saudi 123 agreement's implementation, including but not limited to holding hearings and requiring reports from the executive branch, Congress could also examine adequacy of personnel and resources in support of the BSA and Joint Study. Congress could consider legislation requiring the executive branch to take certain actions before either issuing licenses for nuclear exports or following through with the necessary steps to bring the agreement into force. Legislation concerning U.S. nuclear cooperation agreements with China and India provide illustrative examples of this latter approach.

People's Republic of China (PRC)96

In 1985, President Ronald Reagan submitted the first U.S.-China nuclear cooperation agreement to Congress, which adopted a joint resolution, P.L. 99-183, requiring that the President make certain nonproliferation-related certifications, as well as issue a report "detailing the history and current developments of PRC "nonproliferation policies and practices." This resolution also prohibited the U.S. government from issuing licenses for certain nuclear-related exports to China until 30 days of continuous session of Congress had elapsed following presidential fulfillment of these requirements. The required certification was not submitted until 1998 (see below).

In the context of U.S. concern about the PRC government's actions in response to the Tiananmen Square demonstrations in 1989, the Foreign Relations Authorization Act for Fiscal Years 1990 and 1991 (P.L. 101-246) imposed a number of sanctions on China, including suspending nuclear cooperation under the 1985 agreement, as well as requiring an additional presidential certification that the PRC government had provided additional nonproliferation assurances. On January 12, 1998, President William Clinton signed the required certifications regarding China's nuclear nonproliferation policy and practices. President Clinton also issued a certification and waived a sanction imposed under P.L. 101-246.97 Congressional review ended on March 18, 1998.

India98

The Henry J. Hyde United States and India Nuclear Cooperation Promotion Act of 2006 (P.L. 109-401; hereinafter "Hyde Act"), which permitted the President to waive several AEA provisions with respect to the U.S. nuclear cooperation agreement with India, required that Congress enact a joint resolution of approval in order for the agreement to enter into force.99 President George W. Bush signed the United States-India Nuclear Cooperation Approval and Nonproliferation Enhancement Act (P.L. 110-369), which approved the agreement, into law on October 8, 2008.

The United States-India Nuclear Cooperation Approval and Nonproliferation Enhancement Act contains conditions for both the exchange of diplomatic notes and NRC issuance of export licenses. For example, this law conditioned the exchange of diplomatic notes with India, a necessary step for the agreement's entry into force, on two Presidential certifications. President Bush transmitted these certifications, along with a Memorandum of Justification, on October 20, 2008. The memorandum did not explain the reasoning underlying the determinations.

The same law also required that the President issue certain certifications to Congress before the NRC could issue export licenses. President Barack Obama submitted the required certification to Congress on February 3, 2010, determining that India has satisfied the legal requirement described above. The certification did not describe the reasoning underlying the determination. Both the Hyde Act and the United States-India Nuclear Cooperation Approval and Nonproliferation Enhancement Act also require executive branch reports about various aspects of the agreement.

Other Legislative Options

Congress could consider legislation requiring the President to submit for congressional review amendments or subsequent arrangements to the U.S.-Saudi agreement. As noted, the 123 agreement provides for amendments by mutual agreement. The AEA requires only that the President keep the Senate Foreign Relations and House Foreign Affairs Committees "fully and currently informed of any initiative or negotiations relating to" such an amendment. Congress could also specify procedures for considering subsequent arrangements to the 123 agreement. The AEA provides for congressional notification, but not review, of certain types of subsequent arrangements.100 The United States-India Nuclear Cooperation Approval and Nonproliferation Enhancement Act addressed this latter issue by specifying procedures for a particular subsequent arrangement, including provisions for Congress to review and adopt a joint resolution of disapproval.

Congress could consider legislation that curtails or waives the AEA-mandated congressional review period. For example, in 2016, the President submitted to Congress an extension of the U.S.-Norway nuclear cooperation agreement. To address concerns that an insufficient number of legislative days remained in the 114th Congress for congressional consideration, Congress adopted P.L. 114-320, which approved the agreement notwithstanding AEA review provisions and became law on December 16, 2016.101

Appendix A. Atomic Energy Act and Nuclear Cooperation Agreements102

Under the Atomic Energy Act (AEA) of 1954, as amended, all significant U.S. nondefense nuclear cooperation with other countries requires a nuclear cooperation agreement.103 These agreements authorize cooperation but do not guarantee that cooperation will take place. Currently, the United States has 26 Section 123 agreements in force.104 Significant nuclear cooperation includes the transfer of U.S.-origin special nuclear material subject to licensing for commercial, medical, and industrial purposes, and the export of reactors and critical parts of reactors.105 The NRC issues licenses for the export of nuclear items pursuant to Section 103 of the AEA.

Requirements106

Section 123 of the AEA requires that nuclear cooperation agreements include the terms, conditions, duration, nature, and scope of cooperation, as well as meet nine nonproliferation criteria. The President must submit any such agreement to the House Committee on Foreign Affairs and the Senate Committee on Foreign Relations. The Department of State is required to provide the President an unclassified NPAS, which the President is to submit, along with the agreement, to the committees of referral. The State Department also is required to provide a classified annex to the NPAS, prepared in consultation with the Director of National Intelligence. The NPAS is meant to explain how a proposed agreement would meet the aforementioned nonproliferation criteria. The President also must make a written determination "that the performance of the proposed agreement will promote and will not constitute an unreasonable risk to, the common defense and security."

Section 123a, paragraphs (1) through (9), lists nine criteria that an agreement with a non-nuclear weapon state must meet:107

  • safeguards on transferred nuclear material and equipment are to continue in perpetuity;
  • the IAEA applies comprehensive safeguards in NNWS;
  • nothing transferred is used for any nuclear explosive device or for any other military purpose;
  • the United States has the right to demand the return of transferred nuclear materials and equipment, as well as any special nuclear material produced through their use, if the cooperating state detonates a nuclear explosive device or terminates or abrogates an IAEA safeguards agreement;
  • the recipient state will not retransfer material or restricted data without U.S. consent, which can be in the form of subsequent arrangements. Such arrangements are required for additional forms of nuclear cooperation pursuant to an existing 123 agreement;108
  • the recipient state will maintain physical security on transferred nuclear material;
  • the recipient state will obtain approval before enriching or reprocessing transferred nuclear material or nuclear material produced with materials or facilities transferred pursuant to the agreement;
  • the recipient state will obtain U.S. advance approval for storage of transferred plutonium and HEU; and
  • any material or facilities produced or constructed through use of sensitive nuclear technology transferred under the cooperation agreement will be subject to all of the above requirements.

The AEA and the IAEA Additional Protocol

The AEA does not require a country to adopt an Additional Protocol to its IAEA comprehensive safeguards agreement in order to conclude a 123 agreement with the United States. However, except for Argentina and Brazil, all states with which the United States has a 123 agreement have concluded such a protocol.109

Enrichment and Reprocessing Provisions

As noted, enrichment and reprocessing facilities are a source of proliferation concern. The AEA mandates that 123 agreements require U.S. consent for any "alteration in form or content" (to include enrichment or reprocessing) of U.S.-origin material or any material processed in a facility containing transferred U.S. nuclear technology, including reactors. Section 123 agreements also require U.S. consent for any retransfer of material or technology.110 The AEA, however, does not require governments to forgo possession of enrichment and reprocessing facilities as a condition for concluding a 123 agreement.

U.S. policy has aimed to persuade certain countries with which it is negotiating nuclear cooperation agreements to forgo obtaining enrichment and reprocessing facilities.111 As noted, the 2008 MOU stated Saudi Arabia's intent to refrain from enrichment and reprocessing. The United States concluded similar MOUs that year with Bahrain, Jordan, and the UAE. However, subsequent 123 agreements' provisions concerning enrichment and reprocessing have varied.

The NSG guidelines for transfer of enrichment facilities, equipment, and technology state that suppliers should seek agreement from recipient states that an enrichment facility will not be used to produce enriched uranium containing more than 20% U-235 and should use a "black box" design when building the enrichment plant to prevent the recipient state from replicating the technology transferred.112 It also specifies that safety and regulatory information should be shared "to the extent necessary without divulging enabling technology."

The AEA mandates that U.S. nuclear cooperation agreements require U.S. consent for any "alteration in form or content" (to include enrichment or reprocessing) of U.S.-origin material or any material processed in a plant containing transferred U.S. nuclear technology. But the AEA does not require governments to forgo enrichment and reprocessing as a condition for concluding a nuclear cooperation agreement.

The Obama Administration characterized its December 2009 nuclear cooperation agreement with the UAE as a potential precedent for mitigating the danger of nuclear proliferation associated with enrichment and reprocessing.113 For example, President Obama's May 21, 2009, letter transmitting the agreement to Congress argued that the agreement had "the potential to serve as a model for other countries in the region that wish to pursue responsible nuclear energy development." Similarly, then-State Department spokesperson P. J. Crowley described the agreement as "the gold standard" during an August 5, 2010, press briefing, although the Obama Administration generally did not use this term when describing its nuclear cooperation policies.

The U.S.-UAE agreement states that the UAE

shall not possess sensitive nuclear facilities within its territory or otherwise engage in activities within its territory for, or relating to, the enrichment or reprocessing of material, or for the alteration in form or content (except by irradiation or further irradiation or, if agreed by the Parties, post-irradiation examination) of plutonium, uranium 233, high enriched uranium, or irradiated source or special fissionable material.

The U.S.-UAE agreement also provides the United States with the right to terminate nuclear cooperation and to require the return of any nuclear "material, equipment or components … and any special fissionable material produced through their use" if, after the agreement's entry into force, the UAE "possesses sensitive nuclear facilities within its territory or otherwise engages in activities within its territory relating to enrichment of uranium or reprocessing of nuclear fuel."114

Notwithstanding its characterization of the U.S.-UAE agreement, the Obama Administration announced in December 2013 that renouncing domestic enrichment and reprocessing would not be a prerequisite to concluding a nuclear cooperation agreement for all countries, and each partner country would be considered individually.115 For example, the U.S.-Vietnam nuclear cooperation agreement did not include an explicit renunciation of enrichment or reprocessing, but instead contained a political commitment in the preamble saying the country would rely on international markets for its nuclear fuel supply.116 Article 8 of the 2015 U.S.-South Korea 123 agreement said that the United States would "ensure a reliable supply of low enriched uranium … for processing into nuclear fuel for use in the Republic of Korea."117 That agreement also created a Joint Fuel Cycle Study to examine the feasibility of enrichment and reprocessing in the future.

An Agreed Minute to the U.S.-UAE agreement includes a provision which establishes its conditions as minimum standards for future such U.S. agreements in the Middle East. Stating that "the fields of cooperation, terms and conditions" accorded by the U.S.-UAE agreement "shall be no less favorable in scope and effect than those which may be accorded, from time to time, to any other non-nuclear-weapon State in the Middle East in a peaceful nuclear cooperation agreement," the Minute explains that, in the event that Washington concludes a more-favorable agreement with another regional government, the United States will, at the UAE's request, consult with the UAE "regarding the possibility of amending" the agreement in order to make its terms equally favorable to the new agreement.

Appendix B. Comprehensive Safeguards Agreements and Additional Protocols

Comprehensive Safeguards Agreements

The Nonproliferation Treaty (NPT) requires non-nuclear-weapon states-parties (NNWS) to conclude Comprehensive Safeguards Agreements (CSAs) with the IAEA.118 Such agreements, which apply safeguards "on all nuclear material in all nuclear activities in a State," are intended to enable the agency to detect the diversion of nuclear material from peaceful purposes to nuclear weapons uses, as well as to detect undeclared nuclear activities and material. Safeguards include agency inspections and monitoring of declared nuclear facilities. Although all nuclear material in an NNWS is subject to IAEA safeguards, CSAs, as a practical matter, limit the agency's monitoring and inspections authority in a particular country to facilities that have been declared by the government. CSAs authorize the IAEA to conduct special inspections.119

Although CSAs give the IAEA the authority "to verify the absence of undeclared nuclear material and activities, the tools available to it to do so, under such agreements, are limited," according to the agency.120 As a practical matter, the IAEA's ability to inspect and monitor nuclear facilities, as well as obtain related information pursuant to a particular country's comprehensive safeguards agreement, is limited to facilities and activities that have been declared by the government.

Additional Protocols121

In 1991, IAEA inspectors revealed that Iraq, despite having a CSA in force, had concealed a nuclear weapons program by conducting activities in undeclared locations. As part of a larger effort to improve the agency's ability to detect of undeclared nuclear activities, the IAEA Board of Governors in 1997 adopted the model Additional Protocol (AP) to CSAs.122 APs, which are voluntary, augment the IAEA's ability to detect clandestine nuclear facilities and activities by providing the IAEA with additional authority to inspect certain nuclear-related facilities and demand information from member states. The Additional Protocol also allows for "complementary access" by inspectors to sites on short notice.123  

The multilateral Nuclear Suppliers Group (NSG) export guidelines, which the United States has endorsed as an NSG participant, prohibit transfers of enrichment and reprocessing technology unless the recipient government has brought into force an Additional Protocol or, "pending this, is implementing appropriate safeguards agreements in cooperation with the IAEA … as approved by the IAEA Board of Governors." 124

Information

The AP requires NNWS to provide certain types of information concerning nuclear-related activities that do not involve the use of nuclear material. CSAs already cover such activities that use nuclear material. The information most relevant to producing nuclear weapons falls into several categories:

  • A "general description of and information specifying the location of nuclear fuel cycle-related research and development activities not involving nuclear material." These activities include those "specifically related to any process or system development aspect" of uranium enrichment and spent fuel reprocessing.
  • A "description of the scale of operations for each location engaged in the activities specified" in the AP's Annex I, which covers a variety of activities related to enrichment, reprocessing, and nuclear reactors.
  • Information "specifying the location, operational status and the estimated annual production capacity of uranium mines and [uranium] concentration plants." Uranium ore concentrate is a precursor of enriched uranium.
  • Information about imported and exported nuclear material which has not "reached the composition and purity" necessary for the IAEA application of safeguards pursuant to a CSA.
  • Information about imports and exports of "equipment and nonnuclear material" specified in the AP's Annex II, some of which are related to enrichment and reprocessing.

Access

The AP requires that NNWS provide the IAEA with access to any "location specified" by the agency. Depending on the type of facility, the IAEA may request access in order to "assure the absence of undeclared nuclear material and activities" in the facility or to "resolve a question relating to the correctness and completeness" of the information that a state has provided to the IAEA. The methods and activities (e.g., environmental sampling, radiation detection) that IAEA inspectors may undertake when accessing such a location vary depending on the facility's type.

The AP allows agency access to:

  • uranium mines and uranium ore processing facilities located at the mines;   
  • locations around and including facilities with nuclear material that a state has declared under its safeguards agreement; 
  • locations of nuclear fuel cycle-related research and development activities not involving nuclear material; and
  • locations engaged in activities specified in Annex I of the Model AP. These activities include the manufacture of some components related to enrichment, reprocessing, and nuclear reactors.
