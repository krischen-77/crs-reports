Forecasting Tropical Cyclones
Updated November 17, 2026 (IF10719)

Timely and accurate forecasts of tropical cyclones, including tropical depressions, tropical storms, and hurricanes (hereinafter TCs or storms) can provide life- and property-saving warnings to parts of the United States vulnerable to a storm's impacts. The National Hurricane Center and Central Pacific Hurricane Center (NHC), part of the National Weather Service (NWS) within the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), is responsible for forecasting TCs in the Northern Atlantic Ocean and the Central and Eastern Pacific Ocean. (Other entities are responsible for these storms in other parts of the world.)

Tropical Cyclone Classifications

Tropical cyclones are categorized based on their wind speeds. Classifications include the following:

  • Tropical depressions—maximum sustained winds of ≤ 38 miles per hour (mph).
  • Tropical storms—maximum sustained winds of 39-73 mph. NOAA typically names a storm once it reaches this strength.
  • Hurricanes—maximum sustained winds of 74-95 mph (category 1 on the Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale) and 96-110 mph (category 2). Hurricanes also may be called typhoons or cyclones.
  • Major hurricanes—maximum sustained winds of ≥111 mph, corresponding to a category 3, 4, or 5 hurricane.

NOAA's tropical cyclone forecast uses a broad array of resources and capabilities across the agency, coordinated and interpreted by NHC. NHC estimates a tropical cyclone's track (i.e., the storm's path), intensity (i.e., its wind speed), extent, and atmospheric conditions, and works with other parts of NOAA to predict associated hazards, such as storm surge, rainfall, and tornadoes. Other NOAA entities with TC-related roles include other parts of NWS, the National Environmental Satellite, Data, and Information Service (NESDIS), the National Ocean Service (NOS), the Office of Marine and Aviation Operations (OMAO), the and Office of Oceanic and Atmospheric Research (OAR).

This In Focus describes NHC TC-related activities, from storm formation through forecasts and warnings. It also sets out ongoing TC forecasting challenges that Congress may consider via oversight or legislative actions.

Storm Observations

The forecasting process begins with observations of rainfall, wind speed, and other meteorological and oceanographic conditions. Satellites, aircraft, ships, buoys, radar, and other sources collect data. NOAA weather satellites, such as NESDIS's Geostationary Operational Environmental Satellites and Joint Polar Satellite System, provide the primary remote-sensing observations during the early stages of storm development.

NOAA scientists use a range of instruments to gather data from inside tropical cyclones. For example, NOAA OMAO and U.S. Air Force aircraft (often referred to as Hurricane Hunters) may fly into a storm to collect real-time data. Uncrewed surface and air vehicles, moored and drifting buoys, and floats and gliders managed by multiple line offices collect additional information. Close to shore, storms are observed by land-based radars, ground-based observation systems, and water level observing instruments.

Analyzing the Data

NHC gathers all the observational data to generate numerical weather prediction models. The models use the data to illustrate current atmospheric and oceanic conditions and then use mathematical equations to produce TC track, intensity, and other storm-related hazard forecasts. Not all numerical weather prediction models are the same; they may differ in which equations are used and how the solutions to the equations are used to make forecasts, for example. These differences explain why NHC forecasts may differ from those of other countries or institutions that also produce forecasts (e.g., the European Center for Medium-Range Forecasts).

Forecasts, Watches, and Warnings

Using model outputs and their understanding of the meteorological and oceanographic data, NHC forecasters generate text and graphical products on storm conditions and hazards for emergency managers and the public. These include outlooks covering the next 120 hours (five days), watches and warnings, discussions, and updates. These products note storm location, motion, pressure, and wind speeds; contain information about hazards to land (i.e., storm surge, surf, wind, rainfall, tornadoes, and inland hazards); and indicate the next expected advisory. NHC updates the products on a set schedule throughout a storm's lifetime, and more frequently as appropriate. NHC forecasts, watches, and warnings are coordinated with NWS local Weather Forecast Offices that may be affected by the storm. These offices consider local conditions and release site-specific forecasts.

Tropical Cyclone-Related Watches and Warnings

NWS may issue tropical cyclone-related watches and warnings depending on a tropical cyclone's characteristics.

  • Watches for storm surge and hurricane or tropical storm-force winds. For example, a hurricane watch is an announcement that hurricane conditions (sustained wind speeds of 74 mph or higher) are possible within a specified area, issued 48 hours in advance of the onset of tropical storm-force winds.
  • Warnings for storm surge, hurricane- or tropical storm-force winds, and extreme wind. For example, a hurricane warning is issued when hurricane conditions are expected somewhere within the specified area, issued 36 hours in advance of tropical storm-force winds.

NWS may release other storm-related warnings and watches for hazards such as flooding and tornadoes.

Storm Surge

Forecasts also provide information about potential storm surge in coastal areas. NOAA defines storm surge as an abnormal rise in sea level accompanying a TC or other intense storm, above the predicted astronomical tide (Figure 1). Storm surge is primarily caused by storm winds pushing waters toward the shore. The amount of surge is determined by the storm's intensity, size, forward speed, and angle of approach to the coast; the shape of the coastline; the slope of the ocean bottom; and the incidence of local features, such as bays and rivers. NHC issues storm surge watches or warnings along the U.S. Atlantic and Caribbean coasts.

Rainfall

The NWS Weather Prediction Center provides rainfall forecasts associated with TCs as appropriate. These forecasts note geographic areas at greatest risk, including inland areas, and expected amounts. Storm surge, especially when combined with large amounts of rainfall from a TC, can create dangerous flood conditions for coastal communities.

Working with Other Stakeholders

The Federal Emergency Management Agency's (FEMA's) Hurricane Liaison Team embeds with NWS forecasters at NHC. The team coordinates federal, state, and local emergency managers; FEMA personnel; and NOAA forecasters and hydrologists. FEMA typically activates the team for the duration of the hurricane season, which, depending on the ocean region, runs May 15 (Pacific) or June 1 (Atlantic) through November 30.

In addition to working with local, state, and federal stakeholders, NWS provides briefings on storms to the public and cooperates with meteorological services in other countries (e.g., Mexico and other Central American and Caribbean countries). Neither the Hurricane Liaison Team nor NWS issues evacuations; those decisions are left to state or local officials.

Challenges

Some entities, such as the Department of Commerce Office of Inspector General (DOC OIG) and NOAA's advisory Environmental Information Services Working Group (EISWG), have recommended ways to improve TC intensity and storm-associated rainfall forecasting, and communication with the public, among other actions. Congress may consider conducting oversight of NOAA's response to the recommendations and the agency's ongoing implementation of the broad Weather Research and Forecasting Innovation Act of 2017 (P.L. 115-25), or direct NOAA to address these topics in additional ways.

Intensity

Scientists have found that, while storm track forecasts have improved steadily since the 1960s, improvements to intensity forecasts have not occurred at the same pace. Additionally, understanding how and why a storm may experience rapid intensification (RI), where its wind speed increases at least 35 mph in 24 hours (e.g., Hurricane Polo in 2026), remains a challenge. Storms that undergo RI close to shore may be especially dangerous if forecasts do not predict the possibility of increased storm intensity in time for coastal residents to prepare. In 2020, EISWG recommended several actions that NOAA could take to improve intensity change prediction, from observations to modeling. NOAA communicated its ongoing efforts to improve forecasting in response to the recommendations in 2022, and EISWG noted the agency's continued related activities in 2026.

Rainfall

Forecasting storm precipitation continues to be a challenge. According to NOAA, flooding resulting from extreme precipitation during TCs is responsible for a significant portion of fatalities caused by a storm. The amount of rainfall produced by a storm, however, may not necessarily be related to its intensity. In 2024, researchers at NOAA and other institutions identified ways to improve TC precipitation predictions across multiple NOAA models. According to NOAA-CRS communication, research to implement improvements is ongoing, including as part of NOAA's Precipitation Prediction Grand Challenge.

Communication

Storm-related fatalities continue to occur in the United States, even as storm forecasts improve, in part because of how forecasts, watches, and warnings are communicated to the public. For example, a storm's Saffir-Simpson scale rating (e.g., Category 1) does not reflect the full suite of potential hazards, such as expected storm surge or precipitation-caused flooding. Several of EISWG's 2020 recommendations focused on improving communication; in 2022 NOAA indicated that its budget constrained implementation of recommendations. In 2025, DOC OIG recommended additional actions for NWS to take to improve risk communication and public response; NOAA concurred with the recommendations and described the actions it had taken or planned to take.
