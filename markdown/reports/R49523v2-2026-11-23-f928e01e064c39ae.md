The conflict in Ukraine has challenged assumptions, along with technology and doctrine
development, relating to Army Aviation. The Biden Administration and Trump Administration
have undertaken initiatives to adapt legacy aviation platforms and their employment on the
battlefield. These actions have included the deactivation of some Army Aviation units, particularly in the USAR; the
divestment of aircraft; and the elimination of aviation positions across all components.

In 2024, the Army instituted what it called the Aviation Investment Rebalance, which discontinued the Army’s development
of the crewed Future Attack and Reconnaissance Aircraft (FARA) helicopter while maintaining its investment in the crewed
tilt-rotor Future Long-Range Assault Aircraft (FLRAA) and making new investments in UH-60 Black Hawk and CH-47F
Block II Chinook helicopters. The Army also phased out operations of systems it deemed were not capable or survivable on
the battlefield, including the RQ-7 Shadow and RQ-11 Raven UAS.

In 2025, at the direction of the Secretary of Defense (who is using “Secretary of War” as a “secondary title” under Executive
Order 14347, dated September 5, 2025), the Army instituted the Army Transformation Initiative (ATI) to reorganize the
Army’s overall force structure, organization, and weapon systems. In terms of Army Aviation, the ATI has included

    •   developing the FLRAA;
    •   restructuring Army Aviation by reducing one Air Cavalry Squadron of 24 AH-64 Apache helicopters per
       Combat Aviation Brigade (CAB) in the AC;
    •   divesting two Army Reserve Expeditionary CABs, eliminating more than 3,000 positions and 180 UH-60
        Black Hawk helicopters, and transferring modernized UH-60Ms to the ARNG;
    •   canceling the procurement of what the Army described as “outdated” crewed attack aircraft, such as the
      AH-64D Apache variant, and “obsolete” UAS, such as the Gray Eagle; and
    •   planning to divest more than 300 AH-64 Apache and UH-60 Black Hawk helicopters by FY2027.
The Army may continue to revise its planned changes to Army Aviation’s force structure and equipment. The Army’s
restructuring efforts raise potential oversight issues for Congress, including the following:

    •   the impact of incorporating UAS into Army Aviation;
    •   the ATI restructuring’s impact on RC aviation;
    •   the industrial base effects of changing Army helicopter and UAS requirements;
    •   the reduction in requirements for pilots for crewed platforms;
    •   the evolving threats and the future of Army aviation; and
    •   assessments of impacts to maintenance and readiness.

Introduction: Congress and Army Aviation

Congress authorizes and appropriates funds for the U.S. Army on an annual basis and sets end
strengths that limit the size of the respective forces. Together with the executive branch, Congress
ensures the Army is equipped and supplied, determines pay and benefits, establishes personnel
policies, and exercises oversight of its management. If Congress chooses, it can also allocate roles
and missions for the Army, including the Reserve Component (RC); determine training
requirements; establish or disestablish units; specify unit composition; and designate the number
and types of units within each component. Army restructuring initiatives since 2024, including
the Aviation Investment Rebalance and the Army Transformation Initiative (ATI), have proposed
changing Army Aviation force structure, aircraft modernization priorities, and soldier and
workforce requirements. In its budget request for FY2027, the Army proposed scaling back
previously planned procurement of crewed helicopter programs—the UH-60 Black Hawk and
CH-47 Chinook.1 These changes may present legislative and oversight considerations for
Congress, potentially including the following questions:
    •  What information is Congress receiving from the Army about its plans to change
      Army Aviation force structure and investments? Is the information adequate to
        enable effective legislative decisionmaking?
    •  What are the implications of the Army’s proposed reduction in the procurement
        of crewed Army Aviation helicopters to accelerate the development of the MV-75
       Cheyenne II tilt-rotor aircraft? What opportunities and risks might arise from
       speeding development of emerging technologies to support Army aviation needs?
    •  Does the proposed restructuring of Army Aviation optimize the branch for the
        future? How might cuts to aviation units in the RC affect the capacity and
        effectiveness of the Aviation branch or that of other Army components?
    •  To what extent has the Army relied on lessons learned from Ukraine, Iran,
        Venezuela, and other armed conflicts to inform its plans to restructure Army
        Aviation? What other scenarios might inform the future of Army Aviation? Has
        the Army communicated to Congress a vision for the role the Aviation branch
       would play in future conflicts?
    •  To what extent should the Army Aviation branch be responsible for managing the
        integration of small uncrewed aircraft systems (UAS) into the Army, or should it
        share that responsibility with other branches? What roles and responsibilities
       should UAS have in fulfilling the Army Aviation missions?

Background

The Mission of Army Aviation
The Army describes the mission of Army Aviation, 1 of 20 basic branches of the Army, as2

         to find, fix, and destroy any enemy through fire and maneuver and to provide combat
        support and combat service support in coordinated operations as an integral member of the
       combined arms team.3

Army Aviation’s seven core competencies are as follows:

        Provide accurate and timely information collection.

        Provide early warning, reaction time, and maneuver space.

        Destroy, dislocate, disintegrate, or isolate enemy forces.

        Air assault [operations].

        Air movement [i.e., transport of personnel, supplies, equipment].

         Aerial evacuation.

        Enable command and control.4

               A Brief History of Army Aviation
 The history of Army aviation dates to the Union Army’s brief use of air balloons for reconnaissance during the
 American Civil War.5 The Army Signal Corps acquired the nation’s first military aircraft from the Wright Brothers
  in 1909.6 Congress established the Aviation Section as a subordinate organization of the Signal Corps in 1914.7 In
  1920, Congress established the Army Air Service (later, the Army Air Corps) as a separate arm of the Army.8
  In 1941, the War Department established the Army Air Forces (AAF). In 1942, the department separated the AAF
 and Army Ground Forces into coequal commands,9 and the department approved the formation of “organic air
  observation units” in the Army Ground Forces.10 During World War II, these units were equipped with light,
  fixed-wing aircraft for air observation, liaison, and target acquisition, supplementing the ground support provided
 by the AAF. In the National Security Act of 1947, Congress established the Department of the Air Force and
  transferred the AAF to the Air Force, among other changes to the organization of national defense.11

 The Army retained a role for aviation under the National Security Act of 1947, which stated that the Army would
  include “such aviation and water transport as may be organic therein.”12 Prior the Korean War, the Army’s
  aircraft inventory comprised mostly of light aircraft allocated to Army ground forces in World War II for air
  observation and liaison.13 During the Korean War and the Vietnam War, rotary-wing aircraft, which had made
  their operational debut in the U.S. military in World War II, assumed greater prominence in the Army.14 By 1970,
  the Army’s fleet numbered more than 12,000 aircraft.15
  In 1983, using authority provided by Congress in Title 10, Section 7063(a)(13), of the U.S. Code, the Army
  established Army Aviation as a basic branch.16 The organization of Army Aviation units and the treatment of
  aviation forces in Army doctrine evolved throughout the 1980s.17 Under the Army 86 and Army of Excellence
  initiatives, the Army formed aviation brigades—forerunners to the combat aviation brigade (CAB)—composed of
  aviation battalions and companies tailored to specific missions (e.g., air assault, attack, and reconnaissance).18
 Under the Army’s Modular Force initiative of the early 2000s, the Army standardized aviation brigades by creating
  heavy, medium, and light designs, each comprised of a corresponding number of battalions and companies, aircraft,
 and personnel.19 In 2014, the Army developed the Aviation Restructuring Initiative (ARI), in which it proposed
  reducing the size of the aviation force, retiring older aircraft, and realigning attack helicopters from the Army
  National Guard to the Active Component.20 The ARI resulted in the Army’s divesture of the OH-58 Kiowa scout
  helicopter; other elements of the plan met opposition in Congress and from the National Guard Bureau.21

Army Aviation Organization

Army Aviation is organized across the Active Component (AC) and RC—the Army National
Guard (ARNG) and Army Reserve (USAR)—with each component contributing distinct aviation
capabilities.23 Collectively, Army Aviation provides attack, assault, reconnaissance, medical
evacuation (MEDEVAC), command and control, and sustainment aviation in support of Army
and joint operations. Although aviation units across components operate similar aircraft, they
differ in mission focus, readiness posture, and integration into the Army’s operational plans.

Historically, the Army maintained a broad aviation force structure to support rotational
deployments, facilitate enhanced counterinsurgency operations, and meet global response
requirements. The Army, citing rising sustainment costs, aging aircraft fleets, and the increasing
threat posed by modern air defenses and UAS, has proposed changes to the organization of the
reserve and active components Army Aviation branch.24 (For more information, see “Army
Aviation Restructuring ” section.)

Active Component (AC)

The AC provides the majority of the Army’s deployable aviation combat power. AC aviation units
are organized primarily into combat aviation brigades (CABs), which are designed to support
division- and corps-level operations.25 Since the early 2000s, CABs have included a mix of
Attack Battalions, Assault Helicopter Battalions, Air Cavalry Squadrons, General Support
Aviation Battalions, and Aviation Support Battalions, as well as a Gray Eagle UAS company and
a headquarters company.

At present, Attack Battalions and Air Cavalry Squadrons carry out attack and reconnaissance
missions primarily with AH-64 Apache attack helicopters. Assault Helicopter Battalions conduct
air assault and air movement, among other tasks, with UH-60 Black Hawk utility helicopters.
General Support Aviation Brigades provide command and control, heavy-lift, and MEDEVAC
support with a mix of UH-60s, CH-47 Chinooks, and HH-60 Black Hawk MEDEVAC
helicopters. Aviation Support Battalions provide ground network, maintenance, and field
support.26

Reserve Component (RC)

The RC has historically fulfilled different aviation roles across its two subcomponents. The
ARNG, which operates AH-64 Apaches, UH-60 Black Hawks, HH-60 MEDEVAC aircraft, and
CH-47 Chinooks, provides a substantial portion of the Army’s assault, MEDEVAC, and heavy-lift
aviation capacity (see Appendix A). Guard aviation units routinely support domestic emergency
response missions under state authority and deploy overseas in support of federal missions. Until
the Army restructuring initiative of 2025, the USAR focused on aviation sustainment and
enabling formations, providing depth and support rather than frontline aviation combat power.

Army Aviation Systems

Army Aviation units have consisted primarily of helicopters (i.e., rotary-wing aircraft, or
rotorcraft) with limited UAS (sometimes called drones). The Army is integrating additional
modern UAS into Army Aviation units and non-aviation formations. The following sections
describe both crewed and uncrewed aviation systems.

Impact of Incorporating UAS into Army Aviation

Under the Aviation Investment Rebalance and ATI, the Army announced plans to introduce
growing numbers of UAS and to shift some Aviation branch missions from crewed to uncrewed

        of missions or from operating in certain environments in the future? What new
       missions might this change enable?
    •  What is the Army’s strategy for UAS? How could the Army incorporate UAS
        into the structure of Aviation units?
    •  What are the technical risks associated with the current UAS programs? What
        opportunities does the change represent?
    •  What potential alternative approaches has the Army considered relative to those
        of the current UAS programs?

Congress may consider questions relating to the cost and structure of Army aviation UAS

consider whether or not the Aviation branch is structured with adequate company and battalion
staff to plan and execute small UAS operations and the extent to which the costs of acquiring and

Industrial Base Effects of Changing Army Helicopter and

Following the ATI, the Army’s FY2027 budget request proposed curtailing the procurement of
legacy crewed helicopters. Reducing procurement of the Army’s major helicopter programs could
limit the parts supply chain for the older variants—the AH-64D and the UH-60L. Some say the

64D or the UH-60M.134 One Member expressed concern that international partners may be
disincentivized from procuring these aircraft if they conclude the United States is unlikely to
continue procuring them.135

Another potential effect is on the industrial workforce. GAO noted in a 2025 report that ARNG
maintainer shortages have affected availability of the Army’s CH-47F Chinook.136 While it is
unclear whether or not changes in Army Aviation force structure would alleviate or exacerbate
workforce shortages, Members may or may not seek further information about impacts to
changing helicopter requirements to the legacy industrial base.

Reduction in Requirements for Pilots and Aircraft Maintainers for
Crewed Platforms

Reportedly, in the AC alone, the Army plans to eliminate 6,500 aviation positions, many of which
are pilot positions.138 An Army spokesman reportedly stated that “the move aims to realign the
force toward the use of unmanned drones instead of manned aviation platforms and will occur
over fiscal years 2026 and 2027.”139 Some note that rather than retraining to work on drones,
pilots and aircraft maintainers could leave the Army for jobs in the civilian sector.140 Others note
that the Army reduced the MEDEVAC community in 2004 and reversed course five years later.141
In light of the special requirements and training required for Army pilots and aircraft maintainers,
Members could examine the impact of reductions in pilot and maintainer requirements.

Evolving Threats and the Future of Army Aviation

The Russia-Ukraine war has played a central role in the Army’s thinking about the future of
warfare and the Army itself, with some suggesting that the conflict has “exposed both the
strengths and vulnerabilities of helicopters in modern warfare.”142 Reportedly, “heavy helicopter
losses among Russian forces in Ukraine led top U.S. Army officials to question the survivability
of manned rotorcraft in future conflicts,” leading to an emphasis on UAS.143 One analyst observes
that helicopters

       which fly low and relatively slow, are easy targets for missiles and drones. U.S. leaders are
        watching closely and realizing that in a future war against a strong enemy, their helicopters
        might not survive long. At the same time, unmanned drones are proving they can perform
      many of the jobs once handled only by helicopters—scouting, striking targets, and even
        resupplying troops—without risking pilots.144

Both manned and unmanned Army Aviation platforms face a variety of threats, including
    •   first-person-view (FPV) drones, loitering munitions, and low-cost drone swarms
         that can be used to intercept and destroy helicopters in midair, particularly
        vulnerable troop-carrying helicopters;
    •   ground-based air defense systems, such as man-portable air-defense systems
     (MANPADS) as well as a wide variety of ground-based, anti-aircraft guns;
