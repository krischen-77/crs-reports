Economic Development Revolving Loan Funds (ED-RLFs)

Updated November 3, 2026
(IF11449)

Revolving loan funds (RLFs) are financial instruments frequently used to finance [water](https://crs.gov/Reports/IN12524), [wastewater](https://crs.gov/Reports/IN12524), infrastructure, [brownfields redevelopment](https://crs.gov/Reports/R49055?source=search&index=0&searchGUID=d3f5138a83d24b81a36ef45c0f4a6c1c#_Toc236389379), [disaster recovery,](https://www.fema.gov/grants/mitigation/learn/storm-rlf) and community development activities. RLFs can also be used for economic development purposes. Federally funded economic development RLFs (ED-RLFs) are one of many tools that public agencies and nonprofit organizations may use to make loans to finance small business growth, deploy capital to underserved markets, and incentivize development activity. The main advantage of using an RLF is that the RLF can be configured in a way to be "self-replenishing," thereby reducing the need for annual appropriations or federal credit subsidies. This In Focus provides an overview of how ED-RLFs work, a summary of federal support for ED-RLFs, and a brief review of current issues.

How Does an ED-RLF Work?

Initial funding for the capital base and administrative expenses of an RLF may come from a variety of sources, including government agencies, foundations, or private financial markets. Federal programs may support ED-RLFs by providing grants or loans to capitalize the fund. For instance, the U.S. Economic Development Administration ([EDA](https://www.eda.gov/funding/programs/revolving-loan-fund)) and the [Appalachian Regional Commission](https://www.arc.gov/access-to-capital-projects/) award grants through existing programs with specific guidelines for the use of funds as ED-RLFs. The U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) uses both grant and loan authorities to support ED-RLFs. RLF borrowers typically include for-profit businesses, nonprofit organizations, and government entities. The repayment of principal, interest, and fees can replenish RLFs so that future loans can be made to eligible borrowers and the loan fund can eventually be sustained or "revolved" without the need for continuing subsidies.

ED-RLFs are often designed to target businesses in specific industries, geographic areas, or distressed communities, or to target the unmet needs of borrowers for products and services such as microloans or gap financing. USDA funding, for example, may be used for targeted lending activities (e.g., for microloans) or for specific types of rural borrowers (e.g., to local utility organizations). ED-RLFs may also be used to finance development initiatives, such as infrastructure and revitalization projects sponsored by local government or other entities.

Many ED-RLFs report that they create some form of direct or indirect economic benefit to the region served. Directly, the ED-RLFs may increase the number of jobs created or retained, businesses expanded, or business startups created. Indirectly, ED-RLFs that finance public infrastructure or community development projects facilitate growth by supporting entrepreneurs, encouraging investment, and improving the conditions that expand facilities or amenities. The purpose of ED-RLFs typically is to supplement—not supplant—conventional lending activity where business and development credit needs are not fully addressed. ED-RLF programs can provide credit to businesses and markets that may be underserved by commercial lenders, including small or nontraditional businesses as well as businesses owned by socially or economically disadvantaged individuals. Participating in an ED-RLF program may help small or new businesses build credit and receive additional services, like technical assistance or business coaching. Some ED-RLFs serve businesses that need gap financing or microloans that may be otherwise unavailable from traditional lenders.

Issues and Considerations Related to RLFs

There are limitations and challenges associated with the creation, expansion, and maintenance of RLFs. When capitalized by grants or low-cost loans, [ED-RLFs](https://sk.sagepub.com/book/mono/economic-development-finance/chpt/revolving-loan-funds) may [receive lower financial returns](https://sk.sagepub.com/book/mono/economic-development-finance/chpt/revolving-loan-funds) in exchange for social or economic development outcomes. ED-RLF administrators must balance the overall demand for services, limited administrative resources, and the need to meet social or development goals while also assessing and managing risk. Loan losses are a risk to ED-RLFs and may decrease the capital base over time. Interest rates and fees have to be sufficient to cover potential loan losses, expenses, and inflation. Additionally, ED-RLFs that receive federal support may face [constraints](https://www.urban.org/sites/default/files/2026-06/Financing%20Small%20Business%20Growth%20with%20Economic%20Development%20Administration%20Revolving%20Loan%20Funds_0.pdf#page=45) related to program regulations, including thresholds for interest rates, loan disbursements, and other requirements.

Federally supported ED-RLFs may need to raise additional capital from other government and private sources to sustain their programs or to meet nonfederal cost share requirements. High-performing ED-RLFs may need additional funds to recapitalize the fund when an ED-RLF has an active base of borrowers. The demand for ED-RLF loans can be episodic and depend on economic factors that are outside the control of ED-RLF administrators at the local, state, and regional level. Additionally, a [2026 evaluation](https://www.urban.org/sites/default/files/2026-06/Financing%20Small%20Business%20Growth%20with%20Economic%20Development%20Administration%20Revolving%20Loan%20Funds_0.pdf#page=45) of EDA's RLF program noted that new financial products have created more options for business loans in recent years, which could mean that ED-RLFs may face competition for borrowers.

Both borrowers and institutional intermediaries may need technical assistance to establish an RLF and to ensure the fund becomes sustainable. Thus, loans from an ED-RLF might need to be paired with technical assistance to borrowers (often at an additional cost to the funder, lender, or borrower) to facilitate businesses' financial sustainability and to protect against loan defaults.

Federal Programs That Assist ED-RLFs

**Table 1**provides a summary of selected federal programs that support ED-RLFs. The terms, eligibility requirements, and other conditions of federally supported ED-RLFs vary based on the agency's program objectives, regulations, and statutory requirements. For instance, some ED-RLFs are administered by the federal agency directly; others are administered by an intermediary such as a local government, Indian tribe, nonprofit, utility, university, or economic development agency, including [economic development districts](https://www.eda.gov/about/economic-development-glossary/edd) and [councils of governments](https://narc.org/about/what-is-a-cog-or-mpo/). The ED-RLFs described in **Table 1**often complement other forms of business capital provided by financial institutions, the [Small Business Administration](https://www.crs.gov/Reports/RL33243), and Department of the [Treasury](https://www.crs.gov/Reports/R47169) programs, such as the [State Small Business Credit Initiative](https://crs.gov/Reports/R42581) (SSBCI) program and the Community Development Financial Institutions (CDFI) Fund's [Financial Assistance awards program](https://www.cdfifund.gov/programs-training/programs/cdfi-program). These loan, loan guarantee, and other programs offer assistance to businesses—usually through intermediaries such as community banks, [CDFIs](https://www.cdfifund.gov/programs-training/certification/cdfi), and state economic development agencies and not included in this summary. For more information, see CRS In Focus IF12449, [*Connecting Constituents with Federal Assistance for Businesses*](http://www.crs.gov/Reports/IF12449).
