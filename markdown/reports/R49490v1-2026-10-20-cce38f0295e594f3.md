As automated tools for decisionmaking, including those using artificial intelligence (AI), have
grown in popularity and sophistication, they raise questions about how their use intersects with       Abigail A. Graber
federal antidiscrimination laws. Federal laws address various kinds of discrimination, including       Legislative Attorney
disparate treatment and disparate impact. Disparate treatment generally occurs when a person
makes a decision or classifies people on the basis of a protected trait, such as race, sex, religion,
or another characteristic. Int’l Bhd. of Teamsters v. United States, 431 U.S. 324, 335 n.15 (1977).
Disparate impact occurs when a person makes a decision using neutral criteria, but the decision
adversely and disproportionately affects members of a protected group, and either the decisionmaker cannot put forth a
legally adequate justification for using the criteria, or an alternative exists that would serve the decisionmaker’s purposes
with less of a disparate impact. Tex. Dep’t of Hous. & Cmty. Affs. v. Inclusive Cmtys. Project, Inc., 576 U.S. 519, 524–25
(2015).

How disparate treatment and disparate impact frameworks apply to decisionmaking via automated, algorithmic tools
(including AI) is not always clear. Some situations involving algorithmic decisionmaking would clearly be disparate
treatment under present law. For example, a person covered by antidiscrimination law who intentionally designs an algorithm
to discriminate on the basis of a protected characteristic, or who selects an algorithm because of its discriminatory effect on a
protected group, engages in disparate treatment. See Pers. Adm’r of Mass. v. Feeney, 442 U.S. 256, 279 (1979). However, the
law is unsettled on how to evaluate cases where no human consciously chooses to discriminate, and yet an automated system
classifies people on the basis of protected characteristics. There is also substantial uncertainty over when an algorithmic
system could be said to engage in disparate treatment via proxy—that is, classification based on a facially neutral trait that is
closely correlated with a protected characteristic. The law is not clear on whether proxy discrimination always requires
human intent to use a proxy to discriminate, or whether some characteristics are so closely related to protected traits that they
are inherent proxies. It is also not clear how one could prove that certain AI systems are engaging in proxy discrimination if
there is no way to know how some systems arrive at their conclusions. How laws handle such situations, and how difficult it
is for plaintiffs to prove their case, may also depend on the causation and intent standards specific to each law.

Many unanswered questions persist regarding when and how the use of algorithms can give rise to disparate impact claims,
as well. Using algorithmic tools can result in disparate impacts in several ways. An algorithm could use decision rules or
targeted outcomes that proxy for protected characteristics or that are more or less favorable to certain groups. Algorithms
could rely on criteria that are less predictive for certain groups than others, or fail to consider criteria that would make them
more predictive for particular groups. AI systems may be trained on data that are affected by past discrimination, or that are
over- or under-representative of particular groups, potentially leading to biased or less accurate outcomes for members of
those groups. See Solon Barocas & Andrew D. Selbst, Big Data’s Disparate Impact, 104 CAL. L. REV. 671, 677–94 (2016).

There are already gray areas in legally significant elements of disparate impact doctrine. These uncertainties make it hard to
predict how courts will respond when algorithmic decisionmaking is added to the mix. Courts may have to decide, for
example, whether algorithmic systems should be analyzed as a whole or whether plaintiffs can, or must, challenge particular
elements of how a system works. They may have to determine whether a decisionmaker can rely on the fact that an
algorithm’s predictions are accurate, or whether the decisionmaker has to posit or prove how the algorithm works or why it is
accurate. Courts may also need to decide how much of a premium decisionmakers may place on cost savings or efficiency
when justifying the use of an algorithm that causes a disparate impact. To evaluate plaintiffs’ proposed less discriminatory
alternatives, courts may need to grapple with whether plaintiffs must put forward alternatives that are as efficient and cost-
effective as a challenged algorithm and what standards govern showing that an alternative—including an alternative
algorithm—is available.

Congress has many options if it chooses to regulate algorithmic decisionmaking to prevent discrimination or bias. It may
consider amending current antidiscrimination laws to better take into account the unique features of algorithmic
decisionmaking, or it can pass laws specifically targeted at potential algorithmic bias. It may prohibit discrimination or
require other, affirmative conduct by those that develop or deploy algorithmic systems, such as greater transparency, bias
audits, or guaranteed human participation in certain decisions. Some potential pathways to mitigating the risks of algorithmic
discrimination may raise constitutional concerns under the Takings Clause, First Amendment, and Equal Protection Clause.
Congress may also consider how to balance the risks of discrimination with the risks of regulation, which could stifle the
growth of automated tools, including those that may advance less discriminatory decisionmaking.

Introduction

Automated decisionmaking and artificial intelligence (AI) tools have generated interest from
lawmakers about the effect of new technology and “big data” on privacy, security, and
opportunity, among other areas. One area that has seen limited federal legislative development is
in guarding against algorithmic discrimination. At various points in time, the executive branch,1
lawmakers,2 and others3 have voiced concern about the use of “biased” algorithms and AI systems
in important areas of public life, including employment, housing, financial transactions, and
beyond.4 At the same time, these and other commentators have championed the potential of
algorithmic decisionmaking to root out discrimination and make recommendations or decisions
beyond the reach of human bias.5

This report explores legal frameworks for evaluating claims of algorithmic discrimination. It
reviews the broad legal principles that animate much of federal antidiscrimination law and how
those principles may, or may not, apply when people use algorithms to support decisionmaking.

In general, an algorithm is “a set of step-by-step instructions for solving a problem or completing
a task.”7 In the context of computing, an algorithm tells “a computer how to process inputs such
as data to produce an output, such as a decision, prediction, or sorted list.”8 While people can use
algorithms without computers, in this report the term algorithmic decisionmaking means the use
of automated, machine-based processes to make or assist with decisions, predictions, or
recommendations. Algorithmic decisionmaking can be quite basic, using rules programmed by
human experts. Software programmed to pass all college applicants with a grade point average
above 3.0 to an admissions officer for further consideration and reject all others is an example of
simple algorithmic decisionmaking. An algorithmic system could also use a more complex
statistical formula and consider multiple variables (such as the difficulty of a college applicant’s
high school course of study, the quality of their high school, their extracurriculars, standardized
test scores, or other factors).
Algorithmic systems may rely on AI. While definitions of AI vary,9 this report uses AI to refer to
“algorithms and techniques that aim to give computer systems the ability to learn new concepts or
tasks and solve complex problems in a manner that mimics human intelligence.”10 When experts
discuss AI models, they often mean computer programs engaged on some level in machine
learning, a process in which a program uses algorithms and mathematical functions to process
data and discover patterns and statistical relationships between variables. AI models can then use
these discovered relationships to classify new inputs, that is, to make recommendations or
predictions using previously unencountered data.11 An AI system may include an underlying AI
model and a user-facing application that is powered by the model. Such systems include AI
virtual assistants, chatbots, and screening tools, among others.

There are many different types of AI models, varying in, among other things, how they are
trained, the mathematical functions that power their operations, and the outputs they generate. For
example, AI models may be trained using supervised learning, unsupervised learning, or
reinforcement learning.12 A subset of AI, known as generative AI, can be used to “generate new
content,” such as “text, images, videos, computer code, or music.”13 Other AI models may

As a hypothetical example of the use of one type of AI system, consider an employer using an AI
system to screen resumes. The employer might feed the system data about employees the
employer considers “successful.” “Successfulness” is the employer’s target variable, or the
outcome a person seeks to predict using AI.15 The AI system may then discover statistical
relationships between data points about these employees—perhaps that “successful” employees
are statistically likely to have a certain employment background, use a particular computer
browser, or own a particular car.16 The AI system may assign weights to the elements it finds to
be statistically related to success or lack thereof, and use the resulting algorithms to rank new
applicants (i.e., the program creates internal decision rules).17 In this scenario, the employer may
or may not tell the program what “success” means, and the employer may or may not know which
input data the AI system considers salient and what weight it has assigned them. It may not be
possible to determine the reason for some AI systems’ classifications due to the scale and
complexity of certain AI models, a scenario often referred to as the “black box” problem.18
Additionally, there may not be a single, discoverable reason; the decision rules may change with
each set of inputs.19

example, apply only to government actors and subject laws that classify on certain bases,
including race, national origin, and sex, to more rigorous scrutiny by courts.20 Other federal
antidiscrimination schemes are differently targeted. Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964

A party also engages in disparate treatment when it adopts a facially neutral policy with
discriminatory intent.30 To plead a disparate treatment claim on such facts, a plaintiff must show
that a defendant adopted the policy “‘[b]ecause of,’ not merely ‘in spite of,’ its adverse effects

placing decorations on doors and doorposts may be engaging in disparate treatment if the
company adopted the rule to harass or drive away Jewish tenants (who may want to place
mezuzot on their doorways).32

discrimination occurs when an entity classifies people based on facially neutral traits that are
closely correlated with protected characteristics.33 An example is redlining, where financial
institutions discriminate against consumers based on zip codes, which, due to residential
segregation, can be an effective proxy for race.34 Understanding proxy discrimination is relevant
to understanding algorithmic discrimination because, as discussed below, algorithms that are
programmed to avoid considering protected characteristics might nevertheless rely on proxies.35

Mixed Motives and “Rational” Discrimination

In some cases, a defendant may have multiple motives for a decision, one or more of which may
be discriminatory while others are not. Federal laws take different approaches to these so-called
“mixed-motive” cases.48 Under Title VII, for example, so long as the protected characteristic is a
“motivating factor” for the decision, the fact that other factors may have been determinative does
not matter; the decision is still illegal.49 Courts applying the Equal Protection Clause take a
different approach: Once a plaintiff shows that a decision was at least in part motivated by a
discriminatory purpose, the burden shifts to the defendant to prove it would have made the same
decision without the illegal consideration (and thereby avoid liability).50

Not all laws recognize “motivating factor” discrimination or shift the burden to the defendant
once some discriminatory intent is shown. Under the Age Discrimination in Employment Act
(ADEA),51 for example, private-sector employees must prove that age (greater than forty) was a
“but-for” cause of any adverse treatment. They cannot bring disparate treatment claims if their
employers improperly considered their age but would have reached the same decisions regardless,
and plaintiffs always bear the burden of showing age was determinative.52

Disparity Caused by a Specific Practice

Typically, plaintiffs in disparate impact cases rely on statistical disparities between outcomes for
members of a protected group versus others.68 Plaintiffs might attempt to show, for example, that
a bank approves credit applications at lower rates for members of a particular race, or that a
school suspends or expels students of one sex at higher rates. On its own, however, a statistical
disparity in outcomes does not make out an initial disparate impact claim; the plaintiff must also
identify a specific practice or policy of the defendant’s that causes the disparity.69 The Supreme
Court has repeatedly cautioned that plaintiffs must tie disparities to causes within defendants’
control so that defendants are held liable only for their own acts, rather than general social
conditions; otherwise, the Court has warned, defendants would be forced to turn to forbidden
preferences and quotas to avoid disparate impact liability.70
Disparate impact plaintiffs commonly challenge the use of particular criteria or tests.71 Plaintiffs
may also challenge defendants’ subjective decisionmaking processes in addition to more concrete
policies or criteria.72

Tensions Between Disparate Treatment and Disparate Impact

There are limits on how far people can go to eliminate disparate impacts without running afoul of
prohibitions on disparate treatment. The Supreme Court has cautioned that disparate impact

Beyond cases directly addressing the unknowing use of an allegedly discriminatory algorithm,

prejudice, but the ultimate decisionmaker lacks conscious discriminatory intent. In the
employment discrimination context, for example, courts have considered cases where the ultimate

The Supreme Court has ruled that employers may be liable in cat’s paw cases when they act as a
conduit for the discriminatory intent of low-level supervisors.143 The employer is liable only if the
supervisor with discriminatory animus acted with the intent that the employer take some adverse
action against the victim.144 The Court has not decided whether employers are liable when a
coworker’s discriminatory animus influences the decisionmaking process.145

These facets of the doctrine may make it difficult to analogize an employer’s use of a
discriminatory algorithm to a cat’s paw case. First, a court would have to decide whether an
algorithm was more like a biased low-level supervisor or more like a coworker. Second, these
cases still hinge on intentional human actions. An algorithm cannot intend an adverse action
against an employee, which may make it impossible to use precedent from cat’s paw cases in
algorithmic discrimination matters.

Disparate Impact

How Algorithms May Cause Disparate Impacts

Algorithmic decisionmaking could result in particular groups experiencing disproportionately
negative outcomes in several ways. As a non-exhaustive list of examples, disparate impacts could
result when algorithms rely on proxies for protected characteristics; when a model uses a target
variable or decision rules that disfavor certain groups; when a model relies on criteria that are less
predictive for particular groups; or when models are trained on biased data.

An algorithm’s consideration of proxies for protected characteristics, even if not considered or
proven to be disparate treatment, could give rise to disparate impact claims.161 Take a hypothetical
AI system used to evaluate employees for “fit” with an organization’s culture that disfavors
applicants who use the email sign-off “Have a blessed day.” Even if one could not prove the
system was simply disfavoring religious applicants, such a system could result in an adverse
impact on people of certain faiths, requiring the employer to present a legitimate reason for using
it.

variables or decision rules that favor certain groups above others, even if most people would not
consider those criteria closely related enough to a particular group to be proxy variables. For
example, a landlord who designed an algorithm to select for predicted renter tenure could end up
disproportionately rejecting military families if they tend to move more often than others.162 As
another example, one lawsuit alleged that a tenant-screening tool had an unjustifiable disparate
impact on racial minorities by considering arrests without convictions, because, the plaintiffs
contended, minorities “are significantly more likely to be arrested, charged, and indicted.”163

Open Questions on Disparate Impact and Algorithms

Under antidiscrimination laws that allow for disparate impact claims—which, as explained above,
is not all of them—using disparate impact doctrine would relieve plaintiffs in algorithmic
discrimination cases of the burden of proving intent or showing what variables an algorithm
based its output on. Nevertheless, these cases would likely not be straightforward. There are a
number of unanswered questions about how disparate impact doctrine might apply in this context.

What Is the Specific Practice Causing the Disparity?

As discussed above, disparate impact plaintiffs must identify the specific practice that causes a
disparity. In algorithmic discrimination cases, one question is whether using an algorithm or AI
system is a specific enough practice, or whether plaintiffs will need to prove something more
detailed—how the algorithm works, bias in the training data, or specific problematic inputs, for
example.179 Title VII allows plaintiffs to analyze the entire decisionmaking process as “one
employment practice” when the “elements” of the process “are not capable of separation for
analysis.”180 While that provision seems to point to allowing plaintiffs to challenge an algorithm
or AI system as a whole, whether courts will follow that interpretation in practice, and whether
they will apply that rule outside of Title VII, remains to be seen.181

Regulating Algorithmic Decisionmaking Under General
Antidiscrimination Laws or Using Standalone Antidiscrimination
Rules

Congress may consider whether to regulate algorithmic decisionmaking under general
antidiscrimination authorities or whether to pass new antidiscrimination laws specifically
targeting algorithms or AI.218 Congress could amend existing antidiscrimination laws to clarify
areas that may generate confusion in the context of algorithmic decisionmaking. Congress could,
for example, clarify the intent standard for disparate treatment; the standard to show an adequate
interest justifying a policy or practice that causes disparate impacts; or the standards for less
discriminatory alternatives. Such changes could, depending on how they were framed, impact
both algorithmic discrimination claims and other kinds of claims.219

Alternatively, rather than relying on general antidiscrimination principles to reach algorithmic
decisionmaking, Congress could decide to specify what uses of algorithms are or are not
discriminatory. Some of the ways algorithms may be alleged to be “unfair” are difficult to classify
as disparate impact or disparate treatment, or as discrimination under current law at all, leaving
unclear what standards apply. For example, it is not clear if using an algorithm trained on
unrepresentative data violates antidiscrimination law, and assuming it does, whether the relevant
antidiscrimination framework would be disparate treatment or disparate impact. Congress could
legislate specific rules governing when certain uses of algorithmic models constitute
discrimination. In so doing, it could try to adhere to current legal frameworks or write new ones
specific to this context.

Adopting Broad Versus Tailored Requirements

Congress could seek to apply antidiscrimination standards to algorithmic decisionmaking across a
variety of domains or choose to focus on particular contexts. Some state laws apply the same or
similar standards across many contexts in which algorithms may be used. Colorado, for example,
passed a bill with transparency requirements for when “automated decision-making technology”
is used to “materially influence” a “consequential decision,” including access to education,
employment, housing, financial services, insurance, health care, or “essential government
services and public benefits.”220 Other laws are more narrowly targeted. Montana and New
Hampshire, for example, have passed laws regulating the states’ own use of potentially
discriminatory AI.221

Audits

Some commentators have expressed skepticism that greater transparency, at least alone, would
combat algorithmic “bias.”248 Some regulators and commentators have gone further and required
or suggested that developers and/or users of algorithmic systems conduct some form of audit or
testing to discern whether their programs unfairly or illegally impact certain groups.249

Examining Disparate Impact
The viability of disparate impact doctrine generally is in dispute.274 Federal agencies have
historically assumed that algorithmic decisionmaking could result in disparate treatment or
(where statutes allow it), disparate impact violations.275 In 2025, President Trump issued an
executive order that described disparate impact doctrine as “wholly inconsistent with the
Constitution,” stating that it “imperils the effectiveness of civil rights laws by mandating, rather
than proscribing, discrimination.”276 The order declared that “[i]t is the policy of the United States
to eliminate the use of disparate-impact liability in all contexts to the maximum degree possible”
and instructed agencies to “deprioritize” enforcement of disparate impact liability and take steps
to “amend[] or repeal, as appropriate under applicable law,” agency rules and guidance imposing
disparate impact liability.277 This order, and agency actions implementing it,278 may constrain
federal agencies in how they approach the discriminatory potential of algorithmic
decisionmaking.

If Congress intends for agencies to pursue disparate impact cases, it could strengthen their
statutory mandates to do so. It may, for example, add disparate impact liability to statutes that
lack it or require agencies to devote resources to disparate impact enforcement. Alternatively, if
Congress endorses the Trump Administration’s position on disparate impact, it could repeal
disparate impact authorities or specify that disparate impact causes of action are not available.279
Congress could also alter or clarify the standards by which claimants prove disparate impact
claims, generally or in cases involving algorithmic decisionmaking. Congress may take different
approaches to different statutes or sectors.

The Supreme Court held in Ricci that some attempts to eliminate disparate impacts result in
disparate treatment.280 Additionally, some Justices and other federal judges have concluded that
majority-group plaintiffs also must be able to bring disparate impact claims if the doctrine is to
survive constitutional scrutiny.281 These perspectives could significantly limit how far Congress
can go to require algorithms to avoid disparate impacts, at least when regulating discrimination
on the basis of constitutionally suspect traits, such as race, national origin, and sex.282 Courts may
find, for example, that laws that impose liability without giving defendants enough leeway to
proffer nondiscriminatory justifications for their actions cross the line to mandating demographic
balancing.283 Similar results could occur with laws that allow plaintiffs to challenge isolated
elements of a system, especially given that members of both majority and minority groups can
bring disparate impact cases, regardless of the ultimate impact of the system as a whole. The
more individual parts of a system can be challenged, the more it exposes a user to liability, and
the more it may incentivize users to balance outcomes to avoid the pain of litigation.

Congress does not face these constitutional constraints when addressing discrimination on the
basis of traits like disability, age, and family status, which are not constitutionally suspect.284 To
the extent disparate impact and disparate treatment frameworks are in tension with regard to
discrimination on the basis of such characteristics, Congress has significant leeway to determine
how to resolve those tensions.
