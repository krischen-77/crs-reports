## Summary

The Spending Clause of the U.S. Constitution (Article I, Section 8, Clause 1) gives Congress broad power to authorize spending for the "general Welfare." As part of that power, Congress can restrict how federal funds are used and can place conditions on federal funding to incentivize recipients to change their policies or conduct. The Necessary and Proper Clause (Article I, Section 8, Clause 18) supplements Congress's spending authority, allowing Congress to create conditions or remedies designed to ensure that federal funds are used for their intended purposes.

Funding conditions, though common, are subject to constitutional limitations. First, funding conditions must provide *clear notice* to the recipient of what actions are required in exchange for federal funds and the consequences of noncompliance. The Supreme Court has analogized spending legislation to a contract between the federal government and an applicant for funds, stating that the applicant must "knowingly and voluntarily" accept the terms of the agreement. *Landor v. L**a.* *Dep**'t* *of Corr**.**&**Pub**.* *Safety*, 609 U.S. 59, 68 (2026). Second, funding conditions must be*related* to the purposes of the federally funded program or activity. Though the precise degree of connection is unsettled, a condition that is "reasonably related to the purpose of the expenditure" usually suffices. *New York v. United States*, 505 U.S. 144, 172 (1992). Third, although Congress may incentivize states to adopt a particular policy to obtain specific federal funds, it may not *coerce*state participation. Congress may not, for example, tie an existing funding source on which a state has come to rely to compliance with a new kind of requirement. Fourth, the funding condition may not violate an *independent constitutional bar*or the related unconstitutional conditions doctrine. For example, Congress may not require governmental recipients, as a condition of receiving federal funds, to take an action that would violate an individual's freedom of speech or free exercise of religion.

Whether a funding recipient is a *state*or a *private*entity may affect the applicability of these four general limits on funding conditions. The clear notice and relatedness principles seem to apply regardless of the public or private nature of the funding recipient. The Supreme Court has described these two limits as a means of ensuring that Congress does not attempt to use its spending power to regulate private conduct directly. The independent constitutional bar principle is likely a cross-cutting consideration as well, though its specific application depends on the nature of the funding condition. By comparison, the anti-coercion doctrine primarily concerns the division of power between the federal government and the states, leading some lower courts to question whether it applies when Congress is offering funds to private entities.

Additional legal questions can arise when the *executive branch*, rather than Congress, imposes a funding condition. Congress often delegates the authority to administer grant programs and disburse financial assistance to federal agencies. Ultimately, however, the "power of the purse" belongs to the legislative branch, not the executive branch. Accordingly, when a federal agency places a condition on federal funding, that condition could give rise to constitutional (i.e., separation-of-powers) or statutory (e.g., Administrative Procedure Act) challenges if the condition arguably exceeds the scope of the agency's delegated authority.

Thus, while Congress's power to authorize and condition federal spending is expansive, a funding condition that exceeds the limits discussed in this report could run afoul of the Constitution.

Congress's spending power comes from the Taxing and Spending Clause of the Constitution, which gives Congress the power to spend federal funds to "provide for the common Defence and general Welfare of the United States."[1](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn1) The Supreme Court has interpreted this authority to include the power to place conditions on federal funding. The conditions need not be limited to how the funds are spent. Congress can, and often does, use its spending power to incentivize states to adopt Congress's preferred policies or practices. Sometimes Congress authorizes a federal agency to impose additional conditions on participation in a federally funded program.

This report begins with a discussion of the broad reach of the Spending Clause, whose only express, textual limitation is the requirement that federal spending be in pursuit of the national defense or general welfare. Part one also explores how the Constitution's Necessary and Proper Clause may supplement Congress's spending power in some circumstances. The second part of this report discusses the main constitutional limits on Congress's authority to condition federal funding and their application to governmental and private funding recipients. These limits are the clear notice, relatedness, anti-coercion, and independent constitutional bar principles, and the requirement of knowing and voluntary consent to a funding condition. The third part of this report discusses constitutional and statutory limitations on the executive branch's authority to impose federal funding conditions, including the Constitution's separation-of-powers principle and the Administrative Procedure Act (APA).[2](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn2)

## Broad Spending Power for the General Welfare

In the earliest Spending Clause cases, the Supreme Court considered both whether Congress's spending power was limited to matters on which Congress had the express constitutional authority to legislate and who determines the needs of the "general Welfare."[3](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn3) As explained below, both of these questions were resolved in favor of Congress's broad spending authority. In addition, the Court has long affirmed Congress's authority to place conditions on federal funding, subject to the constitutional constraints discussed in the next part of the report.[4](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn4)

### Scope Not Limited by Other Enumerated Powers

Because the Constitution provides Congress with a limited set of enumerated powers,[5](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn5) an early debate emerged among the Framers over whether the Constitution limits Congress's spending power to areas in which Congress could legislate directly, such as matters of interstate commerce.[6](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn6) James Madison believed the Spending Clause's reference to "general Welfare" in the first clause of Article I, Section 8 "amounted to no more than a reference to the other powers enumerated in the subsequent clauses of the same section."[7](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn7) In other words, Madison maintained that Congress could exercise its spending power only to carry out other powers that the Constitution expressly granted to Congress, such as the power to regulate interstate and foreign commerce or to establish post offices.[8](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn8) In contrast, Alexander Hamilton "maintained the clause confers a power separate and distinct from those later enumerated" and that "Congress consequently has a substantive power to tax and to appropriate, limited only by the requirement that it shall be exercised to provide for the general welfare of the United States."[9](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn9)

In the 1936 case of *United States v. Butler*, the Supreme Court settled on "the Hamiltonian position."[10](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn10) The Court held that while "the power to tax is not unlimited, its confines are set in the clause which confers it, and not in those of section 8 which bestow and define the legislative powers of the Congress."[11](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn11) Thus, "the power of Congress to authorize expenditure of public moneys for public purposes is not limited by the direct grants of legislative power found in the Constitution."[12](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn12) The *Butler* Court nevertheless cautioned that this "broader construction leaves the power to spend subject to limitations," including that federal spending be in pursuit of the "common defence" or "general welfare."[13](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn13)

### Deference to Congress's Judgment on General Welfare Needs

Courts have the ultimate authority to determine whether an appropriation is "in aid of the 'general welfare'" within the meaning of the Spending Clause in light of their constitutional duty to "say what the law is."[14](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn14) However, the Supreme Court has emphasized that in deciding "whether a particular expenditure is intended to serve general public purposes, courts should defer substantially" to Congress's judgment.[15](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn15)

*Helvering v. Davis*, decided a year after *Butler*, illustrated this deference to Congress's judgment.[16](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn16) There, the Court upheld the Social Security Act's provision of "old age benefits," which primarily took the form of a monthly pension payable to a retiree 65 years of age or older, funded by mandatory, wage-based contributions from employees and employers.[17](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn17) The Court rejected a Tenth Amendment challenge to this statutory scheme because it came within Congress's power to spend for the general welfare.[18](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn18) Courts should not second-guess Congress's decision, the Supreme Court stated, unless its "choice is clearly wrong, a display of arbitrary power, not an exercise of judgment," such that there is "no reasonable possibility" that the "legislation fall[s] within the wide range of discretion permitted to the Congress."[19](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn19) The Court also observed that the general welfare is not a "static" concept because "[w]hat is critical or urgent changes with the times."[20](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn20)

The Court then cited two reasons to defer to Congress's judgment with respect to the Social Security Act. First, after recounting the growth of widespread unemployment during the period that came to be known as the Great Depression,[21](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn21) the Court observed that Congress had held hearings and made legislative findings showing that the segment of the U.S. population aged 65 and older who were "unable to take care of themselves [was] growing at a threatening pace."[22](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn22) Second, the Court saw the "problem" as "plainly national in area and dimensions," and beyond the capacity of individual states to handle "effectively."[23](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn23) The Court reiterated that "the concept of welfare" for purposes of federal spending "is shaped by Congress."[24](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn24)

Thus, while the Spending Clause's reference to "general Welfare" is technically a limit on Congress's spending authority, the Supreme Court has interpreted the concept broadly and with deference to Congress's judgment.[25](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn25)

### Discretion for Congress to Apply Conditions

Complementing its discretion to spend funds for a variety of purposes, Congress has "wide latitude to attach conditions to the receipt of federal assistance in order to further its policy objectives."[26](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn26) This latitude is reflected in the range of spending conditions that Congress has enacted over time. For example,

* Congress has required universities receiving federal funds to provide military recruiters with the same level of campus access as other recruiters;[27](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn27)
* Congress has made it a crime to bribe officials of state and local entities that receive a certain level of federal funding;[28](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn28)
* Congress has required libraries to install filtering software to receive federal funds to expand internet access for patrons;[29](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn29)
* Congress has prescribed milestones for the disposal of radioactive waste in exchange for money generated by federally authorized state surcharges on that waste;[30](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn30)
* Congress has incentivized states to adopt a minimum drinking age of 21 by withholding a percentage of federal highway funds;[31](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn31) and
* Congress has required state and local officials working on federally funded activities to comply with the Hatch Act's limitations on government officials' participation in political campaign activities.[32](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn32)

The Supreme Court has upheld each of these laws when challenged on Spending Clause or other constitutional grounds.[33](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn33)

### Supplemental Authority Under the Necessary and Proper Clause

Congress can also adopt certain funding conditions—or remedies for violating them—pursuant to the Constitution's Necessary and Proper Clause, which supplements all of Congress's legislative powers, including its spending power.[34](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn34) The Necessary and Proper Clause states that "Congress shall have Power . . . To make all Laws which shall be necessary and proper for carrying into Execution the foregoing Powers, and all other Powers vested by this Constitution in the Government of the United States, or in any Department or Officer thereof."[35](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn35) In the Court's words, the Clause "authorizes Congress to employ 'necessary and proper' means" for carrying out its spending power.[36](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn36)

In the seminal case *McCulloch v. Maryland*, the Supreme Court adopted a permissive standard for assessing whether a regulation is "necessary and proper" to carry out a federal power: "Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the constitution, and all means which are appropriate, which are plainly adapted to that end, which are not prohibited, but consist with the letter and spirit of the constitution, are constitutional."[37](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn37) The Court cited *McCulloch* in 2004 in upholding a federal law criminalizing bribery involving officials employed by entities that receive more than $10,000 annually in federal funds.[38](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn38) The Court reasoned that the Necessary and Proper Clause authorizes Congress to "see to it that taxpayer dollars appropriated under [the spending] power are in fact spent for the general welfare, and not frittered away in graft or on projects undermined when funds are siphoned off or corrupt public officers are derelict about demanding value for dollars."[39](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn39)

The Court expressed a potentially more constrained view of Congress's "necessary and proper" authority as it relates to spending legislation in its 2026 decision in *Landor v. Louisiana Department of Corrections and Public Safety*.[40](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn40) That case involved the Religious Land Use and Institutionalized Persons Act of 2000 (RLUIPA), a statute that authorizes incarcerated individuals at federally funded correctional facilities (among others) to bring a claim or defense in federal court to challenge government actions that "substantial[ly] burden" their religious exercise.[41](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn41) An incarcerated individual who prevails on a RLUIPA claim may "obtain appropriate relief against a government," which the statute defines to include state and local governmental entities and officials and "any other person acting under color of State law."[42](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn42) The Court had previously ruled, in a 2020 decision involving a related statute, that the phrase "persons acting under color of law" allows claims against government officials in their individual (i.e., personal) capacities and the language "appropriate relief" allows for monetary damages in such cases.[43](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn43) In other words, a defendant sued in his individual capacity would be personally liable for any money damages the court awards if the plaintiff wins.[44](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn44)

In *Landor*, the plaintiff, an incarcerated individual, sought to hold two state prison officials personally liable for money damages for alleged violations of RLUIPA.[45](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn45) The Court held that neither the Spending Clause nor the Necessary and Proper Clause authorized such a remedy.[46](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn46) The Court explained that the Spending Clause requires knowing and voluntary acceptance of a funding condition, but unlike their employer who accepted the conditional funding, the state employees did not consent to facing lawsuits under RLUIPA.[47](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn47) As to the Necessary and Proper Clause, the Court reasoned that a remedy seeking monetary damages from nonparties to a funding agreement does not "help 'carr[y] into execution' Congress's enumerated power to spend money."[48](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn48) The Court stated that "while the Necessary and Proper Clause may allow Congress to enact provisions actually incidental to its spending power, like those protecting federal money against graft, it does not tolerate outcomes that would 'undermine the structure of [the federal] government established by the Constitution.'"[49](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn49)

Thus, Congress can create conditions or remedies that are "necessary and proper" to carrying out its spending legislation, though *Landor* recognizes limits to that authority in reaching individuals or entities that have not consented. Additionally, as with all conditions on federal funding, such conditions must be aligned with the constitutional limits on Congress's spending power discussed in the next section.

## General Constitutional Limits on Federal Funding Conditions

The Supreme Court has identified at least four constitutional limits on Congress's power to attach conditions to federal funding.[50](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn50) First, the condition must be unambiguous so that the recipient can knowingly and voluntarily accept the terms of the funding arrangement (*clear notice*). Second, the condition must be reasonably related to the program or funding stream it restricts (*relatedness*). Third, the condition may not be unduly coercive(*anti-coercion*). Fourth, the condition may not induce the recipient to violate a separate constitutionalprovision, such as the First Amendment's Free Speech or Establishment Clauses (*independent constitutional bar*).

The Court's 2026 decision in *Landor* arguably recognized an additional "consent requirement," explaining that the clear notice, relatedness, and independent constitutional bar principles "apply *in addition to—not instead of*—the rule that Congress may not use the Spending Clause to bind entities and individuals without their knowing and voluntary consent."[51](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn51) At the same time, the Court suggested that its Spending Clause precedents already accounted for the need for funding recipients' consent, explaining that both the clear notice and the anti-coercion limits "help ensure real consent exists."[52](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn52) Whether lower courts come to treat the consent requirement as a separate constitutional limitation or intrinsic to one or more of the first four limits set out above, the requirement principally concerns *who* can be liable for violating a funding condition. Accordingly, this report discusses the consent requirement under the section "[Scope of Liability](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#_Ref242184075)," which is part of the clear notice discussion.

The foregoing constraints on Congress's spending power stem, in part, from the Spending Clause itself and the structure of enumerated powers in Article I. Because states are often the recipients of federal funding, these judicially recognized Spending Clause limits also account for the Tenth Amendment, which limits Congress's legislative power vis-a-vis the states, along with other principles of federalism and state sovereignty reflected in the Constitution's text and structure.[53](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn53) This reason may explain why Spending Clause cases frequently refer to Congress's obligations when imposing conditions on *states* in exchange for federal funding. Still, the Court has not confined these principles to federal funding arrangements with states and has applied at least one of these limits, clear notice, to conditions on funding to private entities.[54](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn54) At the same time, some lower courts have questioned whether the anti-coercion doctrine—a direct outgrowth of a long-standing federalism principle—applies when Congress is offering conditional funding to private entities.[55](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn55)

### Clear Notice

The clear notice principle requires conditions on federal funding to be unambiguous and prospective so that potential recipients have an opportunity to knowingly and voluntarily accept or reject the terms of the funding arrangement.[56](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn56) The Supreme Court has often referred to Spending Clause legislation as akin to a "contract" between the federal government and a funding recipient.[57](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn57) The reasoning behind the contract analogy—the need for a clear offer and knowing acceptance—does not depend on the recipient being a state, even if the rule is often framed in terms of federal-state funding arrangements.[58](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn58) Thus, the clear notice principle applies regardless of the governmental or nongovernmental status of the funding recipient, as confirmed by Supreme Court cases invoking this limit in Spending Clause challenges by private entities.[59](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn59)

#### *Pennhurst*'s Clear Notice Standard

The seminal case on the clear notice standard is *Pennhurst State School and Hospital v. Halderman*.[60](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn60) In *Pennhurst*, the Supreme Court considered a statute in which Congress provided funding for states to provide services to individuals with developmental disabilities.[61](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn61) As part of the plan, states had to agree to "protect[]" the "rights" of persons with developmental disabilities "consistent with" the findings section of the act.[62](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn62) The statute also required states to take certain other actions "as a condition" of receiving assistance.[63](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn63) The parties disagreed, however, on the scope of the conditions. A findings section in the statute provided that persons with developmental disabilities "have a right to appropriate treatment" and that such treatment "should be provided in the setting that is least restrictive of the person's personal liberty."[64](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn64) The case presented the question of whether these findings stated a condition on the federal funding.

While considering this question, the Court examined "the possible sources of Congress'[s] power to legislate," including the Spending Clause.[65](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn65) The Court likened spending laws with conditions to a contract:

[L]egislation enacted pursuant to the spending power is much in the nature of a contract: in return for federal funds, the States agree to comply with federally imposed conditions. The legitimacy of Congress' power to legislate under the spending power thus rests on whether the State voluntarily and knowingly accepts the terms of the "contract." There can, of course, be no knowing acceptance if a State is unaware of the conditions or is unable to ascertain what is expected of it.[66](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn66)

The Court held that "if Congress intends to impose a condition on the grant of federal moneys, it must do so unambiguously."[67](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn67) Applying this rule, the Court concluded that "Congress fell well short of providing clear notice to the States" that accepting funding under the act required compliance with the statute's findings section.[68](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn68) Viewing this section in the context of the statute as a whole, the Court reasoned that the findings reflected "general statements of federal policy, not newly created legal duties."[69](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn69) By contrast, the Court observed, in other parts of the statute Congress used "clear terms" like "condition" when it intended to impose a requirement on a state in exchange for federal funding.[70](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn70) The Court further observed that the statute lacked the typical method of enforcing a funding condition because it did not authorize the federal government to withhold funds for noncompliance with the findings section.[71](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn71) Accordingly, the Court concluded that the statute did not require states to provide for "appropriate treatment" in the "least restrictive" environment in exchange for federal funds.[72](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn72)

The *Pennhurst* Court framed the clear notice principle as a "rule of statutory construction"—that is, in interpreting funding legislation, a reader should look for a clear expression of Congress's intent to impose a condition before concluding that the statute imposes one.[73](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn73) Additionally, the Court suggested that the "appropriate treatment" provision would violate the Constitution if it werea condition because it provided inadequate notice to the states of their obligations in accepting federal funds.[74](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn74) It is evident from the Court's *Pennhurst* discussion and the application of the clear notice principle in subsequent cases that clear notice is a constitutional requirement, not just an interpretive aid in construing statutory language.[75](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn75)

#### Clear Notice as Applied to Remedies

The clear notice requirement applies not only to the condition itself, but also to the *re**medy* that a law imposes for a state's violation of that condition.[76](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn76) The *Pennhurst* Court observed that "the typical remedy for state noncompliance with federally imposed conditions" is "action by the Federal Government to terminate funds to the State."[77](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn77) In practice, termination of funds may not be the agency's first step. Depending on the statutes and regulations governing the funding at issue, an agency may have a range of options to respond to noncompliance, from providing the recipient with notice and an opportunity to correct a violation, to seeking recoupment of federal funding or debarment of the funding recipient.[78](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn78) Stated differently, then, the takeaway from *Pennhurst* is that the presumptive remedy for violation of a funding condition is agency action related to the funding itself or the recipient's eligibility for future funding. In *Pennhurst* and subsequent decisions, the Supreme Court has cautioned against inferring other types of remedies, such as a private right of action to sue a funding recipient for monetary damages.[79](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn79) Because Spending Clause legislation is in the nature of a contract, "atypical" remedies require clear notice to, and knowing and voluntary consent from, the person who might be held liable for violating a funding condition.[80](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn80)

Supreme Court decisions applying the clear notice principle in disputes over available remedies have generally fallen into three categories. The first category involves cases where the plaintiff alleges that violation of a funding condition gives rise to a private cause of action—that is, a right to sue the violator in court to recover monetary damages or obtain injunctive relief.[81](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn81) The second category involves cases where parties dispute the scope of liability or remedies under an available private right of action, including who can be held personally liable. The third category involves cases where the plaintiff argues that a state has waived its sovereign immunity (i.e., agreed to answer private lawsuits) for violations of a funding condition.

##### Private Right of Action

Although funding repercussions are the presumptive remedy for funding condition violations,[82](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn82) Congress maycreatea private right of action against funding recipients through *express* statutory language.[83](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn83) In the absence of express text, courts do not readily infer the availability of such an action. Instead, courts ask, first, whether Congress intended to create a *private* *right*, and second, whether Congress intended to create a *private remedy*.[84](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn84)

To decide whether Congress has created a private right, the Supreme Court has instructed lower courts to consider whether the statute contains clear "'rights-creating' language" and is focused on the individuals Congress aims to protect rather than the parties Congress seeks to regulate.[85](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn85) It is not sufficient, the Court has said, for a statute to merely "benefit or protect" the interests of certain persons; it must have "'an unmistakable focus'" on individuals like the plaintiff."[86](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn86) Congress must "'speak[] with a clear voice,' and manifest[] an 'unambiguous' intent to confer individual rights."[87](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn87)

In two of the three cases applying this "demanding" standard, the Supreme Court concluded that the statutes at issue did not create individual federal rights.[88](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn88) In *Gonzaga University v. Doe*, the Court considered whether funding conditions in the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act of 1974 (FERPA) would allow a student to sue a private university for the unauthorized release of his education records.[89](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn89) FERPA provides that "[n]o funds shall be made available under any applicable program to any educational agency or institution which has a policy or practice of permitting the release of education records . . . of students without the written consent of their parents[.]"[90](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn90) In the Court's view, there was "no question" that the statute "fail[ed] to confer enforceable rights."[91](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn91) For one, the Court reasoned, FERPA "lack[ed] the sort of 'rights-creating' language" present in other laws, such as Titles VI and IX,[92](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn92) which state that "no person shall be subjected to discrimination."[93](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn93) Instead, the Court observed, "FERPA's provisions speak only to the Secretary of Education, directing that 'no funds shall be made available'" to an institution with a noncompliant policy, and thus lacked a focus on individual students' interests.[94](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn94)

The Court observed a similar lack of focus on individual rights with respect to the Medicaid condition at issue in *Medina v. Planned Parenthood South Atlantic*.[95](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn95) The condition at issue required states receiving Medicaid funding to "ensure that 'any individual eligible for medical assistance . . . may obtain' it 'from any [provider] qualified to perform the service . . . who undertakes to provide' it."[96](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn96) The Court reasoned that while "this provision seeks to benefit both providers and patients," it lacked "clear and unambiguous 'rights-creating language.'"[97](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn97) The Court found it relevant, too, that the statute included "various exceptions" to the requirement, allowing states to exclude certain providers from their Medicaid programs, because it doubted Congress would simultaneously create "an individually enforceable right" and allow states to "control its scope."[98](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn98)

By comparison, in *Health and Hospital Corporation of Marion County v.* *Talevski*, the Court ruled that the Federal Nursing Home Reform Act (FNHRA) gave nursing home residents enforceable rights "to be free from unnecessary physical or chemical restraints and to be discharged or transferred only when certain preconditions are satisfied."[99](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn99) Applying "traditional tools of statutory construction," the Court observed that both the text and the headings of the applicable provisions referred to residents and their rights.[100](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn100) For example, the provision requiring a funded nursing facility to "protect and promote the rights of each resident," including the "right to be free from . . . any physical or chemical restraints imposed for purposes of discipline or convenience," appeared in a subsection entitled "[r]equirements relating to residents' rights."[101](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn101) FNHRA's provisions thus "use clear 'rights-creating language,' speak 'in terms of the persons benefited,' and have an 'unmistakable focus on the benefited class.'"[102](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn102)

In addition to demonstrating a federal right, a plaintiff seeking to sue a funding recipient in federal court must point to a corresponding remedy that authorizes the lawsuit.[103](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn103) Since the Civil Rights Act of 1871, a federal statute commonly known as "Section 1983" has supplied the remedy part of the equation in some circumstances.[104](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn104) Section 1983 "allows private parties to sue state actors who violate their 'rights' under 'the Constitution and laws' of the United States."[105](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn105) The "laws" actionable under Section 1983 can include Spending Clause statutes (again, if those statutes create clear individual federal rights).[106](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn106) In cases involving the interplay between funding conditions and Section 1983, the Supreme Court has also considered whether the statutory scheme evinces Congress's intent to foreclose the use of Section 1983 as a remedy. A statute could displace Section 1983 if it includes explicit language barring suits under Section 1983 or its text and context suggest that a different "remedial scheme" is "the exclusive avenue through which a plaintiff may assert [his] claims."[107](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn107)

In the absence of such express language, the Court has examined whether allowing a plaintiff to sue under Section 1983 would be "incompatible" with the enforcement mechanisms described in the statute. In *Talevski*, the Court concluded that nothing in the text or context of FNHRA indicated that Congress intended to foreclose Section 1983 as a potential remedy for nursing home residents.[108](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn108) The Court also found "no incompatibility" between private actions under Section 1983 and FNHRA's "administrative processes concerning inspection of covered nursing facilities and accountability for noncompliant facilities."[109](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn109) The Court explained that the mere existence of a "comprehensive" or "detailed" enforcement scheme is not enough to displace Section 1983 as an available remedy.[110](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn110) The Court contrasted the potential for complementary enforcement mechanisms with the hypothetical of "an express private judicial right of action" containing different procedural requirements or remedies that might be frustrated by allowing parallel suits under Section 1983.[111](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn111)

##### Scope of Liability

In some cases involving privately enforceable rights, the interested parties dispute the scope of liability if a court were to find a violation of that right. They may ask a court to resolve, for example, whether the law allows for compensatory damages, punitive damages, or certain costs or fees.[112](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn112) The Supreme Court has held that funding recipients' potential liability, like the condition itself, "must be set out 'unambiguously.'"[113](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn113) The reviewing court must "view the [statute] from the perspective of a state official who is engaged in the process of deciding whether the State should accept [the statute's] funds and the obligations that go with those funds."[114](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn114) The relevant consideration is "whether such a state official would clearly understand" that the state might incur the type of liability in question in the event of a statutory violation.[115](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn115) In other words, the funding recipient must know "what sort of penalties might be on the table."[116](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn116)

For some statutes enacted before *Pennhurst*, the Court has recognized remedies that were not explicitly set out in the statutory text.[117](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn117) Following *Pennhurst*, however, the Court has demanded more textual clarity. For example, *Arlington Central School District Board of Education v. Murphy* involved the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA), a post-*Pennhurst* statute that expressly provided for a private right of action.[118](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn118) The statute authorized the award of "reasonable attorneys' fees as part of the costs" to a prevailing party.[119](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn119) The Court held that the IDEA did not provide "clear notice" to the states that, by accepting funds under the IDEA, they might be liable for the prevailing parties' expert fees.[120](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn120) The Court reasoned that neither the statutory terms "attorneys' fees" nor "costs" clearly encompassed expert fees.[121](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn121) In the Court's view, a contrary statement of congressional intent in the legislative record did not supply the requisite notice to funding recipients. Specifically, the conference committee report for the IDEA stated: "The conferees intend that the term 'attorneys' fees as part of the costs' include reasonable expenses and fees of expert witnesses and the reasonable costs of any test or evaluation which is found to be necessary for the preparation of the . . . case."[122](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn122) "Whatever weight this legislative history would merit in another context," the Court reasoned, "it is not sufficient here."[123](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn123) The Court stated that "[i]n a Spending Clause case, the key is not what a majority of the Members of both Houses intend but what the States are clearly told regarding the conditions that go along with the acceptance of those funds."[124](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn124)

While the foregoing cases focused on funding recipients' *knowledge* of the scope of liability, the Supreme Court has also emphasized the need for recipients to *voluntar**ily* *consent* to exposure to penalties other than funding repercussions. In *Landor*, discussed above, the Court held that the plaintiff could not seek to hold individual prison officers personally liable for money damages under RLUIPA, because unlike their state employer, the officers had not "entered any agreement with the federal government" or "voluntarily and knowingly consented to answer private suits" under the statute.[125](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn125) The Court suggested that Congress could have used its Spending Clause power to reach individual correctional officers by, for example, incentivizing federally funded prisons to require their employees "to enter separate contracts with the federal government consenting to answer suits" under the statute.[126](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn126) Under any Spending Clause approach, the Court emphasized, "voluntary and knowing consent is key."[127](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn127)

##### Waiver of State Sovereign Immunity

The Supreme Court has held that under the Constitution, states retain sovereign immunity—that is, immunity from lawsuits filed by private citizens.[128](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn128) The Eleventh Amendment is an example of this principle,[129](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn129) providing that courts may not entertain federal actions "commenced or prosecuted against one of the United States" by private citizens.[130](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn130) Although individual states are free to waive their sovereign immunity and consent to certain types of lawsuits, Congress can only abrogate—or remove—a state's sovereign immunity under limited circumstances.[131](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn131)

If Congress cannot abrogate the states' sovereign immunity for a particular purpose, it may use its spending power to incentivize states to waive their own immunity. Specifically, Congress may condition the receipt of federal funds on a state's consent to be sued in certain types of actions.[132](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn132) A "State's consent to suit must be 'unequivocally expressed' in the text of the relevant statute," consistent with *Pennhurst*'s clear notice standard.[133](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn133)

An unambiguous waiver provision addresses the forum where states may be sued. It is well settled that "the mere receipt of federal funds cannot establish that a State has consented to suit in federal court."[134](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn134) A state also does not "necessarily consent[] to suit in federal court by participating in programs funded under the statute."[135](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn135) Additionally, a state's "general waiver of sovereign immunity" is insufficient to subject that state to a private action in federal court, because the state might be consenting only to answer suits by private citizens in state court.[136](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn136) An unequivocal waiver in the Spending Clause context informs states that by accepting federal funds, they agree to be sued in federal court.[137](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn137)

Congress need not employ "magic words" to effect such a waiver.[138](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn138) Lower court cases suggest that words such as "condition" or "waiver" are not required if other statutory language elucidates the connection between accepting funding and exposure to private lawsuits in federal court.[139](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn139) For example, Section 1003 of the Rehabilitation Act provides that a "State shall not be immune under the Eleventh Amendment of the Constitution . . . from suit in Federal court for a violation of" certain enumerated statutes, including Title VI and Title IX.[140](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn140) Courts have held that, despite the absence of the words "condition" or "waiver," "Congress succeeded in its effort to codify a clear, unambiguous, and unequivocal condition of waiver of Eleventh Amendment immunity" in Section 1003—at least for Spending Clause statutes expressly named in this provision.[141](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn141)

The provision that seeks to effect a waiver of state sovereign immunity must also provide a clear statement of the type of liability that a state might face in a private lawsuit. For example, "to sustain a claim that [a state] is liable for awards of monetary damages, the waiver of sovereign immunity must extend unambiguously to such monetary claims."[142](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn142) In the 2011 case *Sossamon v. Texas*, the Court considered whether an inmate at a state correctional facility could sue the State of Texas, a federal funding recipient, for money damages for alleged violations of RLUIPA.[143](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn143) RLUIPA authorizes a private right of action for an aggrieved inmate to "obtain appropriate reliefagainst a government."[144](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn144) The Supreme Court concluded that "RLUIPA's authorization of 'appropriate relief against a government'" fell short of "the unequivocal expression of state consent that our precedents require."[145](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn145) The Court described the language as "open-ended and ambiguous about what types of relief it includes."[146](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn146) Because of this ambiguity, the Court applied the background rule that waivers of sovereign immunity are construed narrowly, in favor of the sovereign, and thus did not allow actions for damages.[147](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn147)

The Court in *Sossamon* also rejected the argument that a "residual clause" in Section 1003 of the Rehabilitation Act supplied the requisite notice. That clause appeared to extend Section 1003's waiver of Eleventh Amendment immunity for specified Spending Clause statutes to "the provisions of any other Federal statute prohibiting discrimination by recipients of Federal financial assistance."[148](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn148) The Court in *Sossamon* seemed to question whether such a "residual clause . . . could constitute an unequivocal textual waiver,"[149](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn149) ultimately deciding that Section 1003 did not clearly extend to RLUIPA because "a State might reasonably conclude that the clause covers only provisions using the term 'discrimination'" (which RLUIPA does not).[150](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn150)

Thus, subjecting states to private lawsuits under a spending statute appears to require a clear and unequivocal statement in the statutory text to inform states that waiving their immunity to lawsuits in federal court is a condition of receiving federal funding, and states will be subject only to the types of liability in those lawsuits that are clearly encompassed in the statutory waiver.

#### Potential Sources of Clear Notice

When determining whether a particular funding condition satisfies the clear notice requirement, courts may disagree about what sources they can consider. Cases such as *Arlington Central School District Board of Education*suggest that statutory text provides the best indication of the presence or absence of clear notice, particularly for statutes enacted after *Pennhurst*.[151](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn151) Still, the Supreme Court has not foreclosed the possibility that an agency regulation, a judicial decision, or evidence of past practice could place a prospective funding recipient on notice of its obligations. In *Jackson v.* *Birmingham Board of Education*, the Supreme Court listed three reasons why states were on notice that they could be liable in a private Title IX action for intentional retaliation.[152](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn152) First, the Court observed that its decisions since the late 1970s apprised states that they could be liable for "diverse forms of intentional sex discrimination" in violation of Title IX.[153](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn153) Second, "regulations implementing Title IX clearly prohibit[ed] retaliation and ha[d] been on the books for nearly 30 years."[154](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn154) Third, appellate courts had ruled that Title IX covered retaliation at the time of the conduct in question in the case.[155](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn155) In these circumstances, the Court concluded, the school board "could not have realistically supposed that . . . it remained free to retaliate against those who reported sex discrimination."[156](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn156)

While the Court decided *Jackson* in 2005, the case involved an implied private right of action first recognized in 1979—before *Pennhur**s**t*'s clear notice requirement.[157](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn157) *Pennhurst* and its progeny marked a "shift in th[e] Court's approach" away from recognizing implied rights and remedies and toward requiring "'clear and unambiguous' notice" that a spending statute "creates a personally enforceable right."[158](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn158) Accordingly, it is uncertain whether the Court's reasoning in *Jackson*—suggesting that a funding recipient might be on notice of its potential liability under a pre-*Pennhurst* statute based on sources outside of the statute's text—would extend to Spending Clause legislation enacted after *Pennhurst*.

Lower courts appear to be divided over the question of whether clear notice must come directly from a statute or can be supplied through other sources, such as agency regulations or guidance. From 2022 to 2024, three federal courts of appeals held that a funding condition in the American Rescue Plan Act (ARPA) violated the clear notice principle despite agency efforts to clarify the condition.[159](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn159) This "offset" condition prohibited states from using ARPA funds to "directly or indirectly offset" reductions in state tax revenue.[160](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn160) All three courts reasoned that the offset provision in the statute was ambiguous because Congress had not defined what it meant to "indirectly" offset tax revenue and the statute lacked a "baseline" against which to measure any reduction in revenue.[161](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn161) However, the reviewing courts gave different weight to agency regulations issued to clarify these standards. The Fifth Circuit rejected the government's argument that "Treasury's subsequent regulations" cleared up any ambiguity, reasoning that a "statutory ambiguity" cannot be "vitiated by regulatory enactments."[162](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn162) The Eleventh Circuit, in contrast, indicated that regulations can sometimes "fill in gaps that may exist in a spending condition," but that the ARPA did not provide an "ascertainable condition" for Treasury to clarify.[163](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn163) In a different vein, the Sixth Circuit seemed to focus on the significance of tax policy, reasoning that "in these particular circumstances"—where "control over taxation is a core aspect of state sovereignty" and the condition affects the "balance of power between the Federal Government and the States"—Congress itself had to clarify the condition.[164](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn164)

Thus, it is unclear whether or under what circumstances sources outside of the statutory text can supply the requisite clear notice, and different jurisdictions might approach these questions differently. As a result, Congress might opt to address questions about a funding condition's scope and the consequences of noncompliance in statutory text to mitigate potential ambiguity.

### Relatedness

A second judicially recognized limit on Congress's spending power is whether a funding condition is related to a purpose of the funded program or activity. The relatedness requirement reflects the structural decision of the Framers and ratifiers of the Constitution to vest Congress with specific, enumerated powers. The Supreme Court has reasoned that without a relatedness requirement, "the spending power could render academic the Constitution's other grants and limits of federal authority."[165](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn165) In other words, the Spending Clause already allows Congress to fund legislative policies that it could not enact directly because they fall outside of its enumerated powers.[166](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn166) Allowing Congress to impose conditions untethered to the purpose of the spending legislation could enlarge Congress's powers to a degree that encroaches on powers that the Tenth Amendment reserves to the states.[167](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn167) These structural considerations suggest that the relatedness principle, while grounded in federalism concerns, applies regardless of whether Congress seeks to leverage its spending power to change state policies or to influence private behavior.

Analysis of the relatedness requirement in Supreme Court cases is sparse. In *South Dakota v. Dole*, the Court suggested that "federal grants might be illegitimate if they are unrelated 'to the federal interest in particular national projects or programs'" but found no relatedness problem in that case.[168](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn168) The Court held that Congress permissibly conditioned a percentage of federal highway funds on states' adoption of a national minimum drinking age because the condition was "directly related" to the federal government's interest in highway safety.[169](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn169) The *Dole* Court did not need to examine the precise interests served by the individual highway grants tied to the condition, reasoning that a minimum drinking age related to "one of the main purposes for which highway funds are expended—safe interstate travel" and that "varying drinking ages among the States" had "frustrated" this purpose.[170](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn170)

In practice, the relatedness requirement appears to be a "low bar."[171](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn171) The Supreme Court has never struck down a spending condition as insufficiently related to the purpose of the spending.[172](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn172) Appellate courts have not interpreted the relatedness standard to be particularly demanding.[173](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn173) For example, the Ninth Circuit has employed the Supreme Court's description of the relatedness requirement in *New York v. United States* (decided five years after *Dole*), in concluding that "the conditions need only 'bear some relationship to the purpose of the federal spending.'"[174](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn174) Other courts, such as the Tenth Circuit, have described the "required degree" of connection between the condition and the federal interest in the funded program as "one of reasonableness or minimum rationality"—a low standard in legal terms.[175](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn175) At least one Supreme Court Justice, Justice Thomas, may view the relatedness requirement in a potentially more stringent way—at least where the government has invoked the Necessary and Proper Clause—suggesting that "there must be 'some obvious, simple, and direct relation' between the condition and the expenditure of the funds."[176](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn176) The Supreme Court did not purport to alter the relatedness standard in*Landor*,[177](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn177) however, and the Sixth Circuit observed in a 2005 decision involving RLUIPA that "*Dole* remains the controlling law" on the question of relatedness.[178](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn178)

Most of the cases in which courts have identified a relatedness defect have involved conditions imposed by a federal agency, where separation-of-powers concerns were also at play.[179](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn179) According to the courts that issued these rulings, some of the conditions at issue lacked a "reasonable relationship" to the funded activity,[180](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn180) while others conflicted with statutory requirements or purposes of the funded program.[181](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn181) In other cases where courts found a relatedness problem, noncompliance with a particular condition could result in the loss of *all* funding disbursed by the agency, regardless of the relationship between any particular program or funding stream and the condition in question.[182](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn182)

Thus, both as framed by the Supreme Court and as applied by lower courts, the relatedness requirement appears to provide a relatively modest limit on Congress's authority to condition federal funding. Spending conditions that bear a reasonable relationship to the goals or functions of the funded program or activity would likely satisfy this standard, whereas conditions that conflict with applicable statutory requirements or place separate funding streams at risk might not satisfy the relatedness limit.

### Anti-Coercion

The third judicially recognized limit that could arise in a Spending Clause challenge to a funding condition is whether the condition is unconstitutionally coercive. To comply with the anti-coercion limit, which stems from federalism principles, Congress can offer states "powerful incentive[s]" to carry out federal policies as long as states remain free to opt out by declining the federal funding.[183](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn183) Congress may not, however, "compel" states to adopt federal policies due to a related constitutional doctrine called anti-commandeering.[184](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn184)

The anti-commandeering doctrine reflects "a fundamental structural decision" in the Constitution—"the decision to withhold from Congress the power to issue orders directly to the States."[185](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn185) Under the U.S. system of "dual sovereignty," both the federal government and the states "wield sovereign powers."[186](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn186) The Constitution allocates powers between the federal and state governments in a number of ways. The Supremacy Clause of Article VI provides that federal law "shall be the supreme Law of the Land," giving Congress the power to preempt state law.[187](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn187) At the same time, Congress's legislative authority is limited to powers enumerated in the Constitution.[188](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn188) The Tenth Amendment provides that all other legislative powers are "reserved to the States."[189](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn189) The Supreme Court developed the anti-commandeering doctrine to help preserve this structure by prohibiting Congress from commanding states to legislate or regulate—or to refrain from legislating or regulating—on the federal government's behalf.[190](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn190)

In the spending context, these same considerations undergird the anti-coercion analysis,[191](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn191) which essentially asks whether a state has a "legitimate choice" about whether to accept the federal conditions.[192](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn192) As the Supreme Court recognized in *South Dakota v. Dole*, sometimes "the financial inducement offered by Congress might be so coercive as to pass the point at which 'pressure turns into compulsion.'"[193](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn193) On the facts of that case, the *Dole* Court concluded that Congress's incentive for states to adopt the national minimum drinking age was "relatively mild encouragement."[194](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn194) In *Dole*, non-acceptance would cost a state "a relatively small percentage of certain federal highway funds"—in South Dakota's case, "5% of the funds otherwise obtainable under specified highway grant programs."[195](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn195)

While the Supreme Court recognized the potential for coercion in *Dole*, it did not strike down a spending condition on anti-coercion grounds until its 2012 decision in *National Federation of Independent Business (NFIB) v. Sebelius*.[196](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn196) In that case, seven Justices, across two different opinions, concluded that Congress violated the anti-coercion principle by conditioning all of a state's Medicaid funds on its acceptance of Medicaid expansion in the Affordable Care Act.[197](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn197) Chief Justice Roberts authored a plurality opinion on the Spending Clause analysis on behalf of himself and Justices Breyer and Kagan, while four Justices—Justices Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, and Alito—discussed their reasoning in a joint dissent.[198](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn198) Because the plurality concluded that the condition was invalid on narrower grounds than the joint dissent, several appellate courts have described Chief Justice Roberts's opinion as precedential.[199](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn199)

In concluding that the condition in *NFIB* was unconstitutionally coercive, the Chief Justice highlighted the states' dependence on existing Medicaid funding and the expansion's resemblance, in the plurality's view, to a "new health care program."[200](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn200) The plurality observed that federal Medicaid funds had become "a substantial part of state budgets," comprising more than "10 percent of most States' total revenue."[201](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn201) The plurality indicated, by comparison, that the highway funds at issue in *Dole* constituted less than 0.5% of South Dakota's budget.[202](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn202) Echoing the clear notice requirement, the plurality also reasoned that Congress's spending power to enact the Medicaid program did not permit it to "surpris[e] participating States with post-acceptance or 'retroactive' conditions."[203](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn203)

*NFIB* remains the only case in which the Supreme Court has struck down a funding condition as unconstitutionally coercive. Accordingly, case law on the anti-coercion principle is limited, and its applications have occurred primarily in the lower courts.[204](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn204) Judges have used the facts and reasoning of *Dole* and *NFIB* as guideposts in the anti-coercion analysis, asking (1) whether the challenged condition attached to existing funding and marked a change in "degree" or "kind" for the program at issue;[205](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn205) (2) whether the condition took "the form of threats to terminate other significant independent grants";[206](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn206) and (3) what percentage of a state's budget was at risk if the state did not comply with the condition.[207](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn207) Courts have sometimes adapted the last factor for challenges by localities or public institutions by examining the percentage of federal funding that a particular recipient could lose,[208](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn208) or how the funding loss would affect the entity's overall budget or operations for the federally funded program or activity at issue.[209](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn209) Additionally, some lower courts have questioned whether the anti-coercion limit applies to conditions on how states *use* funds, as opposed to conditions designed to induce states to make policy changes—something that Congress could not command directly because of the anti-commandeering doctrine.[210](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn210) For example, the Fifth Circuit reasoned that a "direct restriction on how a state uses federal funds" is not coercive, because it simply "'ensures that the funds are spent according to [Congress's] view of the general Welfare.'"[211](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn211)

Because the anti-coercion doctrine has its roots in federalism principles, at least one federal court has questioned whether a private party can raise an anti-coercion claim. In that case, private, long-term care facilities challenged certain Medicare and Medicaid requirements involving arbitration agreements with residents.[212](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn212) In a ruling affirmed on other grounds, the district court concluded that the plaintiffs' asserted dependence on Medicare and Medicaid funding was of their own making, stating that "Courts of Appeals have held time and time again that the participation of private entities in Medicare and Medicaid is always voluntary."[213](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn213) The district court also observed that "no part" of the *NFIB* decision "touched on the government's power to place conditions on private entities."[214](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn214) On appeal, the Eighth Circuit found it unnecessary to decide the question but appeared to agree with the district court that the anti-coercion discussion in *NFIB* concerned "the federal government's limited constitutional authority under the Spending Clause to regulate the states, not a federal agency's ability to regulate [private] facilities' use of federal funding."[215](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn215)

Thus, though the anti-coercion doctrine is a recognized limit on Congress's power to condition federal funding to state recipients, the line between permissible incentive and unconstitutional coercion remains blurry. Questions also remain as to the doctrine's application to conditional funding to private entities and conditions directed at the use of funds rather than the policies or conduct of the recipient.

### Independent Constitutional Bar

A spending condition that meets the clear notice, relatedness, and anti-coercion tests is constitutional unless it violates some other "independent constitutional bar."[216](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn216) There are two general formulations of the independent constitutional bar principle. Additionally, arguments about the constitutionality of a funding condition often implicate a cross-cutting legal doctrine called the "unconstitutional conditions" doctrine.[217](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn217)

#### Two General Formulations

As articulated in *South Dakota v. Dole*, a funding condition runs afoul of an independent constitutional bar when it would require or encourage the recipient to violate the Constitution.[218](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn218) To use an example from *Dole*, if Congress were to condition funding for state prisons on the use of a "cruel and unusual punishment[]," the Eighth Amendment would independently bar such a condition because it prohibits states from using such forms of punishment.[219](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn219) The Supreme Court considered—and ultimately rejected—an independent constitutional bar argument of this nature in *United States v. American Library Association*.[220](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn220) In the Children's Internet Protection Act, Congress had required public libraries receiving federal assistance for internet and information services to install filtering software on library computers to block patrons' access to pornographic websites and other materials deemed "harmful to minors."[221](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn221) Challengers to the law contended that this condition required public libraries—which are governmental entities—to violate the First Amendment rights of library users.[222](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn222) Six Justices, for different reasons, concluded that the law did not induce the libraries to violate their patrons' First Amendment rights, and the condition for receiving federal funds was therefore within Congress's power.[223](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn223)

Some courts and litigants appear to take a broader view of the independent constitutional bar limitation than the *Dole* formulation, treating it as a catchall for analyzing not only whether a condition induces the recipient to violate the Constitution, but also whether the condition *itself* exceeds Congress's authority. For example, according to the Eighth Circuit, this principle "requires a consideration of whether other constitutional provisions prohibit these particular conditions on federal funding."[224](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn224) States have sometimes invoked the "independent constitutional bar" concept to raise federalism-based objections, such as the argument that a funding condition violates states' rights under the Tenth Amendment.[225](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn225) However, because federalism considerations are already embedded in the clear notice, relatedness, and anti-coercion principles,[226](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn226) other courts have concluded that the Tenth Amendment "does not apply" to otherwise "valid spending legislation" that clears these initial hurdles.[227](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn227) Still other courts have analyzed Tenth Amendment objections as separate constitutional claims.[228](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn228)

In sum, under the *Dole* formulation, the independent constitutional bar principle concerns conditions that induce a state or other *governmental* entity to violate the Constitution.[229](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn229) Under a broader formulation used by some courts, and the closely related unconstitutional conditions doctrine discussed below, Congress also may not use its spending power in ways that violate a *private* recipient's constitutional rights.[230](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn230) Thus, a relevant consideration in formulating any funding condition—whether the intended recipients are public or private entities—is whether the condition might violate, or cause another governmental entity to violate, a constitutional provision other than the Spending Clause itself.

#### Unconstitutional Conditions Doctrine

The unconstitutional conditions doctrine "examines the extent to which government benefits may be conditioned or distributed in ways that burden constitutional rights or principles."[231](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn231) A core principle of this doctrine is that once the government has established a benefit, it may not deny that benefit to a person "on a basis that infringes his constitutionally protected interests."[232](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn232)

The unconstitutional conditions doctrine is not unique to the funding context. In contrast to the four limits discussed above, the doctrine has developed as a constraint on Congress's authority generally, rather than the Spending Clause alone, and it has generated a large body of case law that touches on a variety of Congress's powers.[233](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn233) Still, the unconstitutional conditions doctrine frequently arises in cases involving funding conditions, especially conditions that restrict or compel speech. In such circumstances, the Supreme Court generally has upheld conditions that "define the limits of the government spending program—those that specify the activities Congress wants to subsidize," but it has applied heightened scrutiny to conditions that "seek to leverage funding" in a way that burdens constitutional rights "outside the contours of the program itself."[234](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn234)

Cases applying this test illustrate why the distinction is "not always self-evident."[235](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn235) In *Rust v. Sullivan*, the Court upheld regulations prohibiting recipients of funding for family planning projects under Title X of the Public Health Service Act from providing abortion services, counseling, or referrals, or advocating for abortion through their written materials, speakers, or lobbying efforts.[236](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn236) Grantees had to maintain physical and financial separation, as defined in the regulations, between the Title X project and any prohibited abortion-related activities.[237](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn237) In the Court's view, the regulations did not amount to "suppress[ion]" of abortion-related expression, but instead prohibited "a project grantee or its employees from engaging in activities outside of the project's scope," consistent with Title X's focus on family planning rather than prenatal care.[238](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn238)

The Court also upheld a statutory funding condition against an unconstitutional conditions challenge in *American Library Association*.[239](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn239) As previously described, that case involved a requirement that public libraries implement filtering software in exchange for funds to improve internet services.[240](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn240) The Court concluded that the condition did not violate the libraries' First Amendment rights.[241](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn241) A plurality of the Court reasoned that, as in *Rust*, the condition defined the limits of the funded program.[242](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn242) By blocking online materials that libraries traditionally would have excluded from their print collections, the Court reasoned, filtering software helps libraries to "fulfill their traditional role of obtaining material of requisite and appropriate quality for educational and informational purposes."[243](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn243)

By contrast, the Court found it unconstitutional for Congress to require the recipients of funding for global HIV/AIDS programs to have "a policy explicitly opposing prostitution and sex trafficking."[244](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn244) The Court reasoned that the condition exceeded the program's scope because it forced granteesto "adopt—as their own—the Government's view on an issue of public concern," which necessarily required fidelity to that view inside and outside of the program.[245](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn245) It did not matter that the federal government allowed funding recipients to work with organizations that did not have the specified policy so long as the recipients retained "objective integrity and independence from any affiliated organization."[246](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn246) The Court reasoned that this approach required the recipient to either distance itself from its affiliate and the affiliate's message, or clearly identify with its affiliate while espousing the government's message "only at the price of evident hypocrisy."[247](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn247)

Thus, the unconstitutional conditions doctrine serves as a check on Congress's authority to condition federal funding in a way that burdens constitutional rights outside the contours of the government spending program at issue, though where that line is drawn is not always clear.

## Executive Branch Conditions

In many of the cases discussed above, Congress has imposed a spending condition by statute, and courts have considered whether that condition is constitutional. A spending condition might also come from the executive branch—for example, from a federal agency administering a grant program. If the condition is challenged in a lawsuit, a court might consider whether the executive branch has statutory authority from Congress to impose the condition, and if so, whether that condition is consistent with federal statutes and with the Constitution. Some cases also involve threshold questions about the court's jurisdiction.

### Jurisdictional Questions

Litigants challenging an agency's decision to adopt or enforce a particular funding condition often bring claims under the APA.[248](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn248) Pursuant to the APA, a reviewing court can set aside federal agency action that is "in excess of statutory jurisdiction [or] authority," "contrary to constitutional right," or "arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law."[249](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn249) The APA does not authorize money damages and does not authorize a court to "grant relief if any other statute that grants consent to suit expressly or impliedly forbids the relief which is sought."[250](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn250) The Supreme Court has interpreted this provision to preclude a federal district court from hearing APA claims involving grant terminations where the plaintiff seeks "to enforce a contractual obligation to pay money."[251](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn251) According to the Court, a federal statute called the Tucker Act divests district courts of APA jurisdiction in these circumstances, because "the Tucker Act grants the Court of Federal Claims jurisdiction over suits based on 'any express or implied contract with the United States.'"[252](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn252)

The Supreme Court addressed this jurisdictional issue in two rulings on its interim (i.e., nonmerits) docket.[253](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn253) Neither decision squarely addresses whether or in what circumstances a district court may hear an APA challenge to a funding condition—though the views of individual Justices provide insight into the question. In the first case, *Department of Education v. California*, a federal district court had, on APA grounds, ordered the government to resume payments under certain previously terminated education grants.[254](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn254) In a per curiam (i.e., unsigned) order, the Supreme Court concluded that the government was "likely to succeed in showing the District Court lacked jurisdiction to order the payment of money under the APA," because its order amounted to "'enforc[ing] a contractual obligation to pay money'"—a matter that the Tucker Act assigns to the Court of Federal Claims.[255](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn255)

The second case, *N**ational Institutes of Health* *v. American Public Health* *Ass'n* *(APHA)*, involved the federal government's termination of certain research grants.[256](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn256) A federal district court had vacated the grant terminations and certain agency directives on APA grounds.[257](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn257) In an emergency application, the government asked the Supreme Court to stay the district court's orders while it appealed those rulings. With four Justices in favor of granting the application in full and four that would have denied it in full, the Court agreed to stay only the portions of the district court's orders setting aside the grant terminations.[258](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn258) In a concurrence that some courts have come to view as the "controlling" rationale,[259](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn259) Justice Barrett posited that "the District Court likely lacked jurisdiction to hear challenges to the grant terminations, which belong in the Court of Federal Claims (CFC)," but likely had jurisdiction to hear the plaintiff's APA challenge to "internal agency guidance."[260](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn260) Justice Barrett reasoned that discussion of "internal policies related to grants" in agency guidance "does not transform a challenge to that guidance into a claim 'founded upon' contract that only the CFC can hear."[261](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn261) Four Justices—Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, and Jackson—issued a partial concurrence reasoning that the district court had jurisdiction to set aside the challenged agency directives as well as the grant terminations themselves.[262](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn262) They reasoned that vacating the directives "has prospective and generally applicable implications beyond the reinstatement of specific grants" and that the government did not contest that the grant terminations resulted from the directives.[263](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn263) Justices Gorsuch and Kavanaugh, who thought the entire case should have been routed to the CFC, believed that the Court's interim ruling in *Department of Education v. California* addressed the jurisdictional question.[264](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn264) They remarked that "even probabilistic holdings," such as the Court's "top-line conclusion" about APA jurisdiction in that case, "must 'inform how a [lower] court' proceeds 'in like cases.'''[265](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn265)

Following these decisions, some lower courts expressed uncertainty over the scope of APA jurisdiction in challenges to agency actions involving grants.[266](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn266) The *California* action resulted in the "two-track litigation" described by Justice Barrett in her *APHA* concurrence.[267](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn267) Specifically, the federal district court in *California* held that it lacked jurisdiction over "retrospective" claims seeking the reinstatement of terminated grants but had jurisdiction over "prospective" claims asking the court to set aside certain agency actions as procedurally defective under the APA or unconstitutional.[268](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn268) In a similar vein, the First Circuit upheld a district court's award of prospective injunctive relief, even though it might "result in the disbursement of funds," while vacating parts of the order directing the agency to provide previously awarded funding.[269](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn269) Several courts confronted with these types of jurisdictional questions after *California* and *APHA* have continued to apply a "longstanding test"[270](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn270) developed by the D.C. Circuit that examines both "the source of the rights upon which the plaintiff bases its claims" and "the type of relief sought," finding that standard compatible with the Supreme Court's interim rulings.[271](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn271)

### Statutory Authority and the Separation of Powers

Because Congress alone holds the "power of the purse," the Constitution does not authorize the executive branch to unilaterally spend or impose conditions on federal funding.[272](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn272) To do so would violate the constitutional separation of powers.[273](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn273) A funding condition from the executive branch can be constitutional only if Congress has "delegated authority" to the executive branch to impose that condition.[274](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn274)

Because an "agency that acts without statutory authority violates the [APA],"[275](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn275) challenges to an agency's decision to adopt or enforce a particular funding condition often involve APA claims instead of, or in addition to, a separation-of-powers claim.[276](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn276) Courts presented with both types of claims might resolve the challenge on statutory rather than constitutional grounds, sometimes as a matter of constitutional avoidance[277](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn277)—the principle that courts should refrain from adjudicating a constitutional question if it can be resolved on other, narrower grounds.[278](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn278) Some case law has suggested, however, that when an agency imposes a spending condition that Congress has not authorized, it violates not only the underlying statute but also the Constitution.[279](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn279)

Questions regarding an agency's statutory authority to adopt a particular funding condition can involve complex questions of statutory interpretation, as illustrated by litigation involving immigration-related conditions in the Edward Byrne Memorial Justice Assistance Grant (Byrne JAG) Program. The Byrne JAG Program is "the primary source of federal criminal justice funding available to state and local governments."[280](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn280) Administered by the Department of Justice (DOJ), the program provides federal grants to states and localities "to provide additional personnel, equipment, supplies, contractual support, training, technical assistance, and information systems for criminal justice" in eleven program areas.[281](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn281) While a statutorily prescribed formula dictates the amount of funding that each state receives, states still must apply for Byrne JAG funding.[282](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn282) The application must include, among other items, a "certification, made in a form acceptable to the Attorney General" that "the applicant will comply with all provisions" of the Byrne JAG statute[283](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn283) and "*all other applicable Federal laws*."[284](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn284)

The Byrne JAG cases discussed in this section concerned grant conditions that the Attorney General adopted for fiscal years 2017 and 2018.[285](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn285) One of the challenged conditions—the certification condition—required grantees to certify their compliance with Title 8, Section 1373, of the *U.S. Code*.[286](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn286) Section 1373 prohibits states and localities from restricting the exchange of information about an individual's citizenship or immigration status with federal immigration authorities.[287](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn287) In the litigation, DOJ argued that the Byrne JAG statute authorized the condition because the statute itself requires applicants to certify compliance with "applicable Federal laws" and, in DOJ's view, Section 1373 was one such law.[288](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn288)

The First, Third, and Seventh Circuits disagreed, holding that DOJ's grant condition was inconsistent with the Byrne JAG statute.[289](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn289) They construed "applicable Federal laws" to refer to laws that apply to Byrne JAG grant *program**s*, rather than all laws that apply to Byrne JAG *applicants*.[290](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn290) Because Section 1373 generally applied to state and local governments and officials without referencing grant programs or federal funding, the courts reasoned that it was not an "applicable" federal law.[291](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn291) Accordingly, these three circuits held that the Attorney General exceeded his statutory authority in imposing the certification condition.[292](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn292)

One court, the Seventh Circuit, also characterized this as a constitutional violation. The Seventh Circuit expressly held that, because of the lack of statutory authorization, "the Attorney General's decision to attach the conditions to the FY2017 and FY2018 Byrne JAG grants violated the constitutional principle of separation of powers."[293](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn293)

In contrast, in a case brought by seven states and New York City, the Second Circuit held that the Attorney General neither exceeded his statutory authority nor violated the constitutional separation of powers by imposing the certification condition.[294](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn294) The court reasoned that the Byrne JAG statute authorized the Attorney General to identify "other applicable Federal laws" for purposes of the compliance certification by mandating certification in a "form acceptable to the Attorney General."[295](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn295) In the court's view, "form" embraced both format and content.[296](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn296) The court also construed the phrase "applicable Federal laws" to refer to laws applying to the grant program *and* laws applying to the applicant.[297](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn297) The court concluded that Section 1373 applied to the plaintiff-jurisdictions that had applied for Byrne JAG grants.[298](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn298) Even if Congress did not make the condition explicit, therefore, DOJ's condition fell within the discretionary authority that Congress had given to DOJ to specify certification requirements for the Byrne JAG program.[299](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn299)

Although the plaintiff-jurisdictions initially sought the Supreme Court's review, the parties agreed to dismiss the petition after the DOJ "agreed to remove the challenged conditions, allowing" the plaintiffs "to access their withheld grant funds."[300](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn300) While these lawsuits over the FY2017 and FY2018 Byrne JAG conditions have concluded, immigration-related conditions in other grant programs have generated additional litigation.[301](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn301)

Congress has several options to address agency-imposed conditions such as the immigration-related terms in the Byrne JAG funding discussed above. Congress has the option to authorize an agency to impose certain types of conditions for particular federal programs. The Supreme Court observed in *Biden v. Missouri*, for example, that "Congress authorized the Secretary [of HHS] to promulgate, as a condition of a facility's participation in the [Medicare or Medicaid] programs, such 'requirements as [the Secretary] finds necessary in the interest of the health and safety of individuals who are furnished services in the institution" (e.g., hospital).[302](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn302) Congress could also clarify, on a program-by-program or appropriation-by-appropriation basis, the executive branch's authority to require funding recipients to comply with statutory requirements that are not expressly identified in the relevant funding statutes. For example, if Congress were to seek to make compliance with Section 1373 an ongoing condition of Byrne JAG funding, it could amend the Byrne JAG statute to include an explicit condition to that effect. Congress would also have the option of providing appropriations for a program that condition a third party's receipt of those funds on their complying with one or more statutory requirements. For example, in 2025, Congress provided appropriations for certain purposes under the Byrne JAG program and expressly made compliance with Section 1373 a condition of eligibility to receive those funds.[303](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn303) The constitutional limits discussed in the second part of this report (i.e., clear notice, relatedness, anti-coercion, and independent constitutional bar) would apply to each of these legislative approaches.[304](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn304)

If instead Congress were to seek to limit the executive branch's authority to impose funding conditions or interpret spending legislation to require compliance with other laws as a condition of funding, it could consider targeted amendments to address particular executive branch actions or interpretations. For example, Congress could amend language in the program statutes that the executive branch has relied upon to adopt funding conditions, such as the "all other applicable Federal laws" phrase in the Byrne JAG statute.[305](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn305) Additionally or alternatively, Congress could specify the kinds of conditions the agency could implement or prohibit the agency from adopting conditions other than those explicitly identified in the relevant funding statutes.

Congress could also expand or limit the executive branch's authority to establish procedures for grantmaking agencies, including procedures related to implementing new funding conditions or addressing noncompliance with existing conditions.[306](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn306)

### Spending Clause Considerations

Even if an agency is statutorily authorized to adopt a particular funding condition or exercise discretion in interpreting its scope, that condition must still comply with the Spending Clause limits discussed in the second part of this report.[307](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn307) Courts have vacated or enjoined the enforcement of funding conditions, as adopted or interpreted by the executive branch, that they have found to violate the clear notice,[308](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn308) relatedness,[309](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn309) anti-coercion,[310](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn310) or independent constitutional bar[311](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-07_R46827_12c077ffb9f082f707a5e37005d612709396001f.html#fn311) principles. Thus, these four constitutional limits apply regardless of whether a funding condition originates in a statute, executive order, agency regulation, guidance document, or funding agreement.

## Conclusion

Congress has broad constitutional authority to authorize spending for the general welfare. In exercising that authority, Congress may place conditions on federal funding given to states and private entities. Courts reviewing the constitutionality of a condition generally assess whether the condition (1) provides *clear notice* to the funding recipient so that the recipient can *knowingly and voluntarily* accept the terms of the funding arrangement, (2) is *reasonably related* to the program or funding stream it restricts, (3) is *not coercive*, and (4) does not induce the recipient to violate an *independent constitutional bar*. While many of these principles are rooted in federalism concerns, taken together, they cabin Congress's authority to condition funding to states and private entities. Statutory requirements concerning the funding at issue, and the degree of discretion Congress has accorded the executive branch, may affect the legality of any executive order, agency rule, or other executive branch action that imposes or enforces a spending condition.
