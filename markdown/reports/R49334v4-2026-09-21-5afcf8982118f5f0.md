## Summary

In 2021, Section 80603 of the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (IIJA, Pub. L. No. 117-58) defined the term "digital asset" in the Internal Revenue Code (IRC) for tax purposes. Several years before the codified tax definition, however, the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) had begun issuing guidance on how cryptocurrency should be treated for tax purposes. The IRS treats digital assets as property, which means that the tax treatment of digital assets is generally guided by the tax principles applicable to property transactions. These principles cover sales, exchanges, the timing of income recognition, substantiation, and valuation.

Detailed judicial decisions and administrative guidance discussing the tax treatment of digital assets remain limited. Since the enactment of the IIJA, federal taxing authorities have concentrated on providing stakeholders with significant digital asset reporting compliance resources. Federal materials that focus on the tax consequences of digital asset transactions have largely addressed foundational concepts and been preliminary. When detailed tax guidance exists, it has generally been fact-specific and limited in scope. Judicial decisions concerning digital assets exhibit similar constraints, which might limit broader applications.

The few detailed decisions and administrative materials on digital assets taxation often turn on a digital asset's characteristics and the facts and circumstances surrounding a particular digital asset transaction. Courts and federal taxing authorities typically look for analogies among traditional asset classes and transactions to assess whether similar tax treatment is warranted. Courts have issued decisions and the IRS has issued guidance and rulings on the tax treatment of digital assets in specific contexts, such as hard forks, airdrops, mining, staking, and non-fungible tokens (NFTs).

Federal lawmakers have proposed legislation aimed at clarifying the existing tax treatment of digital assets and altering the tax treatment of digital assets in certain types of transactions. As digital asset transactions continue to evolve, Congress may consider whether the existing statutory tax framework adequately captures revenue from digital asset transactions.

## Introduction

In 2021, the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (IIJA) amended the Internal Revenue Code (IRC) to address tax information reporting for "digital asset" transactions.[1](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn1) The IIJA added IRC § 6045(g)(3)(D), which defines a digital asset as "any digital representation of value which is recorded on a cryptographically secured distributed ledger or any similar technology as specified by the Secretary" of the Department of the Treasury (Treasury).[2](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn2) Several years before the IIJA, the Internal Revenue Service (IRS), a bureau within Treasury, began to issue guidance on the tax treatment of cryptocurrency.[3](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn3)

The IRS has described cryptocurrency as a form of virtual currency that uses cryptographic protocols to "secure transactions that are digitally recorded" via distributed ledger technology, such as a blockchain.[4](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn4) A single unit of cryptocurrency is commonly called a coin or token.[5](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn5) "Distributed ledger technology uses independent digital systems [(i.e., nodes)] to record, share, and synchronize transactions" across multiple locations simultaneously, operating without central data storage or administrative authority.[6](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn6) Because a distributed ledger does not have a central database or administrator, a consensus mechanism ensures the validity of the distributed ledger.[7](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn7) "A consensus mechanism is a set of protocols" that nodes use to reach agreement on the validity of transactions and distributed ledger updates.[8](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn8) A distributed ledger can identify the owner of fungible tokens (e.g., cryptocurrency) and non-fungible tokens (i.e., NFTs).[9](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn9)

In IRS Notice 2014-21, the IRS responded to some preliminary questions about cryptocurrency, including by stating that cryptocurrency would be treated as property, instead of currency, for federal tax purposes.[10](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn10) The IRS expanded on Notice 2014-21 by addressing the receipt of cryptocurrency in specific transactions.[11](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn11) In Revenue Ruling 2019-24, the IRS used foundational tax principles to determine when the receipt of cryptocurrency constituted "gross income."[12](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn12) The IRS has also answered frequently asked questions about virtual currency transactions on its website, expanding on Notice 2014-21.[13](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn13)

Following the IIJA's enactment, Treasury and the IRS continued to issue guidance and rulings addressing the tax treatment of digital assets.[14](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn14) In Revenue Ruling 2023-14, the IRS addressed when a taxpayer recognizes income from staking—a process used in a proof-of-stake consensus mechanism where a taxpayer offers tokens as collateral to validate transactions on a distributed ledger and receives validation rewards in exchange.[15](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn15) In IRS Notice 2023-27, the IRS informed taxpayers that it intended to issue guidance to address when an individual retirement account's acquisition of an NFT would be treated as an acquisition of a collectible under IRC § 408(m), which can result in a taxable distribution.[16](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn16)

Over the same period, the U.S. Tax Court—a specialized court created under Article I of the Constitution to adjudicate federal tax matters expressly provided by statute—has established that taxpayers realize income from the sale and disposition of digital assets based on IRC § 61 and existing case law.[17](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn17) Article III courts have issued opinions and orders discussing the tax treatment of staking rewards.[18](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn18) In 2026, the Tax Court issued a memorandum holding that a cash-method taxpayer's staking rewards were includible upon receipt.[19](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn19)

## Tax Principles Generally Applicable to Property

IRS Notice 2014-21 outlines the general tax principles applicable to convertible virtual currency transactions.[20](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn20) The notice only identifies Bitcoin as a convertible virtual currency and states that "[n]o inference should be drawn with respect to virtual currencies not described in this notice."[21](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn21) The notice explains Bitcoin is a convertible virtual currency because it "can be digitally traded . . . and can be purchased . . . or exchanged [for] U.S. dollars, Euros, and other real or virtual currencies."[22](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn22)

In Notice 2014-21, the IRS first announced that it would treat virtual currencies as property, meaning that the general tax principles that apply to property transactions apply to virtual currencies.[23](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn23) While this notice did not address several of the issues surrounding the tax consequences of virtual currency transactions, it did clarify that the IRS would treat convertible virtual currencies as property, not currency.[24](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn24) As a result of this property treatment, the use of digital assets as payment for goods or services or the receipt of digital assets for goods or the performance of services can generate tax liability.[25](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn25)

Pursuant to IRC § 61(a)(3), gains from dealings in property are includible in gross income and thus may be subject to tax.[26](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn26) By default, gain from the sale or exchange of property is ordinary in character unless a special rule applies.[27](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn27) Under IRC § 1011, a taxpayer's adjusted basis for determining the gain or loss from the sale or disposition of property is generally the cost basis determined under IRC § 1012 adjusted as provided under IRC § 1016.[28](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn28) Unless otherwise provided in the IRC, if a taxpayer receives property that is not purchased (e.g., exchanged), the taxpayer's basis in the property is determined by the amount included in gross income, which is the fair market value of the property when it was received.[29](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn29)

The taxable year that a taxpayer includes an amount in gross income depends on whether the taxpayer uses a cash method of accounting or an accrual method of accounting.[30](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn30) A taxpayer using the cash-basis method of accounting includes the amount of any item, such as gain or profit, in gross income in the taxable year in which it is actually or constructively received.[31](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn31) A taxpayer using the accrual method of accounting includes an amount of gross income when "all the events have occurred which fix the right to receive such income and the amount of such income can be determined with reasonable accuracy."[32](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn32)

## Hard Forks and Airdrops

Revenue Ruling 2019-24 addresses two situations involving a hard fork of cryptocurrency.[33](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn33) A hard fork occurs when a cryptocurrency on a distributed ledger[34](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn34) undergoes a protocol change causing a permanent division from the legacy or existing distributed ledger.[35](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn35) A hard fork can produce a new cryptocurrency on a new distributed ledger.[36](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn36) After a hard fork, new cryptocurrency transactions are recorded on the new distributed ledger and legacy transactions are recorded on the legacy distributed ledger.[37](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn37)

An airdrop may follow a hard fork.[38](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn38) The IRS describes an airdrop as the distribution of tokens "to the distributed ledger addresses of multiple taxpayers."[39](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn39) When an airdrop follows a hard fork, units (or tokens)[40](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn40) of the new cryptocurrency are distributed to addresses containing the legacy cryptocurrency.[41](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn41) According to the IRS, a taxpayer receives the new cryptocurrency in an airdrop following a hard fork when a taxpayer can exercise "dominion and control" over the cryptocurrency.[42](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn42) Generally, this is the date and time the airdrop is recorded on the distributed ledger.[43](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn43) That said, under the rule of constructive receipt, a taxpayer may constructively receive income when the taxpayer has the "ability to transfer, sell, exchange, or otherwise dispose of the cryptocurrency."[44](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn44) Therefore, a taxpayer may constructively receive cryptocurrency prior to the date and time an airdrop is recorded on a distributed ledger.[45](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn45) A taxpayer may receive cryptocurrency after the date and time of the airdrop if the cryptocurrency is airdropped in a cryptocurrency exchange managed wallet and the cryptocurrency exchange "does not support the newly-created cryptocurrency such that the airdropped cryptocurrency is not immediately credited to the taxpayer's account" at the exchange.[46](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn46) The taxpayer receives the cryptocurrency if and when the taxpayer can "transfer, sell, exchange, or otherwise dispose of the cryptocurrency."[47](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn47)

In Revenue Ruling 2019-24 situation one, a taxpayer owns cryptocurrency that experiences a hard fork, resulting in the creation of a new cryptocurrency.[48](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn48) The new cryptocurrency is not airdropped or otherwise transferred to the taxpayer's account or controlled by the taxpayer.[49](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn49) The IRS ruled that the taxpayer in situation one does not have gross income under IRC § 61 following the hard fork because the taxpayer did not receive units of the new cryptocurrency.[50](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn50)

Like situation one, in situation two, a taxpayer owns cryptocurrency that experiences a hard fork, resulting in the creation of a new cryptocurrency.[51](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn51) As a result of the taxpayer owning legacy cryptocurrency, new cryptocurrency is airdropped to the taxpayer on Date 1, and the taxpayer can immediately dispose of the new cryptocurrency.[52](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn52) The new cryptocurrency is recorded on the distributed ledger on Date 1 at Time 1.[53](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn53) The IRS concluded that in situation two, the taxpayer has gross income under IRC § 61 because of the taxpayer's ability to immediately dispose of the new cryptocurrency received from the airdrop that followed the hard fork.[54](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn54)

## Mining and Staking

In *Pa**schall v.* *Commissioner*, the Tax Courtfound that blockchains "typically" use two consensus mechanisms to verify transactions and issue new tokens: proof of work and proof of stake.[55](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn55) In a proof-of-work consensus mechanism, "miners" compete via running intensive mathematical computations to form a new block of pending transactions that will be added to the blockchain.[56](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn56) By comparison, in a proof-of-stake consensus mechanism, an algorithm selects a "validator" based on staked cryptocurrency and other factors to propose the next block of transactions to be added to the blockchain.[57](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn57)

### Mining

IRS Notice 2014-21 established a framework for the tax treatment of mining.[58](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn58) A miner solves "complex mathematical problems relating to recent unverified transactions."[59](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn59) When a miner solves a problem, other nodes review the miner's solution to verify that the miner has reached the correct solution.[60](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn60) If the miner's solution is verified as correct, then the miner receives new tokens of the same type as a reward.[61](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn61)

In Notice 2014-21, the IRS concluded that taxpayers who successfully "mine[]" must include in their gross income the fair market value of the new virtual currency they receive on the date of receipt.[62](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn62) Furthermore, if the taxpayer is in the trade or business of mining, and the taxpayer is not participating in the mining activity as an employee, then the net earnings from the self-employment activity is self-employment income and subject to self-employment taxes.[63](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn63)

### Staking

In a proof-of-stake consensus mechanism, an algorithm will select validators to confirm new blocks to the blockchain.[64](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn64) To become a validator, a token holder must "stake" a minimum number of tokens as collateral.[65](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn65) A token holder may also participate in the proof-of-stake validation process by joining a staking pool with other token holders to meet the staking threshold.[66](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn66) An algorithm selects a validator based on "quantity of tokens staked, their tenure as validators, or luck."[67](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn67) If a validator succeeds in validating a transaction on the blockchain, the validator receives rewards of the same token.[68](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn68) If the validator "dishonestly or incorrectly validate[s] transactions," the validator risks forfeiting staked tokens.[69](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn69)

Revenue Ruling 2023-14 addressed the tax treatment of a cash-method taxpayer's "validation rewards," including when the value of the rewards was includible in gross income.[70](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn70) The taxpayer owned units of a cryptocurrency that was a convertible virtual currency.[71](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn71) The taxpayer received additional units of the cryptocurrency as a reward for staking units of the cryptocurrency and validating transactions on the cryptocurrency's blockchain.[72](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn72) The IRS concluded that the fair market value of the validation rewards was included in the taxpayer's gross income in the taxable year in which the taxpayer exercised "dominion and control" over the validation rewards.[73](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn73) The IRS also ruled that the fair market value of the validation rewards was determined as of the date and time the taxpayer gained dominion and control over rewards.[74](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn74) The taxpayer exercised dominion and control over the validation rewards when the taxpayer had the ability to sell, exchange, or dispose of them.[75](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn75) The revenue ruling further explained that the same tax treatment would apply if the taxpayer staked the cryptocurrency through a cryptocurrency exchange and "receive[d] additional units of cryptocurrency as rewards as a result of the validation."[76](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn76)

Relying on Revenue Ruling 2023-14, in IRS Chief Counsel Advice 202444009, the IRS concluded that a cash-method taxpayer who had received rewards in the form of digital assets from staking and other activities had to include the fair market value of those digital assets in gross income, even though the platform where the digital assets were held had filed a Chapter 11 bankruptcy petition and had frozen its customers' accounts.[77](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn77) The platform's user agreement provided that rewards would be distributed to the taxpayer's account "following any applicable lockup or waiting period" and the rewards were the taxpayer's property once credited to the taxpayer's account.[78](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn78) When the rewards were credited to the taxpayer's account, the taxpayer could "sell, exchange, or transfer the rewards."[79](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn79) The taxpayer's digital asset rewards were credited to the taxpayer's account before the Chapter 11 bankruptcy petition and the freezing of customer accounts.[80](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn80) The IRS determined that the user agreement established that the taxpayer had gross income from the credited rewards because the taxpayer had dominion and control over the rewarded digital assets before the platform filed for bankruptcy and froze his account.[81](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn81)

On June 4, 2026, in *Paschall*, the Tax Court ruled that the income attributable to cryptocurrency staking rewards was includible in gross income upon receipt.[82](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn82) The Tax Court rejected the married taxpayers' arguments that staking rewards should be treated like stock dividends or self-created property and subject to tax upon disposition.[83](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn83) The Tax Court expressly stated that its decision did not rest on Revenue Ruling 2023-14.[84](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn84)

In *Paschall*, the husband held Cardano, a cryptocurrency using a proof-of-stake consensus protocol to validate a distributed ledger.[85](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn85) He held his Cardano in an account (a hosted wallet) with the digital assets trading platform eToro.[86](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn86) On October 1, 2020, the platform announced a staking service.[87](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn87) As part of the staking service, by default, a customer's Cardano tokens were staked.[88](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn88) Customers using the staking service retained ownership over their Cardano tokens and received staking rewards in "proportion to the number of tokens they held in their eToro accounts" reduced by a platform fee.[89](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn89) The fee fluctuated between 10% to 25% of staking rewards depending on a customer's membership level.[90](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn90)

Because the husband in *Paschall* did not opt out of the platform's staking service, his Cardano tokens were staked for all of the 2021 tax year.[91](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn91) Each month, the husband received Cardano tokens as staking rewards in his platform account.[92](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn92) The parties stipulated that, in 2021, the husband received $33,354 in Cardano tokens as staking rewards in his platform account.[93](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn93) The husband did not have to take any action to accept the Cardano tokens that he was rewarded, and the tokens were "indistinguishable from the existing tokens in his account."[94](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn94)

On November 23, 2021, the trading platform notified the husband that, in early 2022, it would be delisting Cardano from the platform.[95](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn95) From November 23, 2021, to the end of 2021, the platform "restricted [the husband's] ability to transfer his Cardano tokens to another account or platform."[96](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn96) The husband could still sell his Cardano tokens during this period, but he decided against it.[97](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn97) The husband transferred his Cardano tokens to a different platform the following year.[98](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn98)

The trading platform issued the husband a 2021 Form 1099-MISC reporting $33,354 of other income "attributable to the staking rewards."[99](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn99) In 2023, the IRS issued the married taxpayers a notice showing an income tax deficiency of $24,599 for the 2021 tax year and an accuracy related penalty of $4,920 under IRC § 6662(a).[100](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn100)

In *Paschall*, the question before the Tax Court was whether the husband's staking rewards were taxable upon receipt in 2021.[101](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn101) In reaching its decision, the Tax Court referenced caselaw and IRS revenue rulings and notices.[102](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn102) The Tax Court first decided that "[c]ryptocurrency [wa]s treated as property for federal income tax purposes."[103](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn103) The Tax Court acknowledged the IRS's conclusions in Revenue Ruling 2023-14—validation rewards for staked cryptocurrency were includible in a cash-method taxpayer's gross income when the taxpayer had dominion and control over the validation rewards and the amount included was the fair market value of the validation rewards on the date received.[104](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn104) Despite referencing Revenue Ruling 2023-14, the Tax Court stated that its holding did not "rest" on the revenue ruling.[105](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn105) The court remarked that the taxpayers' position was "doom[ed]" by IRC Section 61 and associated caselaw.[106](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn106)

In *Paschall*, the taxpayers made several arguments for why the staking rewards were not includible in gross income upon receipt.[107](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn107) The taxpayers contended that (1) the platform's restrictions prevented the husband from having dominion and control over the staked tokens, (2) tax on the income from staking rewards should be deferred "until realized through a sale or disposition" because the rewards were like stock dividends and represented "growth or accretion in value" and (3) "staking rewards constitute[d] self-created property," and thus income recognition should be deferred.[108](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn108)

First, the Tax Court addressed the argument that the husband lacked dominion and control over the staked tokens because the platform prevented him from transferring the tokens to a wallet hosted by another trading platform.[109](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn109) To the Tax Court, the husband's inability to transfer the staked tokens to another wallet was inconsequential.[110](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn110) The court determined that the husband had dominion and control over the staked tokens because the husband "could convert the tokens to cash at any time."[111](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn111) In support of its conclusion, the Tax Court quoted the 1940 case *Helvering v. Horst* in which the Supreme Court reasoned that the "power to dispose of income [wa]s the equivalent of ownership of it."[112](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn112) The Tax Court also looked to its 2015 decision in *Webber v.* *Commissioner*, a case discussing investor control over assets underlying insurance policies and annuities.[113](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn113) In *Webber*, the Tax Court relied on the decision of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit (Eighth Circuit) in *Christoffersen v. United States*.[114](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn114) In *Christoffersen*, the Eighth Circuit reasoned that a "limitation of withdrawals to cash, rather than shares, d[id] not reflect a lack of ownership or control."[115](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn115)

In *Paschall*, the married taxpayers' second argument was based on the premise that staking rewards were like stock dividends, and any gain from "growth or accretion in value" should be taxed when "realized through a sale or disposition."[116](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn116) Specifically, the taxpayers likened the husband's staking rewards to the pro-rata stock dividend in the 1920 case *Eisner v. Macomber*, where the Supreme Court held that a pro-rata stock dividend was not income subject to tax upon receipt.[117](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn117) The Tax Court rejected the taxpayers' argument that staking rewards and the pro-rata stock dividend in *Macomber*were analogous and highlighted the differences between the two cases.[118](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn118) The Tax Court explained that in *Macomber*, it was paramount that "the stock dividend . . . created no increase or shift in the value owned."[119](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn119) The Tax Court reiterated that, before and after the stock dividend in *Macomber,* the corporation's shareholders had the same "proportionate interest" in the corporation with the same "intrinsic value."[120](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn120) By contrast, in *Paschall*, the Tax Court found that when the husband received his staking rewards, his proportion of all outstanding Cardano tokens increased.[121](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn121) The Tax Court also found that (1) the automatic staking of Cardano tokens was a feature of the trading platform, not the underlying asset; (2) customers could opt out of the platform's staking service; and (3) in order to receive the staking rewards, customers had to "stake their tokens and risk potential forfeiture."[122](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn122)

Next, the taxpayers in *Paschall* contended that staking rewards should be treated like self-created property, which is subject to tax upon sale.[123](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn123) The taxpayers likened token holders that staked their tokens to bakers baking cakes and writers writing books.[124](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn124) The Tax Court rejected this line of reasoning, because it concluded that token holders staking tokens "d[id] not create anything by themselves."[125](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn125) The Tax Court maintained that a cryptocurrency's proof-of-stake consensus protocol grants new tokens to a person when the person's "staked tokens validate transactions on the blockchain."[126](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn126) The Tax Court also noted that the taxpayers in *Paschall* did not have the "power to decide whether (and when)" property was created because they "were not owners or operators of a staking pool."[127](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn127)

## Non-Fungible Tokens

Unlike cryptocurrency, an NFT is type of digital asset that does not function as a medium of exchange.[128](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn128) An NFT is a "unique digital identifier that is recorded using distributed ledger technology and may be used to certify authenticity and ownership of an associated right or asset."[129](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn129) NFT ownership can provide an NFT holder with certain rights with respect to a digital file, such as a digital image,[130](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn130) digital music, a digital trading card, or a digital sports moment that is typically separate from the NFT.[131](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn131)

In IRS Notice 2023-27, the IRS announced that it intended to issue guidance related to the tax treatment of certain NFTs as collectables under IRC § 408(m).[132](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn132) In general, items treated as collectibles do not have the same advantages as other capital assets.[133](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn133) For example, under IRC § 408(m)(1), when an individual retirement account (IRA) or an individually directed account of a qualified plan acquires a collectible, the acquisition is treated as a distribution from the account equal to the cost to the account of the collectible.[134](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn134) IRC § 408(m)(2) lists specific items that constitute collectibles for certain purposes, such as any works of art, rugs, antiques, metals, gems, stamps, coins, and alcoholic beverages.[135](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn135)

In Notice 2023-27, the IRS announced that, pending additional guidance, it intends to apply a "look-through analysis" to determine when an NFT is treated as a collectible.[136](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn136) Under this analysis, an NFT is treated as a collectible if the NFT's associated right or asset falls under the definition of collectible in the IRC.[137](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn137) For example, if a gem is a collectible under IRC § 408(m), an NFT that certifies ownership of a gem would also be treated as a collectible.[138](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn138)

## Charitable Contributions

On January 13, 2023, the IRS issued Chief Counsel Advice 202302012, which addresses deductions for charitable contributions of cryptocurrency under IRC § 170.[139](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn139) In general, if a taxpayer satisfies certain substantiation requirements, then the taxpayer can take a deduction for a contribution to a charitable organization in the taxable year the contribution is made.[140](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn140) For example, for charitable contributions of $250 or more, a taxpayer must substantiate a charitable contribution deduction by obtaining and retaining a contemporaneous written acknowledgment from the donee charitable organization.[141](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn141) For charitable donations of property for which a deduction of more than $5,000 is claimed, IRC § 170(f)(11)(C) requires taxpayers to substantiate their charitable contribution deductions by obtaining a qualified appraisal of such property for the taxable year in which the contribution is made and attach to a tax return such information as required by the Treasury Secretary.[142](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn142) For charitable donations of property for which a deduction of more than $500,000 is claimed, taxpayers must also attach the qualified appraisal to their tax returns.[143](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn143)

As explained in the IRC and regulations, qualified appraisals are not required for certain "readily valued property," including cash, stock in trade, inventory, property primarily held for sale to customers in the ordinary course of business, publicly traded securities, intellectual property, and certain vehicles.[144](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn144) In Chief Counsel Advice 202302012, the IRS concluded that cryptocurrency held for personal investment required a qualified appraisal because it did not fit within any of the categories for "readily valued property."[145](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn145)

## Tax-Exempt Organizations

In IRS Private Letter Ruling 202019028, the IRS determined that an organization formed to advance a cryptocurrency did not qualify as a 501(c)(3) organization—that is, a charity that meets the requirements for federal tax exemption under IRC Section 501(c)(3).[146](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn146) The IRS determined that the organization was not an educational organization, did not operate for an exclusively charitable purpose, and benefited private interest.[147](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn147) The organization's activities included paying transaction fees and digital asset rewards to validators and facilitating payments to its development team.[148](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn148)

## Considerations for Congress

The dynamism of digital assets complicates the formulation of a flexible and clear digital assets tax regime. Several Members of Congress have introduced legislative proposals aiming to reform and clarify the tax treatment of digital assets.[149](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn149) Some of these proposals seek to change the tax consequences of specific digital asset transactions.[150](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn150) For example, the Tax Clarity for Mining and Staking Act in the 119th Congress would permit a taxpayer to defer the recognition of income from mining and staking activities until a taxpayer disposes of the tokens.[151](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn151) These proposals also seek to eliminate unfavorable tax treatment resulting from treating digital assets as property.[152](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn152) For instance, both the Digital Asset Tax Certainty Act and the Charitable Deductions for Digital Asset Donations Act in the 119th Congress would except "widely traded digital assets" from qualified appraisal requirements.[153](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-21_R49334_2c53a6b3d37b81c187f98e1c340c945b0ae6740c.html#fn153) As new types of digital assets emerge, Congress may evaluate whether existing tax laws adequately address the new digital asset and determine whether new and more targeted legislation is needed.

A qualified appraisal is an appraisal that (1) is treated as a qualified appraisal under regulations or other guidance provided by the Secretary of Treasury and (2) is conducted by a qualified appraiser in accordance with generally accepted appraisal standards and any regulations or other guidance prescribed by the Secretary of Treasury. *Id.* § 170(f)(11)(E)(i); 26 C.F.R. § 1.170A-17. The contents that a qualified appraisal must include are listed in Treasury Regulation § 1.170A-17(a)(3). 26 C.F.R. § 1.170A-17(a)(3). A qualified appraiser means an individual who (1) "has earned an appraisal designation from a recognized professional appraiser organization or has otherwise met minimum education and experience requirements set forth in regulations prescribed by the Secretary," (2) "regularly performs appraisals for which the individual receives compensation," and (3) "meets such other requirements as may be prescribed by the Secretary in regulations or other guidance." 26 U.S.C. § 170(f)(11)(E)(ii); *see* 26 C.F.R. § 1.170A-17(b). Generally accepted appraisal standards mean "the substance and principles of the Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice, as developed by the Appraisal Standards Board of the Appraisal Foundation." *Id.* § 1.170A-17(a)(2). |

Revenue Procedure 2026-1 describes a private letter ruling as "a written determination issued to a taxpayer by an Associate office in response to the taxpayer's written inquiry, filed prior to the filing of returns or reports that are required by the tax laws, about its status for tax purposes or the tax effects of its acts or transactions." Rev. Proc. 2026-1, 2026-1 I.R.B. 1, § 2.01. In a private letter ruling, the IRS "interprets the tax laws and applies them to the taxpayer's specific set of facts." *Id.* In general, a taxpayer cannot use a private letter ruling issued to another taxpayer as precedent binding the IRS. 26 U.S.C. § 6110(k)(3); McKenny v. United States, 973 F.3d 1291, 1300 n.6 (11th Cir. 2020); 26 C.F.R. § 301.6110-7(b). Still, courts consider private letter rulings and the Supreme Court has used private letter rulings as evidence of the IRS's position. Rowan Companies, Inc. v. United States, 452 U.S. 247, 261 n.17 (1981), *superseded in part on other grounds by statute*, Social Security Amendments of 1983, Pub. L. No. 98-21, § 324(a)(3)(A), 97 Stat. 65, 123; Glass v. Comm'r, 471 F.3d 698, 709 (6th Cir. 2006). |
