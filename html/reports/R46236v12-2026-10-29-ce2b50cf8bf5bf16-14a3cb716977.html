Summary

Brazil has the potential to play an influential role in international affairs as the 5th-largest territory, 7th-most populous country, and 11th-largest economy in the world. Given Brazil's potential strategic importance, Members of Congress sometimes have explored ways to bolster U.S.-Brazil cooperation and Brazil periodically has been a focal point of U.S. policy in Latin America. The United States and Brazil historically have maintained robust political and economic ties, including regular high-level engagement on security and other matters and goods and services trade valued at nearly $135 billion in 2025. Nevertheless, differing policy approaches and sometimes divergent national interests appear to have inhibited the development of a closer partnership.

Brazil's Political Situation

On October 25, 2026, President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva (Lula; 2003-2010, 2023-present) of the left-of-center Workers' Party narrowly won a fourth term. He defeated Senator Flávio Bolsonaro of the right-wing Liberal Party in a second-round runoff election, 51.8% to 48.2%. That is roughly double the margin by which Lula defeated Flávio's father, then-incumbent President Jair Bolsonaro (2019-2023), in the 2022 presidential runoff. Jair Bolsonaro is currently serving a 27-year prison sentence under house arrest for attempting a coup d'état to overturn his election loss. Senator Bolsonaro initially stated that he intended to challenge the results of the 2026 runoff, which have been recognized by an Organization of American States election observation mission, many foreign governments, and most of Brazil's political leadership, including Bolsonaro's party. Days later, on October 28, 2026, Senator Bolsonaro entered the United States, fleeing ongoing criminal investigations in Brazil and claiming political persecution.

President Lula is to be inaugurated to his fourth four-year term on January 5, 2027. He has largely pledged continuity with his current administration, which has focused on increasing Brazilians' upward social mobility while addressing environmental destruction in the Brazilian Amazon and expanding Brazil's international influence. Lula has advanced portions of his domestic agenda—such as stricter environmental enforcement—through executive action and secured congressional approval for some of his proposed socioeconomic policies. The center-right Brazilian congress has blocked some of Lula's initiatives and appointments, however, and advanced some legislation opposed by the president. Both houses of the Brazilian congress are to shift further to the right during Lula's fourth term, which could result in increased inter-branch conflict.

U.S.-Brazil Relations

Over the past decade, U.S.-Brazil relations have waxed and waned, depending, in part, on the relative ideological alignment between the administrations in power. Bilateral relations have been strained since 2025. The second Trump Administration imposed tariffs on goods imports from Brazil and sanctions on some Brazilian supreme court justices in response to the criminal prosecution of former President Bolsonaro and the Brazilian judiciary's approach to regulating social media, which the Administration characterized as political persecution and censorship. The Lula administration has condemned these and other U.S. actions—including the designation of two Brazilian criminal groups as foreign terrorist organizations—as politically motivated attacks on Brazilian sovereignty. As of October 28, 2026, the Trump Administration had not recognized the results of Brazil's 2026 presidential election.

Congressional Action

Some Members of Congress have monitored developments in Brazil and sought to influence the trajectory of U.S.-Brazilian relations. Environmental conservation has been a major area of focus for some Members. In the National Security, Department of State, and Related Programs Appropriations Act, 2026 (P.L. 119-75, Division F), Congress designated $38.0 million for environmental programs across the Amazon region. Some Members also have expressed concerns about Brazilian judicial orders regulating social media platforms and have expressed support for the Trump Administration's sanctions against Brazilian supreme court justices who allegedly infringed on freedom of expression. Some other Members have criticized the Trump Administration for purportedly using U.S. sanctions and trade policy to undermine Brazil's democracy and rule of law. Congress may assess whether—and, if so, how—to continue shaping U.S.-Brazil relations on these and other issues as it considers FY2027 appropriations and exercises its other legislative and oversight prerogatives.

Brazil has the potential to play an influential role in international affairs as the 5th-largest territory, 7th-most populous country, and the 11th-largest economy in the world; it is also a top global food and energy producer and home to the majority of the Amazon forest (see Figure 1 for a map of Brazil).1 Brazil has often forged coalitions with other large, developing countries to push for changes to multilateral institutions and to ensure that global agreements on issues ranging from trade to climate change adequately protect developing countries' interests. At times, Brazil also has sought to play a greater role in promoting peace and stability, contributing to UN peacekeeping missions and attempting to mediate conflicts. Although some domestic challenges have led Brazil to turn inward and appear to have weakened its standing as a global leader over the past decade, the country continues to exert some influence on international policy issues that affect the United States.

Some analysts have characterized Brazil as a global "swing state" with the potential to affect the trajectory of the international order.2 Given Brazil's potential strategic importance, some of those analysts argue that the United States should more actively engage the country and devote increased attention and resources to forging a close partnership. Several previous efforts to establish closer ties have left policymakers in both countries frustrated, however, as differing policy approaches and sometimes divergent national interests have inhibited cooperation.3 Some analysts argue that Brazil is unlikely to ever closely align with the United States but the two countries may be able to cooperate on particular issues at particular times.4

Congress may monitor developments in Brazil and the country's approach to policy issues of importance to the United States. Congress also may assess whether and, if so, how to influence U.S.-Brazilian relations as it oversees U.S. defense and trade policies, considers appropriations for security and environmental assistance, and debates sanctions and other potential legislation.

Brazil's Political and Economic Situation

Background

Brazil declared independence from Portugal in 1822, initially establishing a constitutional monarchy and retaining a slave-based, plantation economy. Approximately 5 million enslaved Africans were forcibly transported to Brazil between the mid-1500s and 1888, when Brazil abolished slavery, accounting for about 45% of all enslaved Africans brought to the Americas.5 Brazil became a republic in 1889, but economic and political power remained concentrated in the hands of large rural landowners and most Brazilians remained outside the political system. During the 1910s and 1920s, for example, roughly 2%-3% of Brazil's population voted.6 The authoritarian government of Getúlio Vargas (1930-1945) began to incorporate the working classes into politics but exerted strict control over labor as part of its broader push to centralize power in the federal government. Vargas also began to implement a state-led development model, which endured for much of the 20th century as successive governments supported the expansion of Brazilian industry.

Brazil experienced two decades of multiparty democracy from 1945 to 1964 but struggled with political and economic instability that ultimately led the military to seize power. A 1964 military coup ushered in two decades of authoritarian rule. The U.S. government dispatched military assets to potentially aid the pro-coup forces and expressed support for the post-coup government.7 Brazilian security forces killed at least 434 dissidents and detained and tortured an estimated 30,000-50,000 others during the dictatorship.8 The military government allowed the judiciary and congress to nominally function during its tenure but stifled representative democracy and civic action, carefully preserving its influence during Brazil's protracted democratic transition back to civilian democratic governance.9

Brazil restored civilian rule in 1985, and a national constituent assembly, elected in 1986, promulgated a new constitution in 1988. The constitution divides power among three branches of government: an executive branch led by a president with extensive policymaking authority; a legislative branch consisting of the 513-member Chamber of Deputies and the 81-member Senate; and an independent judicial branch charged with interpreting and applying the numerous political, economic, and social rights enshrined in the constitution.10 The 1988 constitution also established universal suffrage, eliminating literacy requirements that had disenfranchised more than half of the population during the previous democratic era and disproportionately affected Afro-Brazilians.11 Under Brazil's federal structure, the national government shares authority with 26 states, a federal district that includes the capital city of Brasília, and 5,568 municipalities. Organizations that attempt to track respect for democracy globally generally recognize Brazil's democracy for its competitive elections and political pluralism but identify some shortcomings, including endemic corruption and high levels of political polarization and violence.12

Lula Administration (2023-Present)

Brazilian President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva (Lula) of the left-of-center Workers' Party (Partido dos Trabalhadores, or PT) was inaugurated to a third four-year term on January 1, 2023, marking a significant political comeback. Lula originally rose to prominence during the 1970s as the leader of Brazil's metal workers union. He subsequently helped found the PT amid the push to restore democracy in Brazil and led the party in three unsuccessful presidential bids before being elected in 2002. Lula presided over a period of sustained economic growth and improving living conditions in Brazil during his first two terms (2003-2010), and left office with an 87% approval rating.13 He was convicted on corruption charges in 2017, however, and imprisoned for 19 months before Brazil's Federal Supreme Court (Supremo Tribunal Federal, or STF) annulled those convictions on procedural grounds and ruled that the presiding judge had acted with bias.14 Lula then narrowly defeated incumbent President Jair Bolsonaro (2019-2022), a right-wing populist affiliated with the Liberal Party (Partido Liberal, or PL), 50.9% to 49.1%, in an October 2022 presidential runoff election.15

During his third term, Lula has focused on raising Brazilians' living standards, addressing environmental concerns, and expanding Brazil's international influence. He has implemented portions of his agenda through executive action, such as expanding land rights for Indigenous groups and Quilombolas (inhabitants of communities founded by individuals who escaped or were freed from slavery), strengthening environmental conservation efforts, and engaging in presidential diplomacy. (For further discussion, see "Approach to the Amazon Forest" and "Brazil's Foreign Policy.")

Lula has sought to advance other priorities through the fragmented Brazilian congress, in which 17 political parties or party federations have representation.16 Although legislators aligned with Lula held approximately 27% of seats in the Chamber of Deputies and 19% of seats in the Senate at the start of Brazil's 57th legislature (2023-2027), Lula forged working majorities with the conditional support of a bloc of centrist and center-right parties that typically work with whatever administration is in power to increase the parties' influence over government policy and staffing.17 Among other Lula administration proposals, the Brazilian congress has approved minimum wage increases, an expansion of a conditional cash transfer program for low-income Brazilians, a progressive income tax reform, and a labor reform to reduce Brazil's work week from six days to five days (and from 44 to 40 hours).

Lula's working legislative majorities have not always held. In April 2026, for example, the Brazilian Senate rejected Lula's nominee for a vacant seat on the STF—reportedly the first such rejection since 1894.18 The Brazilian congress also has overridden some presidential vetoes. In April 2026, for example, the congress overrode Lula's veto to enact legislation intended to reduce prison sentences for some individuals convicted of crimes related to January 8, 2023, riots, in which Bolsonaro supporters stormed Brazil's congress, supreme court, and presidential palace while calling on the military to oust the newly inaugurated Lula administration.19 The legislation could benefit former President Bolsonaro, who was convicted of an attempted coup and sentenced to 27 years in prison in September 2025.20 According to Brazilian authorities, in addition to facilitating the January 8, 2023, riots, Bolsonaro and his allies engaged in activities to discredit the 2022 elections, blocked opposition voters from getting to the polls, drafted decrees to overturn the election results, and pressured members of the armed forces to support a coup.21 Brazil's STF has temporarily suspended the sentence reduction legislation while it undergoes judicial review.22

Socioeconomic Conditions

During his 2022 campaign, President Lula pledged to restore the upward social mobility that characterized his first two terms in office, when Brazil's gross domestic product (GDP) expanded by an average of 4.1% per year, driven in part by a surge in international demand (particularly from the People's Republic of China [PRC, or China]) for Brazilian commodities such as oil, iron, and soybeans. Brazil's GDP growth, which averaged less than 1.0% between 2011 and 2022, accelerated to an average of 3.0% between 2023 and 2025.23 This acceleration was driven, in part, by rising household consumption tied to improving labor market conditions, an expansion of credit, and government income transfer programs.24 Between the second quarter of 2022 and the second quarter of 2026, Brazil's unemployment rate fell from 9.3% to 5.4%, and real average monthly incomes increased by about 19%.25 Likewise, Brazil's poverty rate fell from 31.6% in 2022 to 23.1% in 2024; the poverty rates of Black and mixed race Brazilians remained higher than the national average, at 25.8% and 29.8%, respectively.26 Despite these socioeconomic improvements, a majority of Brazilians surveyed in September 2026 reported that their household incomes either had not risen or had not kept up with the cost of living over the past year.27

The Economist Intelligence Unit projects 1.8% GDP growth for Brazil in 2026.28 This slowdown is due, in part, to the U.S.-Israel conflict with Iran, which has contributed to higher inflation and interest rates, dampening domestic consumption. Consumer prices in Brazil increased by 4.6% between October 2025 and September 2026, with gasoline and diesel prices increasing by 5.6% and 14.4%, respectively.29 The Lula administration has implemented a mix of subsidies and tax-relief measures intended to mitigate the impact of price surges on Brazilians, using some of the revenues the government has received as a result of Brazil's increased oil exports.30 The Brazilian government is under pressure from international investors to tighten its fiscal policies, however, as the federal government is expected to run a 7.3% deficit and gross government debt is expected to exceed 82% of GDP in 2026.31

Approach to the Amazon Forest

About 62% of the greater Amazon region, encompassing the Amazon Forest and the Amazon Basin, is located within Brazil.32 Deforestation in the Brazilian Amazon,33 which fell by nearly 77% over the course of Lula's first two terms, began rising in 2012 and reached a 15-year high of 5,034 square miles in 2021 under the Bolsonaro administration (see Figure 2). Some analysts linked the 2012-2021 increase in deforestation to a series of government policy reversals that cut federal funding for environmental enforcement, reduced the size of protected areas, and relaxed conservation requirements.34 Market incentives, such as international prices for beef, soybeans, and gold, among other commodities, also appear to have contributed to deforestation trends.35 Some scientists have warned that the Amazon forest may be nearing a tipping point at which the forest, no longer able to sustain itself, could transition to a drier, savanna-like ecosystem.36 This cycle of deforestation and drought could reduce the forest's capacity to absorb and sequester carbon, as well as reduce the precipitation that fuels forest regeneration and growth and Brazil's agricultural and hydropower production.37

The Lula administration has pledged to eliminate net deforestation in the Brazilian Amazon by 2030.38 Effectively monitoring and controlling activities in the region is difficult given the size of the Brazilian Amazon, which encompasses 58.9% of Brazil's territory and is home to 26.7 million inhabitants.39 Among other measures, the Lula administration has increased environmental enforcement operations, expanded protected areas, implemented an income support program for families engaged in conservation, and issued concessions for sustainable forest management and forest restoration.40 The Lula administration, with the support of other countries, also relaunched the Amazon Fund—created in 2008 but frozen during the Bolsonaro administration—as a vehicle for international donors to support such efforts. The politically powerful rural caucus in the Brazilian congress and some state governments, such as Mato Grosso, have pushed back on some of the Lula administration's environmental policies. The Lula administration also has continued to support the expansion of oil and gas production in Brazil, including in environmentally sensitive areas in the Amazon Basin. Nevertheless, the Lula administration's approach appears to have contributed to a 51% decrease in deforestation in the Brazilian Amazon between 2022 and 2025 (see Figure 2).

2026 General Elections

On October 25, 2026, President Lula was elected to a fourth four-year term.41 He defeated former President Bolsonaro's son, Senator Flávio Bolsonaro of the PL, 51.8% to 48.2%, in a second-round runoff election.42 Bolsonaro had edged out Lula in the first-round vote held on October 4, 2026, 47.0% to 45.2%, but was short of the majority needed to avoid a runoff; the remainder of valid votes in the first round were split among 10 other candidates.43 The number of Brazilians casting valid votes increased by nearly 4.8 million between the first and second rounds as the election became a choice between two polarizing candidates.44 The final vote shares obtained by each candidate closely tracked Brazilians' rejection of their opponents; in a poll fielded the week before the runoff, 47% of Brazilians stated they would never vote for Lula while 52% stated they would never vote for Bolsonaro.45

Senator Bolsonaro initially stated that he planned to challenge the election results due to the decision of a Lula-appointed STF justice, Cristiano Zanin, to extend voting by two hours.46 According to press reports, the Lula and Bolsonaro campaigns both requested extended voting hours in certain areas of the country: the Lula campaign in municipalities where local governments allegedly had reduced public transportation service and the Bolsonaro campaign in regions of southern Brazil affected by heavy rainfall. The voting extension order was to apply nationwide but reportedly was implemented unevenly, as some polls had already closed.47 Some 34,000 votes reportedly were cast during the extension; Lula's margin of victory was 4.4 million votes.48 An Organization of American States electoral observation mission, many foreign governments, and most of Brazil's political leadership—including Bolsonaro's PL—have recognized Lula's victory.49 As of October 28, 2026, the Trump Administration had not recognized the results (see "Bolsonaro Prosecution and 2026 Elections").

On October 28, 2026, Senator Bolsonaro entered the United States claiming that Brazil's presidential election had been fraudulent and that he was facing political persecution.50 He reportedly has been under investigation in two cases in Brazil involving potential financial crimes. One case reportedly is focused on financial support for a film about former President Bolsonaro that Senator Bolsonaro sought from a banker allegedly involved in a multibillion-dollar fraud scheme; corruption scandals related to the banker have implicated politicians across the political spectrum as well as some STF justices. The other case reportedly involves potential money laundering tied to an online betting company.51 The STF reportedly ordered the seizure of Bolsonaro's passports following the election due to concerns he planned to flee the country.52

President Lula is to be inaugurated to his fourth term on January 5, 2027. He may face additional congressional opposition in the new term due to the results of legislative elections for the full Chamber of Deputies and two-thirds of the seats in the Brazilian Senate that were held concurrently with the first-round presidential vote. Bolsonaro's PL picked up seats in both houses and is to be the largest party in Brazil's 58th legislature (February 1, 2027-January 31, 2031), holding 121 of 513 seats in the Chamber of Deputies and 29 of 81 seats in the Senate. The PL's gains largely came at the expense of centrist and center-right parties. According to one assessment of the ideologies of the 15 political parties and party federations to be represented in the next congress, legislators affiliated with the PL and other right-wing parties are to collectively hold majorities in both houses.53

Brazil's Foreign Policy

Although the short-term areas of emphasis of Brazilian foreign policy have varied since Brazil's return to democracy, successive Brazilian administrations generally have sought to increase the country's influence on global affairs while maintaining Brazil's autonomy.54 While pursuing these objectives, Brazilian officials have emphasized the principles of multilateralism, peaceful dispute settlement, and nonintervention in the affairs of other countries.55 In practice, this approach has involved the pursuit of cooperative relations with international partners of varying ideologies and systems of government, including the United States, the European Union (EU), Brazil's South American neighbors, and fellow members of the BRICS group (originally named for Brazil, Russia, India, China, and South Africa).56

Since returning to office in 2023, President Lula has sought to reassert Brazil's influence abroad after two Brazilian administrations that arguably were more focused on domestic challenges. He has placed particular emphasis on presidential diplomacy, convening and hosting various gatherings of world leaders. These gatherings have included a summit of South American leaders (May 2023), a meeting of Amazon Basin countries (August 2023), a Group of 20 (G-20) summit (November 2024), a BRICS summit (July 2025), and the 30th session of the Conference of the Parties to the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (November 2025).

Similar to his previous terms, Lula also has sought to mediate some regional and global conflicts since returning to office. Perhaps most prominently, the Lula administration sought to mediate between the Venezuelan government of then-President Nicolás Maduro (2013-2026) and the political opposition following an apparently fraudulent 2024 presidential election. Brazil also put forward a joint peace proposal with the PRC to end the Russia-Ukraine war. Neither diplomatic initiative proved fruitful. Although U.S. officials reportedly have urged the Brazilian government to take on a more prominent role in addressing the security crisis in Haiti, Brazil's involvement has been limited under the Lula administration.57 Some Brazilian policymakers reportedly assess that the United Nations Stabilization Mission in Haiti that Brazil commanded between 2004 and 2017 largely failed to accomplish its mandate and that another international security presence is unlikely to more successful.58

Brazil's approach to the BRICS group and relations with the PRC, which have been of particular interest to some Members of Congress, are discussed below.

Approach to the BRICS Group

Brazil has long sought a more prominent role and greater influence in the UN Security Council and other international institutions, which Brazilian officials have argued need to better represent developing countries.59 Brazil's reform proposals have been frustrated repeatedly, giving rise to what appears to be a widespread perception among Brazilian foreign policymakers that the United States and European countries are unwilling to cede space. According to some analysts, Brazil has responded by working with coalitions like the BRICS group to increase the country's leverage in global policy discussions.60 Inclusion in the BRICS, alongside China and India, also arguably has bolstered Brazil's efforts to portray itself as a rising power.

The Lula administration has described the BRICS as "a strategic platform to promote cooperation between emerging countries," and has pushed back against efforts—within and outside the BRICS—to characterize the bloc as "anti-Western."61 At the same time, President Lula has repeatedly called for the BRICS to adopt alternatives to the U.S. dollar for trade among BRICS countries, in part to reduce vulnerability to dollar exchange rate fluctuations.62 Since 2024, Egypt, Ethiopia, Indonesia, Iran, and the United Arab Emirates have joined the BRICS.63 Brazil reportedly resisted this expansion, due to concerns that it could reduce Brazil's influence and the bloc's cohesion, shift the balance of power within the bloc in a more authoritarian direction, and complicate Brazil's efforts to maintain an autonomous foreign policy.64

Brazil held the rotating presidency of the BRICS in 2025, and Lula's protégé, former President Dilma Rousseff (2011-2016), was appointed to a second five-year term as the President of the New Development Bank (formerly known as the BRICS Bank) in March 2025. During its year-long BRICS presidency, the Brazilian government focused on cooperation in the "Global South," with a particular emphasis on health, trade and investment, climate change, artificial intelligence (AI), UN Security Council reform, and the institutional development of the BRICS group.65 During the July 2025 BRICS summit in Brazil, the bloc issued a 126-point declaration that included calls for reform and increased representation for BRICS and other developing countries in international organizations, including the UN Security Council, and continued cooperation within the BRICS to increase the interoperability of BRICS payment systems. The declaration also condemned the rise of unilateral tariffs, the imposition of economic and secondary sanctions, and military strikes against Iran, without explicitly mentioning the United States.66 Other documents adopted at the summit focused on climate finance, global governance of AI, and the elimination of diseases associated with poverty and inequality.

Brazil-China Relations

In addition to engaging in multilateral cooperation within the BRICS group and other fora, Brazil and China have forged extensive bilateral ties. The countries have maintained formal diplomatic relations since 1974 and a "strategic partnership" since 1993.67 The Brazilian and PRC governments also have engaged in frequent high-level diplomacy, with China hosting state visits for each of the past five Brazilian presidents and Brazil hosting state visits for PRC leader Xi Jinping (most recently in 2024) and his predecessor Hu Jintao.68 During Lula's May 2025 state visit to China, the Brazilian and PRC governments signed 20 bilateral agreements related to AI, energy, infrastructure, mining, space, and trade, among other areas of cooperation.69

Brazil's relationship with China appears to be driven primarily by economic interests. Between 2005 and 2025, Brazil's annual goods trade with China climbed from $12.2 billion to $170.9 billion, and China's share of Brazil's global goods trade rose from 6.3% to 27.2%. In 2025, Brazil ran a $29.0 billion goods trade surplus with China.70 Brazilian export growth has been heavily concentrated in a few products, particularly benefitting Brazil's mineral extraction industry and the politically influential agribusiness sector. In 2025, for example, soybeans, iron ore, crude oil, and frozen beef collectively accounted for 83.0% of the total value of Brazilian exports to China.71 Brazilian manufacturers, on the other hand, have faced increased competition from imports from China, which some blame for deindustrialization.72

PRC investment and development finance in Brazil also have increased over the past 20 years, despite Brazil not having formally signed onto China's "Belt and Road Initiative."73 According to the Brazil-China Business Council, between 2007 and 2025, PRC companies invested $85.5 billion in 355 projects in Brazil. The top sectors by project value were electricity (44% of total), oil (28%), manufacturing (9%), and mining (8%).74 The focus of such investments has shifted over time, with 60% of projects in 2025 focused on green energy and related sectors, including electric vehicles and hydroelectric, solar, and wind energy.75 According to the China Global Investment Tracker database, which tracks investments over $95 million, Brazil received about 5% of PRC investment worldwide between 2005 and 2025, ranking fourth behind the United States (13%), Australia (7%), and the United Kingdom (7%).76 Between 2005 and 2023 (most recent year available), the Brazilian government and state-owned enterprises received $32.4 billion in loans from China's state-owned policy banks (i.e., China Development Bank and the Export-Import Bank of China), primarily for the energy sector.77

Although some Brazilian leaders have expressed concerns at times about PRC investments in strategic sectors, they generally have lacked the political support, political will, or both to restrict such investments. For example, President Bolsonaro entered office reportedly warning that the PRC was "buying Brazil," but did not change the trajectory of Brazil's trade and investment relations with China.78 His administration also permitted PRC companies like Huawei to participate in the development of Brazil's commercial fifth generation (5G) telecommunications infrastructure.79

Brazil's military ties with China appear to be limited. The Brazilian and PRC governments established a joint exchange and cooperation commission in 2004 and signed a defense cooperation framework agreement in 2011. Those mechanisms have facilitated education and training exchanges for some Brazilian and PRC military personnel as well as occasional joint exercises.80 In January 2026, for example, a PRC hospital ship docked in Rio de Janeiro and its officers reportedly met and held some exchanges with a Brazilian navy delegation.81 According to the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute's Arms Transfers Database, there have not been any major arms transfers between China and Brazil.82 Brazil, which by some assessments has the most advanced defense industry in Latin America, also competes with China to supply the South American defense market.83 Some Members of Congress have expressed concern that PRC access to potential dual-use infrastructure in Brazil, including a satellite ground station and radio astronomy laboratory, could support PRC military objectives.84

U.S.-Brazil Relations

The United States and Brazil marked 200 years of diplomatic relations in 2024. Historically, the two countries have maintained robust political and economic ties, but differing perceptions of their respective national interests appear to have hindered the development of a closer partnership. That dynamic changed to some extent in 2019 and 2020, during which then-President Bolsonaro brought Brazil's foreign policy into closer alignment with U.S. foreign policy, President Donald Trump designated Brazil as a major non-NATO ally, and the U.S. and Brazilian governments negotiated a Protocol on Trade Rules and Transparency intended to facilitate trade and regulatory cooperation (see "Defense" and "Trade and Investment Relations"). The Biden Administration continued to engage with the Bolsonaro administration on military and economic matters while carrying out a high-level diplomatic effort that had the stated of aim of ensuring Brazil's 2022 elections were free and fair and resulted in a peaceful transfer of power (see "Brazil's 2022 Elections and Aftermath"). After Lula took office, the Biden Administration's cooperation with Brazil shifted to place more emphasis on environmental concerns (see "Environmental Cooperation").

Bilateral relations have been strained since July 2025. That month, President Donald Trump announced his intention to impose additional 40% tariffs on Brazilian imports and the U.S. Departments of State and Treasury imposed sanctions on some Brazilian supreme court justices in response to the criminal prosecution of former President Bolsonaro and the STF's regulation of social media content, which the Administration characterized as political persecution and censorship.85 A series of high-level meetings following Bolsonaro's September 2025 conviction appear to have contributed to a temporary rapprochement between the Lula and Trump Administrations in late 2025. Bilateral tensions escalated again in 2026, however, reflecting continuing differences between the two governments regarding Brazilian efforts to hold accountable individuals involved in activities Brazil has deemed antidemocratic and U.S. actions perceived by some Brazilian officials as political intervention in Brazil (see "Bolsonaro Prosecution and 2026 Elections" and "U.S. Tariffs").

The Trump Administration's approach to bilateral relations appears to be generating some backlash in Brazil. In Pew Research Center polling conducted between February and April 2026, 76% of Brazilians asserted that the United States interferes "a great deal" or "a fair amount" in the affairs of other countries. The same poll found that 47% of Brazilians had favorable views of the United States (down from 64% in spring 2024) and 30% of Brazilians had confidence in President Trump to "do the right thing regarding world affairs."86

Engagement on Democracy and Freedom of Expression

Over the past four years, some Members of Congress and other U.S. policymakers have expressed concerns about potential threats to democracy and freedom of expression in Brazil. Like Brazilians, they have articulated different views about the source of such threats and U.S. policy responses.

Brazil's 2022 Elections and Aftermath

In the lead up to Brazil's 2022 elections, then-President Bolsonaro repeatedly questioned the integrity of Brazil's electoral system.87 The Biden Administration, in contrast, expressed confidence in Brazil's electoral institutions and reportedly held meetings with Brazilian officials urging them to respect the electoral process.88 In September 2022, the U.S. Senate adopted a resolution (118th Congress, S.Res. 753) that urged the Brazilian government to ensure "free, fair, credible, transparent, and peaceful" elections. The resolution also called on the U.S. government to speak out against efforts to undermine the electoral process, immediately recognize the outcome of elections determined by international observers to be free and fair, and make clear that undemocratic actions would jeopardize U.S.-Brazilian relations, including U.S. security assistance. By some accounts, these U.S. efforts helped ensure a peaceful transition in Brazil.89

In the aftermath of the January 8, 2023, riots in Brazil, some Members of Congress introduced resolutions condemning the attacks on Brazilian government institutions (e.g., 118th Congress, H.Res. 106 and S.Res. 32). Some Members also sought to collaborate with their Brazilian counterparts and share best practices on congressional investigations.90 Additionally, some Members called on the Biden Administration to investigate any actions taken on U.S. soil to organize the events of January 8, and to work with the Brazilian government to uphold the rule of law, including by ensuring former President Bolsonaro was held accountable for any crimes he may have committed.91

Bolsonaro Prosecution and 2026 Elections

Some other Members of Congress and the Trump Administration have expressed concerns about the criminal prosecution of former President Bolsonaro and his allies.92 In July 2025, President Trump issued Executive Order (EO) 14323, which characterized the prosecution of former President Bolsonaro and the Brazilian judiciary's approach to regulating social media content (see "Freedom of Expression in Brazil") as political persecution, censorship, and a threat to free and fair elections in 2026. The EO asserted that these actions constituted an "unusual and extraordinary threat" to the national security, foreign policy, and economy of the United States and invoked the National Emergencies Act (NEA; 50 U.S.C. §§1601 et seq.) and the International Emergency Economic Powers Act of 1977 (IEEPA; 50 U.S.C. §§1701 et seq.) to impose an additional 40% tariff on U.S. imports from Brazil (see "U.S. Tariffs").93 In trade negotiations after Bolsonaro's September 2025 conviction, Trump Administration officials reportedly pressed the Brazilian government to allow "political dissidents"—apparently referring to former President Bolsonaro—to participate in the 2026 elections.94 On July 28, 2026, President Trump extended the national emergency declared with respect to Brazil for another year.95

The U.S. Departments of State and Treasury also imposed sanctions on some Brazilian STF justices and other Brazilian officials in response to the prosecution of former President Bolsonaro and the judiciary's regulation of social media. In July 2025, Secretary of State Marco Rubio ordered visa revocations, pursuant to a provision of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA; 8 U.S.C. §1182(a)(3)(C)), for Justice Alexandre de Moraes, who served as the rapporteur for the investigation into Bolsonaro,96 "his allies on the court," and their immediate family members.97 According to press reports, the State Department revoked the visas of at least 8 of the 11 STF justices as well as the head of the autonomous public prosecutor's office (Procurador-Geral da República), Paulo Gonet, prohibiting their entry into the United States.98 The U.S. Department of the Treasury imposed additional economic sanctions on Justice de Moraes and his spouse in July and September 2025, respectively, pursuant to the Global Magnitsky Human Rights Accountability Act (Title XII, Subtitle F of P.L. 114-328, as amended).99 The sanctions blocked Justice de Moraes and his spouse from accessing any property under U.S. jurisdiction and prohibited U.S. persons from engaging in transactions with them.100 The Department of the Treasury lifted the Global Magnitsky sanctions in December 2025, reportedly linking the decision to Brazil's Chamber of Deputies passing a bill to reduce prison sentences for some individuals convicted of undemocratic actions (potentially including former President Bolsonaro).101 It is unclear if the U.S. visa restrictions remain in place.

U.S. pressure on Brazilian authorities to halt the Bolsonaro prosecution reportedly was sought and welcomed by former President Bolsonaro's family and political allies,102 but rejected by the leaders of all three branches of the Brazilian government.103 Some Members of Congress had urged the Trump Administration to impose sanctions on Justice de Moraes for "human rights abuses and antidemocratic actions," asserting that de Moraes was attempting to "rig the 2026 election."104 Other Members accused the Trump Administration of abusing U.S. tariff and sanction authorities to advance the interests of his political allies and "undermine Brazil's democracy."105

Some Trump Administration officials in the U.S. State Department reportedly sought to support the 2026 electoral campaign of Flávio Bolsonaro.106 Among other actions, they reportedly directed U.S. Embassy personnel not to interact with other potential presidential contenders on the political right and pushed for the U.S. designation of two Brazilian criminal groups as foreign terrorist organizations, as requested by Bolsonaro during a May 2026 White House visit (see "Counternarcotics and Law Enforcement"). The Lula administration repeatedly condemned and sought to block apparent U.S. efforts to influence the elections. In July 2026, for example, the Brazilian government reportedly denied visas to two State Department officials who it suspected were planning to cast doubts on the fairness and integrity of Brazil's electoral system.107 The Brazilian government also characterized other Trump Administration actions as political interference, including the imposition of additional tariffs on imports from Brazil in July 2026 (see "U.S. Tariffs").108 The STF has directed the Brazilian Federal Police to investigate potential foreign interference in the Brazilian elections.109

As of October 28, 2026, the Trump Administration had not recognized the results of Brazil's 2026 presidential runoff, which President Trump alleged "looked like another rigged election."110 Trump Administration officials also reportedly helped Flávio Bolsonaro and his family flee Brazil, where he was facing criminal investigations (see "2026 General Elections"), and arranged for them to be granted temporary parole to enter the United States on October 28, 2026.111 According to press reports, the Administration previously granted permanent U.S. residency to Flávio's brother, Eduardo Bolsonaro, a former Member of Brazil's Chamber of Deputies, in July 2026.112 Brazil's STF sentenced Eduardo Bolsonaro to more than four years in prison in June 2026 for the crime of coercion during legal proceedings as a result of his efforts to lobby the Trump Administration to impose sanctions and other pressure on Brazilian justices to halt the criminal prosecution of former President Bolsonaro.113

Some Members of Congress expressed concerns about the Trump Administration's actions in the lead up to Brazil's 2026 elections, asserting that U.S. officials had engaged in a "campaign of interference and destabilization in Brazil."114 Some Members also questioned what, if any, role the Trump Administration played in helping Flávio Bolsonaro flee criminal charges in Brazil and enter the United States.115 Some other Members of Congress expressed concerns about Brazil's electoral process and STF efforts to prevent Flávio Bolsonaro from leaving Brazil.116

Freedom of Expression in Brazil

Brazil's regulation of digital communications is another topic about which Members of Congress have expressed different views since 2022. During the 118th Congress, some Members expressed concerns about how disinformation and misinformation spread through social media may have contributed to the January 8 riots and introduced a resolution (S.Res. 32) urging social media and group messaging companies to "to take concrete steps to address the ways [their] platforms are used to facilitate disinformation and misinformation in Brazil." Other Members have characterized regulation of social media content as censorship.117

The Brazilian constitution sets forth several principles related to freedom of expression.118 Brazil also has ratified the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, which contains obligations related to freedom of expression while allowing for certain restrictions.119 In 2025, however, Freedom House, a nongovernmental democracy and human rights advocacy organization, asserted that Brazil's legal framework "provides inadequate protection for freedom of expression," due in part to the proscription and regulation of certain types of speech.120 For example, libel, slander, and defamation are criminal offenses, and Brazil's electoral code tightly regulates political campaign activities. Additionally, Brazil's 2014 civil rights framework for the internet established certain rights and obligations related to internet use, and a 2021 law for the defense of the democratic rule of law criminalizes inciting animosity between the armed forces and the executive, legislative, or judicial branches of government, among other offenses.121

The STF has taken a prominent role in regulating online content since March 2019, when then-STF President José Antonio Dias Toffoli opened an investigation into alleged "fake news" and internet threats targeting the STF, its justices, and their family members.122 Some legal analysts questioned the STF's decision to open an investigation unilaterally, and some transparency and press rights advocates condemned the STF for using the investigation to order an online media outlet to remove a report about an STF justice.123 Some observers also have questioned the sprawling nature of the probe, which has remained open for more than seven years.124 While serving as the rapporteur for the fake news investigation between March 2019 and September 2026,125 Justice de Moraes took a variety of unilateral actions that were controversial among sectors of the Brazilian population, including authorizing searches, freezing social media accounts, and ordering arrests; these decisions reportedly were upheld by the broader STF.126

Brazil's Superior Electoral Court (Tribunal Superior Eleitoral, or TSE) also has exercised its authority to combat the spread of information it has deemed false. During the 2022 electoral campaign, for example, the TSE regularly ordered online content to be deleted "at the request of both the Lula and Bolsonaro campaigns."127 The TSE continued to regulate digital campaign content during the 2026 election campaign, setting new standards for AI deepfakes and ordering some anti-Lula and anti-(Flávio) Bolsonaro content to be removed, among other actions.128 Justice de Moraes served as the president of the TSE during the 2022 election, and Justice Kassio Nunes Marques—a Bolsonaro appointee—presided over the TSE during the 2026 election.

Some U.S.-based social media companies have clashed with the STF over online content regulation. In February 2025, for example, Justice de Moraes ordered the suspension of the U.S.-based video-sharing platform Rumble in Brazil due to the company's refusal to comply with previous STF orders to suspend certain accounts, pay resulting fines, or appoint a legal representative in Brazil.129 Rumble and the Trump Media & Technology Group (owner of Truth Social) sued Justice de Moraes in U.S. federal court, arguing he violated free speech protections of the U.S. Constitution's First Amendment by seeking to suspend the accounts of an individual who resides in the United States.130 The individual in question is a pro-Bolsonaro media personality who is wanted in Brazil for defamation and other alleged crimes but who the U.S. government has reportedly declined to extradite.131 A similar dispute with X Corp. (formerly Twitter) resulted in the STF suspending Brazilians' access to that social media platform for more than a month in 2024; X Corp. ultimately complied with the STF orders.132

Information regarding the full scope of court orders related to social media activity in Brazil is not publicly available. In a court filing, X Corp. reportedly stated that the company had blocked 223 accounts between 2020 and September 2024 in response to judicial orders in Brazil—158 in response to STF orders and 65 in response to TSE orders.133 STF and TSE orders subpoenaed from X Corp. by the U.S. House Judiciary Committee during the 118th Congress indicated that the blocked accounts included some prominent figures associated with the ideological right in Brazil, including former President Bolsonaro; at least six federal legislators; and several journalists, political commentators, and social media influencers.134 Additional documents obtained by the House Judiciary Committee during the 119th Congress indicate that some Brazilian judicial orders have sought to compel social media companies to prevent certain content or accounts from being viewed anywhere in the world and that some of those orders have targeted Brazilian nationals residing in the United States.135

The Biden Administration did not comment publicly on Brazil's regulation of digital communications. As noted above ("Bolsonaro Prosecution and 2026 Elections"), the Trump Administration condemned the STF's actions as censorship and imposed tariffs on imports from Brazil and sanctions on Justice de Moraes and other Brazilian justices and officials in 2025. In September 2026, the State Department reportedly obligated $1 million in foreign assistance appropriated for the Human Rights and Democracy Fund to support civil society groups countering purported "lawfare and censorship" in Brazil.136

Options for Congress

Congress may continue to monitor democracy and freedom of expression in Brazil, the extent to which Brazilian judicial orders affect entities and individuals in the United States, and the Trump Administration's approach to such issues, including the Administration's use of sanctions, tariffs, foreign assistance, and visas. In October 2025, the Senate approved a joint resolution to terminate the national emergency declared in EO 14323 related to Brazil's prosecution of former President Bolsonaro and regulation of social media (S.J.Res. 81). The House could consider that measure or a similar joint resolution introduced in the House (H.J.Res. 147). Some Members of Congress have sought additional information regarding the Trump Administration's use of sanctions in Brazil.137 Others reportedly sought to prevent the Administration from moving forward with how it planned to use democracy assistance in Brazil.138 Depending on Members' assessments of conditions in Brazil and the Trump Administration's actions, they could impose restrictions on the Administration's use of sanctions, foreign assistance, and other foreign policy tools or authorize the Administration to take specific actions in Brazil. Members also could engage directly with their Brazilian counterparts, who have been debating a variety of legislative measures related to political rights, freedom of expression, and the separation of powers in Brazil.

Trade and Investment Relations

At times, trade policy has been a contentious issue in U.S.-Brazilian relations. Since the early 1990s, Brazil's trade policy has prioritized integration with its South American neighbors through the Common Market of the South (Mercosur) and multilateral negotiations at the World Trade Organization (WTO).139 Brazil is the industrial hub of Mercosur, which it established with Argentina, Paraguay, and Uruguay in 1991 with the stated goal of facilitating economic integration.140 As a partial customs union, Mercosur facilitates mostly duty-free trade amongst its members and levies a common external tariff on imports from outside the bloc. Within the WTO, Brazil has joined with other developing countries to push the United States and other developed countries to reduce their agricultural tariffs and subsidies while resisting developed countries' calls for increased access to developing countries' industrial and services sectors. These policy differences were among the barriers to concluding the most recent round of multilateral trade negotiations (the WTO's Doha Round), as well as U.S. efforts in the 1990s and 2000s to establish a hemisphere-wide Free Trade Area of the Americas.141

A 2011 Agreement on Trade and Economic Cooperation (ATEC) has served as the primary formal mechanism for U.S.-Brazilian discussions of trade and investment issues. In October 2022, the U.S. and Brazilian governments concluded a Protocol on Trade Rules and Transparency, which added three annexes to the ATEC related to cooperation on trade facilitation and customs administration, regulatory practices, and anti-corruption measures.142 Brazil's congress ratified the protocol in November 2021, and the agreement entered into force in February 2022. The U.S. Congress has not specifically authorized or approved the protocol, which did not include any provisions eliminating tariffs or nontariff barriers to trade that would have required changes to U.S. law.143

U.S. Tariffs

The second Trump Administration has imposed various tariffs on U.S. imports from Brazil. As noted previously ("Bolsonaro Prosecution and 2026 Elections"), in July 2025, President Trump declared a national emergency related to the criminal prosecution of former President Bolsonaro and the Brazilian judiciary's approach to regulating social media and invoked IEEPA to impose an additional 40% tariff on U.S. imports from Brazil, effective August 6, 2025.144 This tariff, which reportedly exempted about half of Brazilian exports to the United States, was in addition to a minimum 10% IEEPA tariff that the President had imposed on most U.S. trade partners in April 2025.145 The President modified the Brazil-specific IEEPA tariff in November 2025 to exempt additional products and lifted all IEEPA tariffs in February 2026 following a Supreme Court ruling that held IEEPA cannot be used to impose tariffs.146

Imports from Brazil are currently subject to U.S. tariffs imposed under other authorities. In June 2026, the Office of the U.S. Trade Representative (USTR) concluded two investigations under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 (19 U.S.C. §§2411-2420).147 In one of those investigations, USTR determined that "Brazil's acts, policies, and practices related to digital trade and electronic payment services; unfair, preferential tariffs; anti-corruption enforcement; intellectual property protection; ethanol market access; and illegal deforestation are unreasonable or discriminatory and burden or restrict U.S. commerce," and imposed a 25% tariff on Brazilian goods, effective July 22, 2026.148 In the second investigation, USTR found that Brazil (and 59 other economies) "failed to impose a forced labor import prohibition and to effectively enforce such a prohibition," and imposed a 12.5% tariff on imports from Brazil, effective July 24, 2026.149 Some imports from Brazil, such as steel, are subject to other tariffs under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 (19 U.S.C. §1862, as amended).150

Some imports from Brazil, including certain types of beef, orange juice, aircraft, and energy products, are exempted from U.S. tariffs.151 The Brazilian government calculated how the Trump Administration's tariffs had affected the mix of products that Brazil had exported to the United States in 2024 (prior to the tariff actions). As of July 2026, about 53% of Brazilian products (by value) were not affected by Section 301 or Section 232 tariffs. Nearly 17% of Brazilian products were subject to both Section 301 tariffs (combined tariff rate of 37.5%), 5% of Brazilian products were subject only to the forced labor Section 301 tariff (12.5% tariff rate), and 2% of Brazilian products were subject only to the Brazil-specific Section 301 tariff (25% tariff rate). Roughly 24% of Brazilian products exported to the United States were subject to Section 232 tariffs.152

The Brazilian government repeatedly has rejected U.S. tariff actions, characterizing them as politically motivated attacks on Brazilian sovereignty.153 Lula administration officials have asserted that Brazil remains open to trade negotiations with the United States but "will not be intimidated" by "threats and false allegations."154 The Brazilian government also has requested dispute consultations at the World Trade Organization and considered—but not yet taken—retaliatory trade measures against the United States; Brazil's Economic Reciprocity Law authorizes Brazil's executive branch to impose tariffs and suspend intellectual property rights obligations, among other retaliatory measures.155 Additionally, Brazil has sought to diversify its trade relations, including by concluding a free trade agreement between Mercosur and the European Union after more than 25 years of intermittent negotiations and advocating for trade negotiations with China.156

Trade and Investment Flows

The total value of U.S.-Brazilian trade in goods and services reached a record high of $134.9 billion in 2025, according to the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA). Compared with 2024, U.S. goods exports to Brazil increased by nearly 11% to $53.9 billion and U.S. goods imports from Brazil declined by about 7% to $39.5 billion, resulting in a $14.4 billion U.S. goods trade surplus.157 According to U.S. Census Bureau data, the top U.S. goods imports from Brazil in 2025 included mineral fuels (primarily crude oil), iron and steel, coffee, machinery, and aircraft and parts. Top U.S. goods exports to Brazil included mineral fuels (primarily refined products), aircraft and parts, machinery, and pharmaceutical products.158

According to the BEA, the United States also ran a $27.4 billion surplus in services trade with Brazil in 2025, as U.S. exports were valued at $34.4 billion and U.S. imports were valued at $7.0 billion.159 Travel and charges for the use of U.S. intellectual property were the top categories of U.S. services exports to Brazil. Professional and management consulting services, travel, and transport were the top categories of U.S. services imports from Brazil.

According to the U.S. Department of State, the Lula administration has sought to attract foreign direct investment (FDI) in certain sectors, including defense, digital connectivity, education, health, sustainable cities, transportation, and water management.160 The Department of State notes that Brazil imposes restrictions on FDI in other sectors, including aerospace, health care, insurance, maritime, mass media, rural property, and telecommunications. The United States is the largest source of FDI in Brazil. As of 2025, the accumulated stock of U.S. FDI in Brazil was $90.2 billion, with significant investments in manufacturing ($25.2 billion), finance ($15.8 billion), and mining ($15.7 billion), among other sectors.161 The same year, the stock of Brazilian FDI in the United States totaled $5.5 billion.162

Critical Minerals

The U.S. and Brazilian governments and some private sector entities have identified critical minerals as a potential area for enhanced bilateral cooperation.163 According to the U.S. Geological Survey, Brazil is among the top suppliers to the United States of 11 mineral commodities for which imports account for more than half of U.S. consumption, including three minerals determined to be essential to U.S. national security and the U.S. economy (niobium, used in steels and alloys; silicon, used in semiconductors; and tin, used in metallurgy).164 Since 2020, the U.S. and Brazilian governments have engaged within a Critical Minerals Working Group, and U.S. agencies have supported some critical minerals projects in Brazil intended to contribute to the development of secure supply chains for the United States:165

  • In 2020 and 2022, respectively, the U.S. International Development Finance Corporation (DFC) approved a $30 million loan and $25 million equity investment for Ireland-based TechMet Limited to operate and expand a nickel and cobalt mine in the Brazilian state of Piauí.
  • In 2025, DFC approved $5 million in pre-investment project development financing for Aclara Resources Mineração Ltda, the Brazilian subsidiary of a Canadian-listed company, to study the feasibility of mining and processing rare earth elements in the Brazilian state of Goiás.
  • In February 2026, DFC approved $565 million in debt financing for SVRE Holdings Ltd., a British Virgin Islands-based holding company, to improve the Pela Ema rare earth elements mine in Goiás.
  • In August 2026, the U.S. Department of Defense (DOD) announced it had mobilized $1.55 billion in funding166—including a $750 million investment through DOD's Industrial Base Analysis and Sustainment program, a $300 million purchase commitment from the Defense Logistics Agency, and a $500 million commitment from a money-center bank—to support a rare earth element offtake agreement.167 The funds supported U.S.-based USA Rare Earth's acquisition of SVRE Holdings Ltd and its Pela Ema mine in Goiás.168

The Lula administration has sought increased FDI in the critical minerals sector but reportedly has been reluctant to enter into agreements that would grant exclusive purchasing rights to the United States (or other countries).169 In September 2026, the Brazilian government enacted a new National Policy for Critical and Strategic Minerals.170 Among other provisions, the legislation provides R$2 billion (about $383 million) for activities linked to critical mineral production and R$5 billion (about $957 million) in incentives to develop critical mineral processing in Brazil. The legislation also creates a National Council for the Industrialization of Critical and Strategic Minerals linked to the presidency to regulate the sector, including the power to approve mergers and acquisitions. It is unclear if Brazil's approach to the sector would allow for Brazil's participation in a potential "plurilateral agreement on trade in critical minerals and downstream products" that USTR aims to negotiate with "like-minded partners."171

Options for Congress

Congress has primary authority over U.S. trade policy through its constitutional power to levy tariffs and regulate foreign commerce, though it has delegated some of that authority to the executive branch.172 Some Members of Congress have argued that the President has abused his trade authorities by imposing IEEPA and Section 301 tariffs to interfere in Brazil's justice system.173 Some other Members have expressed support for the President's efforts to negotiate a new trade arrangement with Brazil while calling for more targeted tariffs.174 Members could assess the trajectory of U.S.-Brazilian trade relations as well as the potential implications of the Trump Administration's trade policies. Such assessments could inform congressional decisions regarding potential measures to codify, terminate, or modify the Administration's tariffs on imports from Brazil. Congress also could set negotiating objectives for any trade discussions with the Brazilian government, including discussions related to critical minerals. Additionally, Congress could consider whether or not to reauthorize the Generalized System of Preferences (GSP) program, which provided nonreciprocal, duty-free tariff treatment to certain products imported from designated developing countries, prior to its expiration on December 31, 2020.175 Brazil was the third-largest beneficiary of the program in 2020, with duty-free U.S. imports from Brazil valued at $2.2 billion.176

Security Cooperation

Although U.S.-Brazilian security cooperation has been limited at times due to political disputes and policy differences, bilateral military and law enforcement ties grew closer between 2015 and 2024. During that period, the U.S. and Brazilian governments held regular high-level security discussions, the countries' armed forces participated in joint training and exercises, and U.S. and Brazilian law enforcement agencies cooperated on counternarcotics efforts, among other issues. The United States also provided some military and law enforcement aid to Brazilian security forces intended to strengthen their capacities and foster interoperability and collaboration. Some security cooperation appears to have continued during the second Trump Administration, despite differing approaches to some security challenges and tensions in the broader bilateral relationship.

Defense

The United States and Brazil are both parties to the Inter-American Treaty of Reciprocal Assistance, a collective security pact signed in 1947, but bilateral defense cooperation has varied over time.177 During the 1970s, for example, Brazil withdrew from a series of military agreements with the United States in response to U.S. criticism on human rights issues and U.S. opposition to a Brazilian nuclear agreement with West Germany. The countries did not conclude a new umbrella Defense Cooperation Agreement until 2010. That same year, Brazil and the United States signed a General Security of Military Information Agreement intended to facilitate the sharing of classified information. The Brazilian congress did not approve either of those agreements until 2015, due in part to a cooling of relations after press reports revealed that the U.S. National Security Agency had engaged in extensive electronic surveillance in Brazil.178 These defense agreements, negotiated as executive agreements, did not require U.S. congressional approval.179

As noted previously, in 2019, President Trump designated Brazil as a major non-NATO ally for the purposes of the Foreign Assistance Act of 1961, as amended (22 U.S.C. §§2151 et seq.), and the Arms Export Control Act (22 U.S.C. §§2751 et seq.).180 That designation made Brazil eligible for privileged access to the U.S. defense industry and increased joint military exchanges, exercises, and training, among other benefits. According to the U.S. Embassy in Brazil, a 2020 Research, Development, Test, and Evaluation Agreement enabled bilateral collaboration on basic, exploratory, and advanced technologies at a "level enjoyed only by NATO allies and the closest strategic security partners of the United States."181

Some defense cooperation has continued during the second Trump Administration. U.S. and Brazilian forces have engaged in some multinational and bilateral military exercises and engagements, although press reports suggest a few planned events may have been canceled amid bilateral tensions.182 Brazil has continued to purchase some U.S. military equipment. In June 2026, for example, the State Department informed Congress that it had approved a potential Foreign Military Sale to the Brazilian government of Stinger missiles and related equipment valued at $330 million.183 The United States also has provided some military assistance to Brazil. In FY2025, the State Department obligated $1 million in International Military Education and Training aid to support Brazilian military officer training at U.S. professional military education institutions.184

Counternarcotics and Law Enforcement

Brazil is not among the countries that President Trump designated as major producers or transit points for illicit drugs that significantly affect the United States for FY2027.185 Brazil is the world's second-largest consumer of cocaine (after the United States), however, and serves as a transit point for some illicit drugs destined for Africa, Europe, and the United States, according to the U.S. State Department.186 Several large, well-organized, and heavily armed criminal groups in Brazil—such as the First Capital Command (Primeiro Comando da Capital, or PCC) and the Red Command (Comando Vermelho, or CV)—have a transnational reach. Some analysts assess that violence in Brazil is closely correlated with battles among the PCC, CV, and their allies over drug trafficking routes.187 These groups are also reportedly involved in various other illicit activities, including arms trafficking and illegal mining in the Amazon.188

U.S. and Brazilian authorities have worked together to combat drug trafficking and other organized crime. In April 2026, for example, Brazil's Federal Revenue Service and U.S. Customs and Border Protection created a new Mutual Interdiction Team to identify and intercept illicit arms and drug shipments between the two countries. A digital platform allows U.S. and Brazilian authorities to share information in real time, identify patterns, and map illicit networks.189 Such intelligence sharing reportedly has contributed to multiple arms and drug seizures, including a June 2026 joint operation involving the Brazilian Federal Police, the U.S. Drug Enforcement Administration, and Bolivian authorities that seized 50 tons of cocaine.190

Nevertheless, the U.S. and Brazilian governments have disagreed at times on some counternarcotics matters. In June 2026, the Trump Administration designated the PCC and CV as foreign terrorist organizations (FTOs) and specially designated global terrorists (SDGTs).191 Consequences of these designations include restrictions on entry into the United States, asset-blocking sanctions, and secondary sanctions risks for foreign financial institutions that knowingly facilitate transactions on behalf of sanctioned persons.192 The Lula administration opposed the FTO and SDGT designations and expressed concern that they could undermine Brazil's sovereignty and economy and pave the way for unilateral U.S. military strikes on Brazilian territory.193 Then-opposition presidential candidate Flávio Bolsonaro, who met with President Trump at the White House two days prior to the U.S. announcement, reportedly celebrated and claimed credit for the decision.194 The Trump Administration maintains that the Brazilian government has "failed to confront" the PCC and CV, and that the organizations are "a growing threat to peace and security around the world."195

Strains in the broader U.S.-Brazil relationship reportedly have interrupted some other law enforcement cooperation. In April 2026, the State Department expelled a Brazilian Federal Police liaison from the United States for allegedly seeking to "circumvent formal extradition requests" and "extend political witch hunts into U.S. territory."196 The Brazilian officer reportedly had been involved in U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement's (ICE's) detention of Alexandre Ramagem, who is seeking asylum in the United States after being sentenced to 16 years in prison in Brazil for his involvement in Bolsonaro's attempted coup while serving as the head of Brazil's intelligence agency. Brazil has requested Ramagem's extradition; ICE reportedly released him two days after his detention.197 Brazil responded to the expulsion by revoking the credentials of a U.S. law enforcement officer in Brasília. As of September 2026, the successors of the expelled Brazilian and U.S. officers reportedly had been unable to take up their posts due to the State Department withholding visas and the Brazilian government responding in kind.198

Options for Congress

Congress oversees bilateral security ties and authorizes and appropriates funding for security cooperation programs. Among other actions, Members could assess the current state of U.S.-Brazilian security relations, including the extent to which strains in the broader U.S.-Brazil relationship have affected cooperation on defense and law enforcement matters. During the FY2027 appropriations process, Congress could consider whether to appropriate military and/or law enforcement assistance specifically for Brazil or to leave such allocation decisions to the Trump Administration. The National Security, Department of State, and Related Programs (NSRP) Appropriations Act, 2027 (H.R. 8595/H.Rept. 119-631), as passed by the House in July 2026, would not designate any security assistance specifically for Brazil. However, the bill would designate $20 million in International Narcotics Control and Law Enforcement aid for the Southern Cone Cooperation Program, which historically has included Brazil. The Senate Appropriations Committee has yet to mark up an FY2027 NSRP appropriations bill.

Environmental Cooperation

In 2023 and 2024, Presidents Biden and Lula placed environmental cooperation at the center of the U.S.-Brazil relationship. In addition to increasing diplomatic engagement on such issues, the Biden Administration sought to increase U.S. financial support for conservation in the Brazilian Amazon. In 2023, President Biden announced his intention to seek $500 million from Congress over five years for Brazil's Amazon Fund.199 The Biden Administration asserted that such funding was "a top priority for—and consistent ask from—the Brazilian government" and "central to strengthening the U.S. partnership with Brazil."200 The Biden Administration ultimately delivered $53.5 million to Brazil for the Amazon Fund between December 2023 and October 2024.201

Congress has designated some funding for U.S.-managed foreign assistance programs in the Brazilian Amazon in annual appropriations legislation but has not directed any funding to the Amazon Fund specifically. For example, the Department of State, Foreign Operations, and Related Programs Appropriations Act, 2024 (P.L. 118-47, Division F) designated $23.75 million for environment programs in the Brazilian Amazon.202 The Full-Year Continuing Appropriations and Extensions Act, 2025 (P.L. 119-4) funded U.S. foreign assistance programs in Brazil at the same level, and under the same conditions and authority, as FY2024. The FY2026 NSRP Appropriations Act (P.L. 119-75, Division F) designated $38.0 million for programs across the Amazon region.203

The Trump Administration's foreign assistance policies have affected the implementation of these funds. The Administration dismantled the U.S. Agency for International Development (USAID), which had been the lead U.S. agency responsible for managing environmental assistance programs in Brazil.204 The Administration also terminated nearly all USAID- and U.S. Forest Service-managed environmental programs in Brazil.205 Such programs generally provided support to Brazilian government institutions, nongovernmental organizations, and/or communities—including Indigenous peoples and Quilombolas—with the stated purpose of strengthening protected area management and promoting sustainable economic activities, among other objectives.206 It is unclear if the Trump Administration intends to issue any new awards for conservation efforts in Brazil.

The status of other U.S. agencies' engagement with Brazil on environmental matters is uncertain. NASA historically has provided some data and technical support to Brazil to help the country better monitor Amazon deforestation. At least some of that support, which had been facilitated by USAID, has ended.207 Other agencies, such as the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, have assisted Brazil in law enforcement to combat wildlife trafficking, illegal logging, and other conservation crimes. As of 2026, the U.S. State Department continued to identify Brazil as a "focus country" for anti-wildlife trafficking efforts, pursuant to the Eliminate, Neutralize, and Disrupt (END) Wildlife Trafficking Act of 2016, as amended (P.L. 114-231; 16 U.S.C. §§7601-7644).208

Options for Congress

Congress may examine U.S.-Brazilian cooperation on environmental matters and whether and, if so, how to influence the trajectory of such cooperation. During the FY2027 appropriations process, Congress may assess whether to designate funding for environmental programs in the Brazilian Amazon; the Trump Administration did not request any such funding. The FY2027 NSRP appropriations bill (H.R. 8595/H.Rept. 119-631), as passed by the House in July 2026, would not specifically designate any funds for the Brazilian Amazon. The bill would provide "not less than" $274.3 million for biodiversity conservation programs worldwide, as well as $100 million for the United States Foundation for Natural Security and Counterterrorism, a nonprofit corporation that is to provide grants to support protected areas in countries with "a high degree of biodiversity or species and ecosystems of significant ecological value."209 The Senate Appropriations Committee has yet to mark up a FY2027 NSRP appropriations bill. Congress also could consider other legislative measures, such as the Strengthening the Rule of Law in the Brazilian Amazon Act (S. 2578), reported by the Senate Committee on Foreign Relations in July 2026, which aims to strengthen U.S.-Brazilian cooperation in combatting environmental crimes and support sustainable economic opportunities in the Amazon region.
