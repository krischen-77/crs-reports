Floodplain Buyouts: Federal Funding for Property Acquisition

Updated November 24, 2026
(IN11911)

Flooding is the [most frequent natural disaster](https://www.dhs.gov/natural-disasters) in the United States and [experts expect effects to intensify in the future](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg2/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGII_SummaryForPolicymakers.pdf). A [floodplain buyout](https://knowledge.uli.org/-/media/files/research-reports/2021/onsaferground_final_june14.pdf) is a property acquisition in which a government agency purchases private property, relocates or demolishes any structures on it, and preserves the land as [open space](https://www.fema.gov/sites/default/files/2020-07/fema_DRRA-1231-acquisition-fact-sheet.pdf). Buyouts offer one way to reduce flood risk. Unlike other approaches to flood mitigation, buyouts eliminate future losses by removing properties from areas at risk of flooding. Buyouts can also improve [stormwater management](https://code200-external.gsfc.nasa.gov/250/environmental/storm-water-management), restore and conserve [natural floodplain functions](https://www.fema.gov/floodplain-management/wildlife-conservation/benefits-natural), and provide other [ecosystem services](https://www.climatehubs.usda.gov/ecosystem-services).

Federal Funding for Buyouts

Flood buyouts can be funded by several federal programs:

* Any of the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) [Hazard Mitigation Assistance](https://www.crs.gov/Reports/R46989) (HMA) grant programs: [Building Resilient Infrastructure and Communities](https://www.fema.gov/grants/mitigation/learn/building-resilient-infrastructure-communities) (BRIC); the [Hazard Mitigation Grant Program](https://www.fema.gov/grants/mitigation/hazard-mitigation) (HMGP), the [Flood Mitigation Assistance Grant Program](https://www.fema.gov/grants/mitigation/floods) (FMA), and the [Safeguarding Tomorrow Revolving Loan Fund Program](https://www.crs.gov/Reports/IN12224);
* National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) [Increased Cost of Compliance Coverage](https://www.fema.gov/floodplain-management/financial-help/increased-cost-compliance);
* Department of Housing and Urban Development [Community Development Block Grant Disaster Recovery](https://www.hudexchange.info/programs/cdbg-dr/disaster-recovery-buyout-program/#buyout-program-overview-considerations-and-strategies) (CDBG-DR) program;

and under some circumstances:

* FEMA [Public Assistance](https://www.fema.gov/sites/default/files/documents/fema_pappg-v4-updated-links_policy_6-1-2020.pdf);
* Department of Agriculture [Emergency Watershed Protection Program](https://www.nrcs.usda.gov/programs-initiatives/ewp-emergency-watershed-protection);
* [National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration land acquisition](https://coast.noaa.gov/czm/landconservation/resources/) projects;
* [Land and Water Conservation Fund](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/lwcf/federalside.htm);
* [United States Army Corps of Engineers](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2015-title33/pdf/USCODE-2015-title33-chap15-sec701b-11.pdf) flood risk reduction projects; and
* Department of Interior [Voluntary Community-Driven Relocation Program](https://www.bia.gov/service/community-driven-relocation).

FEMA Floodplain Buyouts

Although there is no database that identifies all federal funding for floodplain buyouts, some research indicates that the [majority of buyout funding](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/12/23/10112) has been provided by FEMA. [FEMA-funded buyouts are entirely voluntary](https://www.fema.gov/news-release/20200220/fey-enfomasyon-akizisyon-pwopriyete-apre-yon-inondasyon) and property owners cannot be forced to participate. In the case of [property acquisition](https://help.riskfactor.com/hc/en-us/articles/360050891613--Participate-in-buyouts-and-acquisition-programs) and demolition, a local or state government purchases flood-prone land and structures and demolishes the structures. Alternatively, state or local governments purchase land and the property owners [relocate](https://www.fema.gov/hmgp-appeal-categories/property-acquisition-and-structure-relocation) to another site. If the new location is in a [Special Flood Hazard Area](https://www.fema.gov/glossary/special-flood-hazard-area-sfha) (SFHA), the structure must meet the community's [floodplain management ordinances](https://www.fema.gov/glossary/floodplain-management-ordinances). All FEMA-funded projects must comply with open space and other regulations in [44 C.F.R. Part 80](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-1996-title44-vol1/pdf/CFR-1996-title44-vol1-part80.pdf). After the date of property settlement, no federal entity may provide [disaster assistance](https://www.disasterassistance.gov/) to households or businesses [for any purpose with respect to the property](https://www.fema.gov/sites/default/files/2020-07/fy15_hma_addendum.pdf). The property is not eligible for NFIP coverage for structural damage after the date of settlement.

Considerations for Congress

Length of Time for Buyouts

One of the biggest considerations for homeowners is the speed of the buyout. Some studies show that the average FEMA HMGP buyout project [takes over five years](https://www.nrdc.org/resources/going-under-long-wait-times-post-flood-buyouts-leave-homeowners-underwater) from the start of the associated disaster to project closeout, although property acquisition typically occurs before a funding program is closed out. According to FEMA, the average time to obligate HMGP funding for buyouts is 19.5 months, with 80% of acquisitions approved in under two years and 93% in three years or less ([**Figure 1**](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IN/HTML/IN11911.html#_Ref100311866)). According to FEMA, the average time to obligate FMA-funded buyouts is 16 months.

Funding may be insufficient to buy out all of the properties requested, and volunteers on waiting lists may [return to their flooded homes as they wait](https://features.propublica.org/houston-buyouts/hurricane-harvey-home-buyouts-harris-county/) for a buyout. If they repair their flood-damaged house for habitability while waiting, they [may no longer wish to relocate](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10168608). Longer buyout timelines [may restrict the opportunity for lower-income households to participate](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10584-022-03453-5).

Challenges for Local Governments

Buyout programs may be controversial, even though they reduce long-term flood risk. Buyouts may [reduce state and local income from property taxes](https://esg.wharton.upenn.edu/news/must-floodplain-buyouts-decrease-tax-revenue/), reduce housing stock, cause a decline in real estate values in neighborhoods with large numbers of vacant lots, create fears that low-income communities are being removed, or fragment communities through resident displacement and loss of neighborhood cohesion. The local government is responsible for maintaining parcels of bought-out land and buyout programs generally do not include funding for future design, maintenance, or use of bought-out land. Nonfederal [cost-share](https://www.crs.gov/Reports/R47646) requirements and the cost of maintaining open space may prove to be a disincentive, particularly for small or disadvantaged communities.

With voluntary buyout programs, some properties are acquired and converted while others are left to remain, creating a "checkerboard" effect with some properties surrounded by vacant or underutilized parcels. This may [create numerous problems](https://www.flseagrant.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/Managing.pdf), including blight, community fragmentation, difficulty with providing municipal services, and inability to restore full floodplain functionality.

For these reasons, buyouts are often a [politically unpopular option unless there is a particularly catastrophic event](https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-20-488.pdf) that changes people's willingness to move and creates unified state and local support for relocation.

Policy Options

There are a number of policy options available to increase participation in buyout programs. Agencies could dedicate a special round of funding for buyouts, offer a higher federal cost-share for buyouts that relocate contiguous properties, or provide greater assistance to low-income communities for post-buyout maintenance. Homeowners are generally offered the pre-disaster fair market value of the property. Programs could incentivize participation by offering [higher payments to low-income residents](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9607794/) or [encouraging relocation in a lower-risk area](https://stormrecovery.ny.gov/sites/default/files/crp/community/documents/20210729_BuyoutAcquisition_PolicyManual_7.1_Final_0.pdf).

One option to speed up buyouts could be to fast-track funding to be available immediately after a disaster for buyouts. Local governments could be reimbursed up to a specified percentage of total federal funding if they undertake buyouts immediately after a disaster, or an agency could establish a pre-approval process. States and communities could pre-approve or guarantee buyouts to interested homeowners before a flood, or acquire homes and rent them back to owners or tenants for as long as the property remains habitable. For example, the federal government has used [life estates](http://npshistory.com/publications/residential-occupancy.pdf) to acquire property for its own use, such as national parks, allowing homeowners to remain for the rest of their lives or until they choose to leave. A similar approach could be used to encourage flood buyouts.

Federal agencies usually do not track the [new locations of households after a buyout](https://www.npr.org/2023/06/15/1181693629/climate-change-is-causing-people-to-move-they-usually-stay-local-study-finds), so it is typically not known whether households have moved to a less vulnerable location. Buyout programs could provide more assistance in the relocation process and greater emphasis on developing affordable housing in nonvulnerable locations.
