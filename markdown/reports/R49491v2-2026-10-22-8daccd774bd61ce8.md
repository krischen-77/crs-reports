Introduction

When Congress does not appropriate funding for federal agencies and programs or when the
President and Congress do not reach an agreement on funding measures after full-year or interim
appropriations (commonly known as a “continuing resolution [CR]”) expire, affected government
agencies and programs will experience a lapse in appropriations. This funding disruption is
known as a “funding gap” or commonly referred to as a federal government “shutdown.”
Shutdowns can be government-wide or partial, meaning they affect all or certain federal agencies
and programs depending on what appropriations lapse. Generally, shutting down agencies and
programs results in federal employee furloughs and suspensions of programs and operations.

Shutdowns can affect some civil aviation programs that are administered by the Department of
Transportation (DOT), including those managed by the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA),
as well as aviation security functions carried out by other federal entities that also play significant
roles in civil aviation, particularly the Transportation Security Administration (TSA) under the
Department of Homeland Security (DHS). Although large numbers of federal employees are
typically furloughed during a shutdown, many FAA and TSA employees are “excepted,” meaning
they are to remain on the job. During a shutdown, pay for these excepted workers is typically
withheld until appropriations are enacted. FAA air traffic controllers and systems technicians, as
well as TSA screeners and certain other employees, are excepted because their work is directly
tied to the operation of the civil aviation system and considered essential to protect lives and
property. Various other employees at these agencies also continue to work because their duties are
linked to funding that is separate from annual appropriations or because their work is directly
related to certain presidential powers, constitutional requirements, or statutes that expressly or
implicitly except them from being furloughed. Excepted personnel may experience personal
financial burdens as they work while their pay is deferred.1 These financial strains are associated
with increased callouts (employees’ requests for unscheduled leave or temporary furlough for
personal reasons) and resignations during shutdowns.2

This report focuses on federal government shutdowns that are associated with lapses in annual
appropriations and the direct effect on civil aviation operations and programs. It examines
impacts on civil aviation during selected shutdowns: the 2018-2019 partial government
shutdown; the government-wide shutdown in October-November 2025; the partial government
shutdown from January 31 through February 3, 2026; and the partial shutdown affecting DHS
from February 14 to April 30, 2026. It also provides (1) a brief overview of the funding
mechanism supporting agency operations and aviation-related programs; (2) discussion and
analysis of how a lapse in appropriations and resulting government shutdown may affect DOT
(particularly FAA) and TSA, as well as the recipients of their services (e.g., airlines, airports, and
their customers); and (3) legislative options and actions that might avert or alleviate the effects of
a future shutdown.

Federal Government Shutdowns

Funding Lapses and Shutdowns

When federal agencies and programs do not receive funding after full-year or interim
appropriations expire, they experience a funding gap due to a lapse in appropriations. If funding
does not resume in time to continue government operations, the government generally begins a
shutdown of affected activities,3 though not all funding gaps in the past led to government
shutdowns.

Twenty-three lapses in appropriations occurred between FY1977 and FY2026. Some of these
funding gaps were brief and do not appear to have resulted in government shutdowns, while some
other funding gaps did lead to shutdowns.4 The four most recent shutdowns, examined in detail in
this report, were lengthier and more disruptive to civil aviation than prior shutdowns. Some
observers have attributed the current budget process and political climate to shutdowns that may
be longer, more frequent, and more disruptive than in the past.5

Since the federal government plays a key role in operating the National Airspace System (NAS),
providing air traffic control (ATC) and air navigation services, and conducting aviation security
operations at the nation’s airports, the aviation industry can be directly and significantly impacted
by government shutdowns. Even though federal workers who perform these services are
generally excepted from furlough during a shutdown, the fact that they typically are not paid
during a shutdown has demonstrated impacts on absenteeism and resignations, potentially
affecting the federal government’s ability to fully provide these services without disruption or
degradation.

The federal government’s discretionary spending is generally funded through the passage of 12
separate annual appropriations bills from 12 appropriations subcommittees in the House of
Representatives and their counterparts in the Senate, each covering distinct areas of federal
functions (such as defense, homeland security, transportation, and agriculture). When there is a
lapse in all these appropriations bills, then a full-scale government-wide shutdown occurs. If a
lapse occurs to some of the appropriations bills, then there is likely to be an agency-specific
partial shutdown. FAA activities are covered in DOT appropriations, and TSA aviation security
functions are funded under DHS appropriations.

On October 1, 2018, the beginning of FY2019, five of the twelve appropriations bills were
enacted in two consolidated appropriations bills,6 and the remaining seven were funded under two
CRs. Because no legislative agreement was reached to extend the CRs when they expired, a
funding gap began at midnight on December 21, 2018, which led to a partial government

Agency Planning and Preparation

Executive branch departments and agencies prepare plans for government shutdowns in
accordance with formal guidelines and periodic update requirements established by the Office of
Management and Budget (OMB).8 These plans provide information regarding the number of
agency employees that are to be retained and the number that are to be furloughed in the event of
a shutdown, along with a general categorization of retained employees (those excepted or
exempted from furlough),9 a description of the nature of activities to be performed by these
employees, and the legal basis for retaining them.10 One broad category describing many
excepted FAA and TSA workers are employees who are “necessary to protect life and property.”11
Other categories include employees who perform activities expressly authorized by law, activities
implied by law, or activities related to the President’s constitutional duties and powers.
Employees whose compensation is financed by carryover funds or by an appropriation specified
in permanent law are generally exempted from furlough under an agency shutdown.12

Federal Aviation Administration

Under the DOT shutdown plan issued in January 2026, roughly 34,000 FAA employees would be
excepted during a lapse in appropriations and about 10,500 would be furloughed.13 Most who
would be excepted have jobs that are considered necessary to protect life and property; others
would continue to work because their duties are either expressly or implicitly authorized under
statute or are funded outside of annual appropriations.

The plan lists several excepted activities rather than identifying specific employees or job
positions that would be required to continue working. Compared with prior DOT shutdown plans,
a broader range of activities are specified in the January 2026 plan.14 Excepted activities include
ATC operations and air traffic controller hiring and training. The plan specifically notes that
almost 14,000 air traffic controllers would be excepted. Past DOT shutdown plans did not include
air traffic controller hiring and training among excepted activities.15 FAA has experienced a
shortage of controllers, and inclusion of hiring and training activities in the January 2026 plan
may reflect concerns that pauses in these activities could prolong efforts to hire and train new
controllers. The January 2026 plan notes that training at the FAA Academy for new air traffic
controllers would continue under any existing prior-year funds until those funds were exhausted
and thereafter would continue as an excepted activity.

Activities relating to command, control, communications, and intelligence, as well as facility
security inspections, would also be excepted under the plan. Limited elements of air traffic safety
oversight, including activities of the voluntary safety reporting program event review committee
and control tower operator certification and credentialing, also would be excepted.

Activities relating to the maintenance and operations of air navigation facilities and navigational
aids would be excepted, along with certain support functions for reimbursable Department of
Defense16 and DHS activities. Activities concerning the development, evaluation, testing, and
deployment of air traffic modernization technologies, equipment, and systems that sustain or
improve safety would be excepted, which could encompass a broad range of work being
conducted under a new effort called the “Brand New Air Traffic Control System.”17

Commercial space launch oversight and licensing activities would be excepted, as would on-call
accident investigation activities and foreign-relations-related functions concerning aviation-
safety-related matters. Also, support functions necessary to provide timely payments of contracts
and grants and certain congressional liaison functions would be excepted.

Various aviation safety functions, including flight standards field inspections, airworthiness
directives, and medical certification activities, would be excepted under the plan. In contrast,
during the 2018-2019 partial shutdown, most aviation safety inspectors were initially furloughed

Transportation Security Administration

The DHS shutdown plan issued in September 2025 indicates that roughly 61,000 of about 64,000
TSA employees would be excepted or exempted from furlough during a lapse in appropriations.22
The plan notes that these totals do not include Federal Air Marshal Service (FAMS) personnel,
whose numbers are considered Sensitive Security Information. Therefore, the undisclosed number
of FAMS employees excepted from furlough during a shutdown would be in addition to other
excepted or exempted TSA employees.

The DHS plan provides a general summary rather than a detailed description of activities that
would continue or cease during a lapse in appropriations. Activities that would continue include
those deemed necessary by interpretation of existing law and the Constitution or necessary for the
safety of human life or the protection of property. Also, law enforcement operations,
counterterrorism watches, threat warnings, and related activities to gather or disseminate
intelligence information would be expressly excepted. The plan also calls for retaining a minimal
number of personnel necessary to maintain telecommunications for excepted activities.

Under the DHS plan, activities that would cease under a lapse in appropriations include most
administrative, policy, and planning functions, including strategic or business activities, as well as
research and development activities. Additionally, legislative, public, and intergovernmental
affairs; regulatory development; and auditing functions would cease unless justified by an
exception. Moreover, any training and development that is not deemed an excepted activity would
cease for the duration of a shutdown.

Funding of Aviation Programs and Operations

FAA Operations and Programs

FAA operations and programs are funded under four broad budget accounts: (1) Operations and
Maintenance (O&M) funds the administration, operation, repair, and maintenance of the NAS—
principally air traffic operations and aviation safety programs; (2) Facilities and Equipment
(F&E) provides funding for the acquisition and maintenance of air traffic facilities and equipment
and for engineering, development, testing, and evaluation of technologies related to the federal air
traffic system; (3) Grants-in-Aid for Airports (also known as AIP) funds the development of a
nationwide system of public-use airports to meet the current capacity needs and the projected
growth of civil aviation; and (4) Research, Engineering, and Development (RE&D) supports
research on the improvement of aviation safety and operational efficiency and reduction of
environmental impacts of aviation operations.

These four major FAA accounts are funded primarily through the Airport and Airway Trust Fund
(AATF)—a dedicated U.S. Treasury fund—with the remainder coming from Treasury General
Fund (general fund) appropriations. Revenue sources for the AATF include passenger ticket taxes,
segment fees, air cargo fees, and fuel taxes paid by commercial and general aviation aircraft. The
trust fund revenues from these excise taxes were estimated by the Congressional Budget Office
(CBO) to exceed $21 billion in FY2026, leaving a balance of over $23 billion at the end of
FY2026, including an uncommitted balance of more than $10 billion.23

The O&M account, funded by the AATF and general fund contributions, receives approximately
60% of total FAA appropriations—about $13.7 billion for FY2026. The F&E and AIP accounts
were funded at $4 billion each in FY2026. The smallest account, RE&D, received $290 million
for FY2026.

Some aviation programs are administered by other parts of DOT, such as the Essential Air Service
program (EAS) under the DOT Office of the Secretary.24 Established by the Airline Deregulation
Act of 1978, EAS subsidizes airline services to approximately 175 eligible small or isolated
communities. EAS is partially funded by overflight fees imposed on flights that fly over U.S.-
controlled airspace but do not take off from or land in the United States; the remaining funding is
from discretionary appropriations from the general fund. EAS funding for FY2026 was over $680
million, with about 75% from appropriations and nearly 25% (about $167 million) from
overflight fees.25 During the FY2026 government-wide shutdown that started on October 1, 2025,
DOT issued a notice stating that the potential shortfall of appropriated EAS funding could lead to
the program’s suspension, which was averted after additional program funding was secured.26

Lapse in Authorization

Both the authority to collect aviation excise taxes and the authority to spend tax revenues from
the AATF must be reauthorized periodically by Congress. FAA reauthorization refers to a periodic
process through which Congress develops legislation to renew authorizing statutes as well as
revise and update relevant laws governing civil aviation programs and functions primarily carried
out by FAA. The FAA Reauthorization Act of 2024 (P.L. 118-63) authorized AATF taxes and
revenue collections and civil aviation program expenditures through FY2028, with annual FAA
expenditures ranging from approximately $20 billion to over $22 billion.

Congress would need to enact legislation to reauthorize or extend the authorities for aviation
excise tax collection and for FAA program funding to prevent them from expiring. There have
been a few cases when FAA authorization expired without Congress passing full reauthorization
legislation, although usually short-term extensions were passed to avoid lapses in these
authorities.

The most recent lapse in FAA’s authority to collect AATF revenues and expend money from the
trust fund occurred in 2011 and lasted for two weeks, from July 23 to August 5. FAA estimated
that the 2011 lapse resulted in approximately $200 million of forgone trust fund revenues each
week, or about $400 million for the entire lapse. Although the Airport and Airway Extension Act
of 2011 (P.L. 112-27), signed into law on August 5, 2011, retroactively reinstated the taxes as
though they had never expired, the Internal Revenue Service granted relief to airlines and to
taxpayers who purchased tickets during the lapse.27

                         Suspension of Aviation Taxes
 Congress also has the authority to suspend aviation excise taxes, which is not a lapse in authorization but may
 have a similar effect on trust fund revenue collections. In early 2020, air travel dropped significantly because of the
 COVID-19 pandemic. The Coronavirus Aid, Relief, and Economic Security (CARES) Act (P.L. 116-136), enacted on
 March 27, 2020, provided loans, loan guarantees, and payroll support programs, along with emergency program
 funding, as economic assistance to the U.S. aviation sector. Section 4007 of the CARES Act authorized suspension
 of aviation excise taxes through calendar year 2020 to reduce overall costs and encourage air travel. The
 suspended taxes included the 7.5% tax on airline passenger ticket sales, segment fees, the air cargo waybill tax, and
 the tax on aviation fuel used by commercial aircraft.


Lapse in Appropriations

With FAA reauthorization in place, most of FAA’s spending also requires annual appropriations
by Congress, except spending for AIP. AIP funding is disbursed as contract authority,28 which
permits funds to be committed prior to an annual appropriation. Funds associated with other
accounts (i.e., O&M, F&E, and RE&D) may be spent only with a corresponding congressional
appropriation.

Transportation Security Administration Funding

TSA derives revenue from the general fund. A portion of the TSA revenue allocated for aviation
security functions is received from the collection of aviation security service fees charged to
airline passengers. The fees are set in statute at $5.60 per passenger for a one-way trip and are
capped to not exceed $11.20 for each passenger round trip purchased.31 These fees are collected
by airlines at the time of ticket purchase and remitted to the U.S. Treasury one month in arrears at
the end of each calendar month. Some of the passenger security fees are treated as offsetting
collections that are credited toward amounts appropriated for TSA aviation security operations,
but only after certain other statutory obligations have been met.

By statute, the first $250 million collected in fees is deposited into the Aviation Security Capital
Fund (ASCF), which was established to fund airport grants for the installation and integration of
explosives detection systems and for other airport security capital improvement projects.32 This
money is therefore unavailable for TSA aviation security operations. This fee-based funding
structure of the ASCF is set to expire at the end of FY2028 unless reauthorized.

Since FY2014, there has been an additional statutory requirement to treat a specified amount of
fees received after the $250 million has been collected and applied to the ASCF as “offsetting
receipts” to the general fund, essentially serving as a revenue source for general deficit reduction
and not specifically for TSA aviation security operations.33 The amounts to be treated in this
manner are specified in statute and vary from fiscal year to fiscal year.34 Specified amounts
include $1,640 million for FY2026 and $1,680 million for FY2027. After FY2027, the
requirement to treat specified amounts of fees collected in this manner is set to cease unless
extended.

Additional passenger security service fees collected beyond the amounts applied to the ASCF and
treated as offsetting receipts are placed in the general fund and are to be treated as “offsetting
collections” against appropriated amounts specified for aviation security measures carried out by
TSA and are to remain available until expended. Therefore, while the authority to collect these

passenger security fees available for expenditure during a lapse in appropriations. Under existing
statute, the authority to collect mandatory passenger security fees continues during a lapse in
appropriations, and airlines must continue to remit fees that they collect. However, the existing
statute may limit the availability of these funds, especially early in a fiscal year, since both the

Overall FAA Programs and Operations

FAA regulates the safety of aircraft, aircraft operators, safety-critical personnel, and the NAS. It
also directly operates the NAS by providing ATC and air navigation services to users of the
system. In general, the day-to-day operations of the NAS are considered necessary to protect life
and property, and FAA employees whose jobs are central to the operation of the system are
generally excepted from furlough during a shutdown. Similarly, certain regulatory oversight
functions, such as inspections to ensure that users of the NAS (including airlines, other aircraft
operators, and facilities that perform aircraft maintenance) are in compliance with operational
safety standards, are also generally considered necessary to protect life and property. Other
employees whose functions are not directly related to the day-to-day operation and safety of
existing airspace and aircraft may be excepted, although Administration interpretations regarding
which FAA employees are furloughed and which are excepted have varied. For example, during
the 2018-2019 partial shutdown, employees performing aircraft certification oversight functions
were initially furloughed, but some were recalled in mid-January 2019.39 In contrast, the January
2026 DOT shutdown plan would expressly except certain certification activities.40

Certain regulatory activities, such as the issuance of airworthiness directives to correct safety
issues identified on operational aircraft, would continue during a shutdown. Other rulemaking
activities that are deemed less critical may be put on pause until a shutdown has ended. Also,
administrative functions, including the administration and oversight of certain contracts and
grants, may be paused during a shutdown. The January 2026 DOT plan excepts certain support
functions to issue payments to contractors and grant recipients. Both AIP grants and contracts for
ATC modernization paid from P.L. 119-21 funds, for example, would continue during a
shutdown, and some FAA workers who administer these contracts and grants would remain on
the job to disburse payments and fulfill financial obligations. Under the January 2026 DOT
shutdown plan, various activities to modernize ATC systems that sustain or improve safety would
be excepted, contrasting with prior plans that generally limited excepted ATC activities to the
operation and upkeep of deployed systems and equipment. This in part reflects the funding for
these activities provided for in the FY2025 reconciliation law that is not tied to annual
appropriations.

flying within U.S. airspace. In addition, almost 40,000 additional visual flight rules (VFR) aircraft
operate inside U.S. airspace on a typical day.41 Some of these flights may receive in-flight ATC
assistance, called “flight following,” and those that operate to or from towered airports receive
ATC services during taxi, takeoff, and landing.

hiring and training. During the 2018-2019 partial shutdown, FAA Academy training was halted

newly hired air traffic controllers. This pause, along with a much longer hiring and training pause

  staffing. In March 2020, FAA suspended new-hire training, which did not restart until July 2020. When the

Airports, Airlines, and Related Businesses

Longer waits at security lines and delayed and canceled flights may result in lost work time and
productivity for travelers, although CRS is not aware of any quantitative assessment of this.
Immediate impacts on airlines, airports, and related businesses were primarily the result of
reduced government, business, and leisure travelers. In addition to lost revenue from airline
passengers, including federal workers and government contractors whose air travel is usually
canceled during a government shutdown, airport vendors may have forgone a portion of their
revenue that derives from federal workers at airports, such as TSA security screeners, who may
make fewer food purchases and other consumer transactions because of their lack of income
during a shutdown.82

Although the immediate effects of a government shutdown to aviation operations and air travel
tend to be limited, negative impact on air travel can become more pronounced during a prolonged
shutdown when TSA screeners and/or FAA air traffic controllers begin to miss work shifts. For
example, during the 2018-2019 shutdown, the increase in unscheduled absences contributed to
longer wait times at airport security checkpoints, discussed above, and temporary closures of
parts of terminals or concourses at certain airports due to lack of TSA staff.

Airlines and other aircraft operators, including charter operators and other companies requiring
FAA operator certification, may face delays in adding new planned routes and new aircraft during
a shutdown, as these actions typically require FAA approvals that may be delayed or disrupted
because of staff furloughs. Additionally, aircraft and aircraft component manufacturers may face
delays obtaining FAA approvals for design modifications, changes to production processes, and
other actions requiring FAA certification actions. This could result in delays in the delivery of
aircraft, aircraft engines, and certain aircraft parts, which could impact airline service and
schedules long after a shutdown ends. Similarly, approvals for new aircraft maintenance facilities
or the expansion of existing aircraft maintenance facilities may face certification delays during a
shutdown due to the reduced FAA staffing previously discussed. This could affect passenger and
cargo airlines and other aircraft operators who may face longer down times before aircraft can be
returned to service.83 While these types of disruptions have been mentioned by the aviation
industry, CRS has not identified specific data or quantifiable examples of how a shutdown may
affect FAA certification activities relating to airlines, other aircraft operators, maintenance
facilities, and aviation manufacturing.

Potential Legislative Actions

Members of Congress may or may not opt to pass legislation to mitigate potential impacts that
might be caused by a future government shutdown. Appropriators might decide that “the existing
array of statutes and chamber rules strikes the appropriate balance between continuing
government services and asserting Congress’s power of the purse.”91 Congress could choose to
establish in permanent law that stop-gap appropriations become available upon a lapse in regular
appropriations. Congress could opt to fund certain functions while it continues deliberations on
broader government funding during a lapse.

Following the 2018-2019 shutdown, some Members of Congress introduced bills aiming to
prevent future shutdowns and mitigate their effects on federal workers and the economy. For
example, the Government Shutdown Prevention Act of 2019 (S. 147) and the Ban Government
Shutdowns Act (H.R. 809), both introduced in the 116th Congress, sought to avoid funding gaps
by providing temporary continuing appropriations if regular appropriations bills were not passed
on time.

Some Members of Congress introduced bills to ensure the continuation of aviation operations
during the lapse in appropriations from October 1, 2025, through FY2026, until funding by
normal appropriations was restored. Some bills focused on continuing appropriations to pay
essential government workers or essential FAA and TSA personnel during a shutdown, while
others offered to establish certain types of automatic CRs that would provide a fallback funding
source for ATC operations and other core aviation safety operations in the event of potential
funding gaps. The bills discussed below show some legislative routes Congress could consider.

The Shutdown Fairness Act (S. 3012) would have provided appropriations for federal agencies to

term excepted employee includes certain contractors who support federal employees during a
