## Summary

The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026 (BAAJA; S. 5653) was introduced on September 30, 2026. The bill as introduced contains seven titles within two divisions, which aim to reform certain federal environmental review and permitting processes to expedite a wide range of project types, with an emphasis on energy-related projects.

Some Members have shown sustained interest over several Congresses in amending the nation's permitting laws to increase energy production and expedite a variety of projects (e.g., transportation infrastructure, energy, data center development, ecological restoration, stormwater infrastructure). Some Members have introduced numerous permitting-reform-related bills, some of which have focused on specific environmental statutes with review and/or permitting provisions (e.g., the National Environmental Policy Act, Clean Water Act, Endangered Species Act, National Historic Preservation Act). Others have focused on specific project types (e.g., electricity transmission lines, natural gas pipelines, geothermal energy development), and some have included broader reforms to permitting and reviews across project types.

Areas of debate on previous legislative proposals have included whether reforms should target conventional or renewable forms of energy, whether amending bedrock environmental laws to shorten reviews may weaken environmental protections, and whether certain proposals may narrow the role of states in the review process and/or reduce opportunities for public input and judicial review.

BAAJA is the product of negotiations involving Members of the Senate Committees on Environment and Public Works and Energy and Natural Resources. In a majority press release announcing the introduction of the bill, the Senate Committee on Energy and Natural Resources asserted that the legislation "will strengthen American energy security by accelerating the permitting process for critical energy and mineral projects across the United States." A majority press release from the Senate Committee on Environment and Public Works stated that the agreement "will deliver cheaper energy, more jobs, and responsible growth across the United States."

This report analyzes the provisions in BAAJA specific to the Clean Water Act (CWA, Division A, Title II). Section 1201 would formally require the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) to issue water quality criteria through notice and comment rulemaking. It would specify the judicial review provision applicable to issuance of water quality criteria and regulations defining the scope of "waters of the United States." Section 1202 would amend CWA Section 401 to narrow the scope of what certifying authorities (typically states) can consider in their water quality certification reviews and the scope of conditions they could include when granting certifications generally. It would further narrow the scope of certification reviews and conditions for certain projects, including interstate transmission line and natural gas pipeline projects. It would also narrow the bases on which certification may be denied and would amend several aspects of Section 401 certification timelines, processes, and judicial review. Section 1203 would extend the permit term limit for National Pollutant Discharge Elimination System (NPDES) permits from 5 years to 10 years. Section 1204 would limit the period of time during which EPA could issue a Section 404(c) "veto" of a Section 404 permit for the discharge of dredged or fill material into waters of the United States. It would extend the permit term limits for Section 404 general permits from 5 years to 10 years. It would specify that the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (USACE) consideration of environmental effects for general permits be limited to *water quality requirements*, which is newly defined in the bill, and that any adverse effects of discharges of dredge or fill material into less than 2 acres of waters of the United States are considered minimal. It would require USACE to maintain certain nationwide permits and would raise the threshold of allowable impacts for coverage under these permits. Section 1205 would impose a 150-day statute of limitations for lawsuits challenging Section 404 permits and certain other actions taken pursuant to Section 404. It would limit the availability of judicial review to parties that commented on an issue related to the lawsuit during the public comment period. It would limit the ability of courts to vacate permits or agency actions found to be unlawful and would require courts to set and enforce specific deadlines for agencies to act on remand.

## Overview

The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026 (BAAJA; S. 5653) was introduced on September 30, 2026. The bill as introduced contains seven titles within two divisions, which aim to reform certain federal environmental review and permitting processes to expedite a wide range of project types, with an emphasis on energy-related projects.

BAAJA is organized according to two divisions:

* 1. Division A—Committee on Environment and Public Works—has four titles: the National Environmental Policy Act of 1969 (NEPA), the Clean Water Act (CWA), the Endangered Species Act of 1973 (ESA), and Project Certainty.
* 2. Division B—Committee on Energy and Natural Resources—has three titles: Electric Transmission, Energy Permitting, and Historic Preservation.

This report examines the sections in BAAJA specific to the CWA (Division A, Title II):

* Section 1201. Water Quality Criteria
* Section 1202. Clean Water Act permitting and State certification
* Section 1203. Permit term for NPDES permits[1](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn1)
* Section 1205. Judicial review timeline clarity

The report provides background on the permitting and review provisions BAAJA amends, compares each CWA-related provision in BAAJA to existing law and regulations, and provides an analysis of selected changes in the bill.

## Clean Water Act Background

To help achieve the CWA's objective and goals, the statute prohibits the discharge of pollutants from any point source (e.g., a discrete conveyance such as a pipe) to waters of the United States (WOTUS) without a permit. The CWA establishes two permitting programs that may be used to authorize certain discharges to WOTUS. These are the Section 402 National Pollutant Discharge Elimination System (NPDES) permitting program and the Section 404 permitting program for dredged or fill material.[2](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn2) Separate from, but related to permitting, CWA Section 401 requires a type of approval, called a *water quality certification*, for any federal permit or license that may result in a discharge to WOTUS.[3](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn3) Division A, Title II, of BAAJA would make changes to these three programs as well as to the provisions governing judicial review of various actions taken under the CWA.

NPDES permits are issued by 47 states and the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) for discharges of pollutants from point sources, such as municipal and industrial sources.[4](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn4) These sources include wastewater treatment facilities, municipal stormwater collection systems, and a wide variety of industrial dischargers. The CWA limits permit terms for NPDES permits, which can be issued as individual or general permits, to five-year terms.[5](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn5)

Section 404 permits are issued primarily by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (USACE) and two states (Michigan and New Jersey) for discharges of dredged or fill material.[6](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn6) Activities that may require a Section 404 permit include pipeline projects, water resource projects, mining projects, transmission line projects, or other development projects. Similar to NPDES permits, there are two main types of Section 404 permits: individual permits and general permits.[7](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn7) USACE or states issue individual permits for individual projects or applicants, typically when the project's discharge may cause more than minimal individual or cumulative impacts.[8](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn8) USACE (and authorized states) may issue general permits for proposed activities that are similar in nature and will result in only minimal individual and cumulative adverse environmental effects.[9](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn9) These permits, authorized under CWA Section 404(e), are issued on a nationwide, regional, or state basis for particular categories of activities.[10](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn10) The CWA limits general permit terms to no more than five years.[11](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn11)

*Nationwide permits* (NWPs) are general permits that authorize categories of similar activities on a nationwide basis.[12](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn12) USACE issues NWPs (along with general conditions and definitions for the NWPs) through rulemaking, typically every five years. In January 2026, USACE reissued 56 existing NWPs (with some modifications) and issued one new NWP.[13](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn13) These NWPs went into effect on March 15, 2026. Examples of activities covered under NWPs, if they meet terms and conditions of the permit, are oil and natural gas pipeline activities, linear transportation projects, utility line activities, residential developments, commercial and institutional developments, agricultural activities, mining activities, and wetland and stream restoration activities.[14](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn14)

The CWA also establishes distinct responsibilities for EPA in implementing Section 404. In addition, EPA has authority under Section 404(c) to prohibit, deny, restrict, or withdraw the use of a specified area as a disposal site for dredged or fill material whenever the Administrator determines that the discharge will have an unacceptable adverse effect on municipal water supplies, shellfish beds and fishery areas, wildlife, or recreational areas.[15](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn15)

Separate from, but related to permitting, CWA Section 401 requires a type of approval, called a *water quality certification*, for any federal permit or license that may result in a discharge to WOTUS.[16](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn16) A water quality certification, issued by the state (or other certifying authority)[17](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn17) in which the discharge originates, attests that the discharge will comply with applicable provisions of certain enumerated sections of the CWA (301, 302, 303, 306, and 307) and may include consideration of other water quality requirements.[18](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn18) Examples of activities that may require a CWA Section 401 water quality certification include activities requiring a USACE-issued CWA Section 404 permit, projects requiring USACE Rivers and Harbors Act permits, hydropower projects licensed by the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC), and certificates of public convenience and necessity issued by FERC. A certifying authority may grant, grant with conditions, deny, or waive certification of proposed federal licenses or permits.[19](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn19)

## Summary of Selected Changes Proposed in BAAJA

As introduced, BAAJA's CWA-related provisions propose numerous changes to CWA permitting and water quality certification requirements. Other provisions in the bill, such as Section 1401, may also affect EPA's and USACE's ability to modify or revoke CWA permits. This section focuses on changes to the Section 401 water quality certification process, NPDES permit term limits, Section 404 permitting, and judicial review.

### Section 401 Water Quality Certification Process (BAAJA Section 1201)

Although CWA Section 401 water quality certifications are not permits, federally permitted or licensed projects that are subject to the certification requirement may not proceed unless certification is granted or waived. As a result, the role of water quality certifications in and their effect on issuance of federal permits and licenses have attracted attention in permitting reform debates. Some areas of policy interest have included the scope of states' certification reviews, the scope of conditions states can impose when granting a certification, and time frames for issuing certifications.[20](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn20)

Regarding the scope of certification reviews and conditions, one area of debate is whether a certifying authority's review and conditions can consider the impacts of the "activity" (i.e., the impacts from the permitted activity as a whole, including the point source discharge as well as nonpoint source impacts from the activity) or may only more narrowly consider the impacts from the point source discharge. Other areas of debate include which water quality requirements may be considered and which waters may be considered.

Regarding time frames for issuing certifications, Section 401 requires that certifying authorities act on a certification request within a *reasonable period of time* (which shall not exceed one year) after receipt of such request. If a certifying authority does not act on a certification request within that time frame, the statute provides that the certification requirements are waived, and the certification is not required for the federal licensing or permitting agency to issue the license or permit. Areas of debate have included what constitutes a reasonable period of time and who determines it.

Another area of debate is whether water quality certification should include different procedures and requirements based on the type of project at issue. BAAJA would specify rules that apply generally for Section 401 certifications as well as special rules that apply for certain projects—interstate transmission line projects and interstate natural gas pipeline projects.[21](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn21)

#### Scope of Reviews

BAAJA would specify that generally, the scope of a certification shall be limited to ensuring compliance with *water quality requirements*, which the bill defines to mean (A) the applicable provisions of Sections 301, 302, 303, 306, and 307; and (B) any federal and state or tribal laws (including regulations) implementing Sections 301, 302, 303, 306, and 307 that are applicable to discharges to WOTUS.[22](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn22) The first part of this definition does not specify whether the review can consider the impacts of the activity or is limited to the impacts of the discharge. The second half of the definition would limit the scope of what the certifying authority can consider in comparison to current regulations. Under current regulations, certifying authorities can consider any federal and state or tribal laws or regulations implementing the enumerated sections of the CWA and "any other water quality-related requirement of state or tribal law."[23](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn23) BAAJA would limit these considerations to the laws and regulations implementing the enumerated CWA sections that are applicable to *discharges into WOTUS*. Under BAAJA, for example, certifying authorities would not be able to consider water quality impacts to downstream non-WOTUS waters, such as waters that may be subject to state regulation. They also could only consider "any other water quality-related requirement of state or tribal law" where those requirements are implementing the enumerated sections of the CWA and applicable to discharges to WOTUS.

BAAJA's special rules for interstate transmission line and natural gas pipeline projects would further narrow the scope of certification reviews for those projects. BAAJA would specify that the scope of reviews for these projects is limited to reviewing whether the *direct point source discharge* complies with water quality requirements.[24](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn24) This limitation in reviewing the direct point source discharge would apply to both aspects of the definition of *water quality requirements*. The extent to which the term *direct point source discharge* further narrows considerations in comparison to *discharges into* *WOTUS*is unclear. The CWA specifies that the term *discharge*, "when used without qualification includes a discharge of a pollutant, and a discharge of pollutants."[25](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn25) The CWA defines the term *discharge of a pollutant* to mean any addition of any pollutant to navigable waters (i.e., WOTUS) from any point source.[26](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn26)

#### Scope of Conditions

BAAJA would also narrow the scope of what conditions a certifying authority can include when granting a certification with conditions. Under current regulations, certifying authorities shall include any conditions in a grant of certification necessary to assure that the *activity* will comply with applicable *water quality requirements*, which is defined more broadly than it is defined in BAAJA.[27](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn27) BAAJA would limit the scope of conditions to those the certifying authority determines with clear and convincing evidence are (1) necessary to ensure compliance with *water quality requirements* as that term is more narrowly defined in the bill, (2) the least burdensome conditions to ensure compliance with *water quality requirements*, and (3) consistent with the requirements of the federal license or permit for which the application was made.[28](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn28) Combined with the bill's definition of *water quality requirements*, this would narrow the scope of conditions and no longer allow certifying authorities to add conditions to address water-quality-related impacts to downstream non-WOTUS waters, conditions to ensure the *activity* complies with state or tribal requirements, or conditions to address "any other water quality-related requirement of state or tribal law" unless those requirements are implementing the enumerated sections of the CWA and applicable to discharges to WOTUS. In addition, the certifying authorities would have to newly meet the higher threshold of demonstrating with clear and convincing evidence that the conditions are necessary and the least burdensome to comply with water quality requirements.

BAAJA's special rules for interstate transmission line and natural gas pipeline projects would further narrow the scope of conditions certifying authorities may include. BAAJA specifies that the scope of conditions certifying authorities may add would be limited to assuring that any *direct point source discharge* to WOTUS will comply with CWA Sections 301, 302, 303, 306, and 307.[29](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn29) This would preclude certifying authorities from adding conditions to address any federal, state, or tribal laws implementing the enumerated CWA sections in addition to the narrowing discussed above. In addition, the same uncertainty discussed above regarding the use of the term *direct point source discharge* applies to the scope of conditions as well.

#### Certification Time Frames

BAAJA would change the certifying authority's role in determining the *reasonable period of time*. Section 401(a)(1) of the CWA provides that if a certifying authority fails or refuses to act on a request for certification within a reasonable period of time (not to exceed one year), the certification requirements are deemed waived with respect to the underlying permit or license application.[30](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn30) Under current regulations, the federal permitting or licensing agency and the certifying authority may jointly agree to the reasonable period of time for the certifying authority to act on a request (provided it does not exceed one year from the date the certification was received) and establishes a default of six months if they cannot agree.[31](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn31) BAAJA specifies that the federal licensing or permitting agency, in *consultation with* the certifying authority, would determine the reasonable period of time (not to exceed one year from the date of receipt of the certification application).[32](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn32) BAAJA's special rules for interstate transmission line and natural gas pipeline projects would also shorten the statutory maximum for the reasonable period of time from one year to nine months.[33](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn33)

#### Other Potential Changes

In addition to changes addressing scope and timing considerations for Section 401 certifications, BAAJA also proposes changes related to certification denials, waivers, and the effect of certification actions. BAAJA would specify that the only actions to be taken in response to a certification application are to grant the application, grant the application with conditions, deny the application, or waive certification requirements.[34](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn34)

With respect to waiver, BAAJA would provide that a certifying authority may "affirmatively waive" certification requirements at any time during the reasonable period of time. With respect to certification denials, BAAJA would narrow the bases on which certifications could be denied. Similar to the new requirements for conditions, BAAJA would prohibit a certifying authority from denying a certification unless it determines by clear and convincing evidence that no modification to or reasonable condition on the certification would make it possible to grant the certification without violating water quality requirements.

BAAJA also includes a new provision that would newly deem water quality requirements satisfied for the purpose of a federal license or permit when a certifying authority grants a certification application, waives it, or grants it with conditions. The intent of this provision is unclear, including in how it may relate to a provision in the bill under Section 1204 that aims to limit USACE's consideration of environmental effects of an activity, for purposes of issuing general permits, to water quality requirements.

### NPDES Permit Term Limits

BAAJA proposes to extend the permit term limits for NPDES permits from 5 years to 10 years.[35](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn35) For a discussion of the policy considerations specific to permit terms, see "NPDES Permit Terms" in CRS Report R49486, *Clean Water Act Permitting: Overview and Policy Issues*, by Laura Gatz.

### Section 404 Permitting

Section 1204 of BAAJA proposes numerous changes to Section 404 permitting processes and requirements. Section 1204(a) would narrow the timing within which the EPA Administrator could exercise 404(c) authority. The CWA currently authorizes the EPA Administrator to exercise 404(c) authority (often referred to as "veto authority") *whenever* he determines, after notice and opportunity for public hearings, that the discharge of such materials into such area will have an unacceptable adverse effect on municipal water supplies, shellfish beds and fishery areas (including spawning and breeding areas), wildlife, or recreational areas.[36](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn36) BAAJA would limit the time period in which the EPA Administrator may issue a 404(c) determination to the time period that begins on the date on which an applicant submits a complete application and ends on the date on which the Secretary (USACE) issues the permit.[37](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn37)

BAAJA Section 1204(b) would also make numerous changes to general permits issued under Section 404, including changes to nationwide permits. Similar to NPDES permits, BAAJA would extend the permit term limit for general permits issued under Section 404 from 5 years to 10 years.[38](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn38) BAAJA also specifies what USACE can consider in determining the environmental effects of an activity for purposes of issuing general permits, requires that USACE maintain certain NWPs, and specifies reissuance procedures specific to those NWPs. Collectively, these changes would result in more projects qualifying for coverage under general permits.

#### Determination of Environmental Effects

Under existing law, USACE may issue general permits for proposed activities that are similar in nature and will result in only minimal individual and cumulative adverse environmental effects.[39](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn39) BAAJA would specify that in determining the environmental effects of an activity, for purposes of determining whether a general permit can be issued, USACE shall consider only the effects on compliance with *water quality requirements*, as defined in the bill (under new Section 401(h)) of any discharge of dredged or fill material resulting from the activity. The implications of the limitation to *water quality requirements* is unclear. While the provision appears to aim to narrow what is considered for Section 404 general permits, it does not strike the current requirement in CWA Section 404(e) to evaluate the activities in accordance with the CWA's Section 404(b)(1) guidelines, which include broader considerations.[40](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn40)

BAAJA would also specify that USACE is to consider any effects of a discharge of dredged or fill material into less than 2 acres of WOTUS to be a minimal adverse environmental effect.[41](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn41) Under current law and regulations, USACE determines what acreages are considered to be minimal adverse environmental effects.[42](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn42) While there is no current acreage limit that applies to all general permits, acreage limits established under existing NWPs, as discussed in more detail below, range from ⅒ of an acre to 10 acres, with ½ acre as the most common threshold.[43](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn43)

BAAJA's provision deeming the effects of a discharge of dredged or fill material into less than 2 acres of WOTUS to be a minimal adverse environmental effect may have additional implications for USACE's ability to modify or revoke a general permit, including NWPs. The CWA specifies that general permits may be revoked or modified by the Secretary of the Army, acting through the Chief of Engineers (USACE) if, after opportunity for public hearing, the Secretary determines that the activities authorized by such general permit have an adverse impact on the environment or such activities are more appropriately authorized by individual permits.[44](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn44) BAAJA would seemingly prevent the Secretary from revoking or modifying general permits for discharges of dredged or fill material into less than 2 acres of WOTUS based on a finding that they have an adverse impact on the environment. In addition, under current regulations, USACE division engineers can modify, suspend, or revoke NWPs in a particular region, or for a specific category of activities or waters, and USACE district engineers can modify, suspend, or revoke activity-specific NWP authorizations in their districts.[45](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn45) BAAJA would limit a USACE division or district engineer's ability to modify or revoke an NWP issued by USACE headquarters if it is to address adverse impacts from discharges of dredged or fill material into less than 2 acres of WOTUS. BAAJA's provisions addressing project certainty may also limit more generally the ability to modify or revoke permits, including CWA permits.[46](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn46) Specifically, Section 1401 of BAAJA would, with limited exceptions, prohibit a federal agency from revoking, rescinding, withdrawing, terminating, suspending, amending, or altering a federal authorization or permit in effect on or after September 16, 2026, necessary for project construction or "operation at full capacity."[47](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn47)

Additionally, BAAJA would provide that, for reissuance of NWPs that meet certain conditions pertaining to preconstruction requirements, consultation under the Endangered Species Act and the National Historic Preservation Act would not be required, and that a programmatic environmental assessment with respect to the general permit would suffice for compliance with the National Environmental Policy Act.[48](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn48)

#### Requirement to Maintain Certain NWPs

BAAJA would specify that notwithstanding any other provision of the section, USACE shall maintain 5 of its 57 existing NWPs as they are described in the final rule establishing the 2026 NWPs.[49](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn49) However, it would increase the acreage limit for allowable impacts to WOTUS for these NWPs to 2 acres for each *single and complete project*. These five NWPs are NWP 12 (Oil or Natural Gas Pipeline Activities), NWP 14 (Linear Transportation Projects), NWP 43 (Stormwater Management Facilities), NWP 57 (Electric Utility Line and Telecommunications Activities), and NWP 58 (Utility Line Activities for Water and Other Substances). Currently, activities authorized under NWPs 12, 43, 57, and 58 may not cause the loss of greater than ½ acre of WOTUS for each single and complete project. Activities authorized under NWP 14 may not cause greater than ½ acre of nontidal WOTUS or ⅓ acre of tidal WOTUS for each single and complete project.

The proposed changes in BAAJA are likely to increase the number of activities that can obtain coverage under NWPs, as well as under other types of USACE general permits. Given that issuance time frames for all general permits, including NWPs, are shorter than time frames for individual permits, this also could result in shorter permitting time frames for more activities.[50](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn50)

### Judicial Review

BAAJA's amendments to the CWA would alter various aspects of judicial review of actions taken pursuant to the statute. The CWA's judicial review provision, Section 509(b), applies to seven enumerated categories of actions and specifies a 120-day statute of limitations and direct review in the federal appellate courts.[51](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn51) For actions not covered by that provision, the Administrative Procedure Act's (APA's) judicial review framework governs, providing a six-year statute of limitations and review in the federal district courts.[52](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn52) Actions taken by state agencies pursuant to state law (e.g., state permitting requirements or water quality certification procedures) are typically subject to state-specific procedures for judicial review.

Section 1201(b) of BAAJA would amend the CWA's judicial review provision to extend to development of water quality criteria and regulations defining *WOTUS*, thus streamlining the judicial review process for those actions and subjecting them to a shorter statute of limitations.[53](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn53)

Section 1202 of BAAJA would affect various aspects of judicial review of actions related to water quality certification. The CWA's judicial review provision does not cover actions taken under Section 401.[54](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn54) Under current law, the forum and applicable standards for judicial review of certification decisions are not uniform across all such decisions. A state's decision on a certification application or adherence to state-law certification requirements is generally reviewable in state court, while questions regarding the state's adherence to requirements imposed directly by Section 401 are reviewable in federal court.[55](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn55) When a state acts as a certifying authority, state law may govern the burden of proof or standard of review in judicial challenges to actions on certification applications. The judicial review provisions in Section 1202 of BAAJA would impose more uniform procedures and standards of review for some aspects of Section 401 litigation.

Specifically, BAAJA would provide a mechanism for certification applicants to seek review of a certifying authority's alleged failure to follow the bill's newly specified procedural requirements. It would also specify the burden of proof in judicial review of certification denials or certification conditions.[56](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn56) BAAJA would provide that in challenges to certification denials, the certifying authority would bear the burden of demonstrating by clear and convincing evidence that no modification to or reasonable condition on the certification would make it possible to grant the certification without violating water quality requirements.[57](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn57) In challenges to certification conditions, the certifying authority would bear the burden of demonstrating by clear and convincing evidence that the challenged conditions are necessary to ensure compliance with water quality requirements, the least burdensome to ensure compliance with water quality requirements, and consistent with the requirements of the underlying federal license or permit.[58](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn58) In addition, BAAJA would provide that waiver of certification requirements is not subject to judicial review.[59](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn59)

Actions taken under Section 404 of the CWA are also not covered by the CWA's judicial review provision and instead are governed by the APA.[60](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn60) Section 1205 of BAAJA would add a new subsection 404(t) to govern judicial review of the approval of state 404 permit programs, issuance of individual or general permits, and verification that an activity involving a discharge of dredged or fill material is authorized by a general permit. BAAJA would impose a shorter, 150-day statute of limitations for those actions, limit the availability of judicial review to parties that meet certain requirements for participating in the public comment process, limit the ability of courts to vacate an action found to be unlawful, and require courts to set and enforce a deadline for agency action on remand (not to exceed 180 days). Section 1205 contains some elements that are similar but not identical to BAAJA's judicial review provisions for NEPA,[61](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn61) the Endangered Species Act,[62](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn62) the National Historic Preservation Act,[63](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-26_R49492_248c38b16e2edd8e18cc24846a7db3032eb231f8.html#fn63) and in Section 2201 (which would establish a framework for accelerating judicial review of certain claims relating to project authorizations). It is not clear what requirements would apply in lawsuits alleging claims under more than one statute.
