Farm Bill Primer: Forestry Title

Updated November 18, 2026
(IF12054)

Forest management generally, as well as forest research and forestry assistance, is within the jurisdiction of the agriculture committees in Congress. Although most forestry programs are permanently authorized, forestry often is addressed in the periodic farm bills to reauthorize many agriculture programs. Five of the past six enacted farm bills included a separate forestry title, including the most recently enacted farm bill, Title VIII of the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018 ([P.L. 115-334](https://www.congress.gov/law/115/pub/334); the 2018 farm bill). Authorities in the 2018 farm bill have been extended three times, for a year at a time, and generally were extended to cover FY2026 through [P.L. 119-37](https://www.congress.gov/law/119/pub/37), Division E, §5002. This In Focus summarizes some of the forestry provisions addressed in the 2018 farm bill and issues Congress may debate in future farm bills.

Forestry in the United States

According to [Forest Service (FS) data](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/80035.pdf#page=58), one-third of the land area in the United States is forestland ([763.3 million acres](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/80035.pdf#page=17); [**Figure 1**](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IF/HTML/IF12054.html#_Ref485891911)). These lands provide ecological services, including air and water resources; fish and wildlife habitat; opportunities for recreation and cultural use; and timber resources for lumber, plywood, paper, and other materials, among other uses and benefits.

Most forestland in the United States is [privately owned](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/80035.pdf#page=21) (439.6 million acres, or 58%). Private, noncorporate entities that do not own wood-processing facilities—such as individuals, families, and nonprofit organizations—own 274.4 million acres; private corporate landowners (e.g., timber investment trusts) own the remaining 165.2 million acres. The federal government owns 237.5 million acres of forestland, and states and other public entities own 86.2 million acres of forestland.

The federal government engages in four types of forestry activities: managing federal forests; providing financial, technical, or other resources to promote forest ownership and stewardship and the forest products industry generally (referred to as [*forestry assistance*](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R45219)); sponsoring or conducting research to advance the science of forestry; and engaging in international forestry assistance and research. In addition to the FS, which is within the U.S. Department of Agriculture, the Department of the Interior's Bureau of Land Management, National Park Service, and Fish and Wildlife Service, among other federal agencies, also engage in some of these forestry activities.

The FS is the principal federal forest management agency. In addition to administering most forestry assistance programs, conducting forestry research, and leading U.S. international forestry assistance and research efforts, the FS also is responsible for managing 18% of all U.S. forestlands (140.6 million acres) as part of the [National Forest System](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R43872) (NFS). Many of FS's land management, assistance, and research programs have permanent authorities and receive appropriations annually through the discretionary appropriations process.

Forestry in the 2018 Farm Bill

Title VIII of the 2018 farm bill, entitled "Forestry," repealed, modified, reauthorized, and created several forestry research, assistance, and federal land management programs.

* **Research.** The 2018 farm bill modified one and repealed several forestry research programs. The repeals included a grant program to support minority and female students studying forestry and a project demonstrating wood bioenergy.
* **Assistance.** The 2018 farm bill repealed, modified, and reauthorized some forestry assistance programs. This included providing explicit statutory authorization and congressional direction for programs that had been operating under existing but broad authorization, such as the [Landscape Scale Restoration](https://www.fs.usda.gov/managing-land/private-land/landscape-scale-restoration) Program. The law also established, reauthorized, and modified assistance programs to promote [wood innovation](https://www.fs.usda.gov/science-technology/energy-forest-products/wood-innovation) for energy use, building construction, and other purposes to facilitate the removal of forest biomass on both federal and nonfederal lands and to mitigate wildfire risk.
* **Federal** **Forest** **Management****.** The 2018 farm bill included provisions related to federal and tribal forest management, such as provisions modifying planning requirements; establishing two watershed protection programs; expanding the availability of agreements to perform cross-boundary projects; reauthorizing and extending the [Collaborative Forest Landscape Restoration Program](https://www.fs.usda.gov/managing-land/restoration/collaborative-forest-landscape-restoration); and adding or modifying FS's authorities to lease, sell, or exchange NFS lands.

Forestry-related provisions also were included in other 2018 farm bill titles. For example, the Conservation (Title II), Research, Extension, and Related Matters (Title VII), Energy (Title IX), and Miscellaneous (Title XII) titles each contained provisions related to forestry or forest ownership.

Considerations for a Future Farm Bill

Congress may use a future farm bill to modify, extend, or abolish existing programs or funding authorizations, or to establish new options for forestry research, assistance to nonfederal forest owners, and management of federal forestlands. Alternatively, Congress may elect not to address forestry issues in a new farm bill if, for example, Congress determines existing authorities and programs adequately address the nation's forestry needs, or decides to address forestry issues in another vehicle, among other reasons.

Congress also could use a new farm bill to address any concerns related to forest health management on both federal and nonfederal lands. For example, this could include authorizing or amending programs to reduce the risk of catastrophic disturbance events, such as uncharacteristically severe wildfire or insect or disease infestations. For nonfederal forests, considerations might include whether to establish or modify assistance programs to enhance wildfire protection, preparedness, and forest resiliency. For federal forests, considerations might include whether to establish new authorities or alter existing authorities to address the accumulation of vegetation that contributes to increased wildfire risk—often referred to as [*hazardous fuel**s reduction*](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R48779)—or addressing other forest restoration concerns.

Because many forest risks span multiple ownership boundaries, Congress may use a future farm bill to consider if and how to expand or facilitate cross-boundary forest management activities. This could be done by authorizing and/or incentivizing various federal and nonfederal partnerships and collaborations. In contrast, Congress may want to restrict cross-boundary management activities, for example, to target more specific concerns or areas or facilitate simpler oversight of federal land management.

A new farm bill could extend, expand, restrict, otherwise alter, or terminate programs that facilitate the development or advancement of wood products. In previous farm bills, and in other legislation, Congress established programs to promote new markets and uses for woody biomass, in part to encourage forest restoration and reduce wildfire threats.

A new farm bill could consider the relationship between forests and climate change in a variety of ways. Forests have the potential to mitigate climate risk but also may be impacted by changing climatic conditions. Forests sequester large amounts of [carbon](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R46312/R46312.7.pdf) and have the potential to mitigate future greenhouse gas emissions. The effects of changing climatic conditions on forests are uncertain but include potential impacts to the range and distribution of tree species, changes in [wildland fire behavior](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IN12288), and uncertainties related to future carbon sequestration potential, among others. To address some of the uncertainties regarding climate impacts to forest management, Congress may consider using a new farm bill to modify existing [inventory](https://research.fs.usda.gov/programs/fia) and research programs or establish new ones, domestically and internationally. Additionally, Congress could use a new farm bill to evaluate whether to establish programs to increase or optimize carbon sequestration on both federal and nonfederal lands, through market or nonmarket mechanisms.
