Summary

The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026 (BAAJA; S. 5653) was introduced on September 30, 2026. The bill as introduced contains seven titles within two divisions, the first of which—Division A—aims to reform certain federal environmental review and permitting processes (e.g., those contained within the National Environmental Policy Act [NEPA], Clean Water Act, Endangered Species Act) and provide new provisions associated with project certainty and judicial review. Division B contains reforms to the National Historic Preservation Act and has provisions focused on specific project types (e.g., electricity transmission lines, natural gas pipelines, geothermal energy development), including some broader reforms to permitting processes and reviews across project types. This report provides background on the permitting and environmental review processes. It also analyzes BAAJA provisions specific to Division A, Title I, which primarily include proposed amendments to NEPA (Subtitles A [§§1101-1110] and B [§1121]) and provisions broadly related to federal permitting improvements (Subtitle C [§1131-1133]) as compared to existing NEPA requirements.

Section 1101 of BAAJA would specify that NEPA prescribes a procedure to inform the public about environmental effects of federal actions, describes the result of the NEPA process as an informational document, and does not mandate a particular outcome. Section 1102 would reorganize NEPA's definitions, remove two existing definitions, modify several existing definitions, add 15 new definitions, and make conforming amendments. For example, it would expand the list of exclusions in the definition of "major federal action," remove the definition of "participating Federal agencies," and add definitions for terms such as "authorization" and "make public." Section 1103 would require federal agencies to update their agency-specific NEPA procedures and direct the Council on Environmental Quality (CEQ) to promote standardized implementation of NEPA. Section 1104 would require federal agencies to implement NEPA consistent with minimum functional requirements and data and technology standards (as specified in BAAJA Section 1121). Section 1105 would amend the process for determining whether and what level of environmental review applies to a proposed action.

Section 1106 would, among other changes, expand requirements governing the roles and responsibilities of lead, joint lead, and cooperating agencies for federal decisions "relating to an authorization"; add interagency scheduling, coordination, and deadline requirements; eliminate consideration of certain alternatives; specify aspects of CEQ's role in resolving disputes associated with participating agencies and deadlines; place limitations on when an agency is required to consider new scientific or technical research; provide administrative consequences if an agency misses a deadline for an environmental review or related authorization; expand judicial review for missed deadlines; and specify annual reporting requirements for agencies and CEQ. Section 1107 would extend the timeline for reliance on programmatic environmental documents. Section 1108 would require CEQ to maintain a central public record of all categorical exclusions (CEs) and specify the criteria under which agencies could use a listed CE maintained by another agency. Section 1109 would establish application completeness and level of review determination deadlines and require project sponsors to submit specified permitting, stakeholder engagement, and other information in a permitting-related application. Section 1110 would affect the timing, venue, available remedies, and other considerations associated with judicial review of NEPA claims and potentially related claims, including a 150-day statute of limitations to file a claim and a general prohibition on any judicial remedy imposing vacatur or injunctive relief if based on a NEPA violation, among other requirements.

Subtitle B (Section 1121) would direct CEQ to lead development of electronic tools for environmental reviews and authorizations and create an interconnected federal permitting data system with a common and publicly accessible authorization portal. Section 1121 would also increase authorized annual CEQ appropriations from $1 million to $5 million. Subtitle C (Sections 1131-1133) would require CEQ to maintain an online database to provide information on and track the status of environmental reviews and related authorizations for specified environmental documents.

Overview

The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026 (BAAJA; S. 5653) was introduced on September 30, 2026. The bill as introduced contains seven titles within two divisions, which aim to reform certain federal environmental review and permitting processes to expedite a wide range of project types, with an emphasis on energy-related projects.

Background on Environmental Reviews and Federal Permitting

Certain activities that support the nation's economy and infrastructure, including energy production, transportation, and natural resource development, require one or more federal authorizations.1 Many federal permitting processes (e.g., issuing permits, licensing, or granting rights-of-way across federal lands) require compliance with the National Environmental Policy Act of 1969, as amended (NEPA; 42 U.S.C. §§4321 et seq.). NEPA establishes a national policy with respect to environmental quality and a process for integrating environmental considerations into federal decisionmaking (i.e., through an environmental review). Although NEPA often informs federal action—and thus decisions relating to certain authorizations—it does not itself authorize or prohibit projects; rather, it operates alongside the substantive statutes that govern individual federal authorizations.

Congress made substantial amendments to NEPA in the Fiscal Responsibility Act of 2023 (FRA; P.L. 118-5).2 Among other changes, the FRA amended NEPA to address circumstances in which the statute applies, the procedure for determining the level of review, page and time limits, and the use of programmatic environmental documents.3 Congress amended NEPA again in the FY2025 budget reconciliation act (P.L. 119-21) to allow project sponsors to pay a fee for expedited environmental reviews.4 Congress has also enacted and considered a variety of other measures that would affect various aspects of NEPA with respect to federal permitting processes. One such example is Title 41 of the Fixing America's Surface Transportation Act ("FAST-41"; P.L. 114-94).5 Among other things, FAST-41 established the Federal Permitting Improvement Steering Council ("Permitting Council"), authorized a streamlined administrative process for certain covered infrastructure projects, and directed the Permitting Council to identify the types of environmental reviews and authorizations most commonly required for certain types of infrastructure projects. It also directed the Permitting Council to maintain an online database known as the "Permitting Dashboard" to track the status of federal environmental reviews and authorizations for covered projects.6 FAST-41 is an example of a separate statutory federal process that coordinates schedules for environmental reviews, authorizations, and permitting decisions for certain infrastructure projects that meet specific criteria for eligibility.7

Division A Title I of BAAJA would affect environmental reviews and certain procedural aspects of federal permitting. NEPA's environmental reviews serve as inputs to federal decisionmaking on permitting and other federal activities. Statutory requirements for permitting processes other than environmental reviews—such as deadlines and coordination between agencies—are not found in NEPA but rather in other laws. BAAJA would amend NEPA to connect the environmental review process to additional aspects of federal permitting and authorizations—for example, by setting a unified schedule for the timing of certain decisions.

While NEPA's environmental review requirements apply broadly across the federal government, the specific federal permitting requirements and the agencies involved for any particular project depend on factors such as the nature of the proposed action, applicable jurisdictions, and anticipated environmental impacts. Though beyond the scope of this report, state and local permits (e.g., zoning approvals, land use permits, and state-specific environmental certifications) are often required in addition to the approvals or requirements imposed at the federal level.

Unless Congress exempts an action from environmental review, NEPA requires agencies, prior to making final decisions, to assess reasonably foreseeable environmental effects, identify "irreversible and irretrievable" resource commitments, and consider a reasonable range of alternatives for "major Federal actions significantly affecting the quality of the human environment."8 A federal agency's issuance of a permit or other authorization is often considered a major federal action under NEPA.9 During an environmental review, agencies typically consider and analyze social, ecological, and health impacts. NEPA does not require agencies to select the most environmentally protective outcome or any other specific alternative option. Rather, for major federal actions likely to have significant impacts, NEPA requires agencies to conduct thorough analyses before making final decisions.

How a federal agency demonstrates compliance with NEPA depends on the level of the proposed action's impacts. If the proposed action has "a reasonably foreseeable significant effect on the quality of the human environment," an agency prepares an environmental impact statement (EIS).10 If the proposed action "does not have a reasonably foreseeable significant effect on the quality of the human environment, or if the significance of such effect is unknown," an agency typically prepares an environmental assessment (EA).11 An agency is not required to prepare an EIS or EA for a proposed agency action if the action is covered by one of the agency's own categorical exclusions (CEs) or if another agency's CE is applied to the action consistent with 42 U.S.C. §4336c.12

NEPA establishes a procedural framework for evaluating the environmental impacts of a given project and for informing federal agency decisionmaking; it does not itself confer authority to approve or deny a project or replace the need for specific permits or other authorizations mandated by regulatory statutes. In contrast, federal permitting statutes grant agencies substantive decisionmaking authority and define the criteria under which an authorization may be issued. In practice, agencies often integrate NEPA analyses with permitting requirements.13 NEPA documents may inform permitting decisions; completion of NEPA does not guarantee issuance of a permit, and a permit may be denied after an environmental document is finalized.

Section 1105(1)(D) Threshold Determinations

Would add two conditions to NEPA Section 106 for when an agency is not required to prepare an environmental document (EA or EIS):

  • the proposed agency action relates to a project or action that has been (1) reviewed pursuant to a state environmental review statute or certain tribal environmental reviews and (2) the lead agency determines that the review serves a similar function as federal agency compliance with NEPA; or
  • the agency determines that compliance with the requirements of another federal law for the proposed agency action would serve a "similar function" as compliance with NEPA.

NEPA Section 106(a) establishes threshold determinations that identify the circumstances under which an agency is not required to prepare an environmental document for a proposed agency action but it does not provide a general authority to substitute compliance with the requirements of another federal law for NEPA. (42 U.S.C. 4336(a))

In some instances, federal agencies—based on earlier guidance that preceded the 2023 NEPA amendments—have determined that other federal statutes serve a similarly equivalent function as NEPA and have allowed compliance with another statute to satisfy NEPA review requirements. Court cases have affirmed that NEPA review is not necessary when a federal agency's action complies with another federal statute that is "functionally equivalent" to NEPA. See, for example, Env't Def. Fund, Inc. v. EPA, 489 F.2d 1247 (D.C. Cir. 1973) Pac. Legal Found. v. Andrus, 657 F.2d 829, 834 (6th Cir. 1981). To date, the application of the "functional equivalence" doctrine has been limited to federal statutes concerning federal agency compliance with NEPA.

NEPA Section 102(2)(G) permits a state agency to prepare an EIS for certain federally funded actions, given certain specified conditions; however, NEPA does not provide a general authority to substitute a state or tribal review for a federal environmental document. (42 U.S.C. §4332(2)(G))

At times, Congress has also authorized specific agencies to allow

  • states to assume responsibilities for NEPA compliance (see, e.g., 23 U.S.C. §327);
  • states to substitute state environmental laws for NEPA, subject to findings that the state requirements are "at least as stringent as" the corresponding federal requirements (see, e.g., 23 U.S.C. §330); or
  • Tribes to authorize certain activities on tribal lands without case-by-case approval by the Secretary of the Interior (Secretary, as delegated to the Bureau of Indian Affairs), provided the Tribe has an environmental review process meeting specified statutory requirements (see, e.g., 25 U.S.C. §§415(h) and 3504(e)).

Would add a new provision to NEPA Section 106 that would specify that once a lead agency has begun preparing an EA, the agency must discontinue the EA and prepare an EIS if requested by the project sponsor.

NEPA does not include a similar provision allowing the project sponsor to request that an agency prepare an EIS.

Under NEPA Section 106(b), an agency must prepare an EIS when a proposed action has a reasonably foreseeable significant effect on the human environment. Agencies otherwise prepare an EA for actions that do not have a reasonably foreseeable significant effect on the quality of the human environment, or if the significance of such effect is unknown and there are no applicable CEs or other exemptions under another provision of law. (42 U.S.C. §4336(b))

Section 1105(3) Effect of Threshold Determination on Other Agencies

Would add a new subsection (c) to NEPA Section 106 that would bind other agencies to the lead agency's threshold determination by specifying that if the lead agency determines that an environmental document is not required, then no other agency shall prepare an environmental document "with respect to the proposed agency action."

NEPA does not include a similar provision that would bind all agencies to the lead agency's threshold determination.

As currently implemented, agencies may reach different determinations regarding a proposed action's anticipated environmental impacts and their significance and which level of review is necessary to support their decision. Under NEPA Section 107(b), "to the extent practicable, if a proposed agency action will require action by more than one [f]ederal agency and the lead agency has determined that it requires preparation of an environmental document, the lead and cooperating agencies shall evaluate the proposal in a single environmental document." Section 1110. Judicial Review

Section 1110 Judicial Review

BAAJA Section 1110 would govern legal challenges to an agency's compliance with an environmental review under NEPA and would apply to claims filed on or after enactment seeking judicial review "under any provision of Federal law of an authorization based on a claim that an environmental review failed to be properly conducted" pursuant to NEPA. Barring certain exceptions, would

  • prohibit legal challenges to an agency's compliance with NEPA environmental reviews, unless filed within 150 days of when the authorization was published in the Federal Register, if following preparation an EIS, or otherwise made public (as defined in BAAJA Section 1102); by plaintiffs who submitted comments (if a public comment opportunity was provided) with sufficient detail to put the agency on notice of the issue for which the plaintiffs seek judicial review; and alleging an "actual injury in fact amounting to a direct and immediate harm";
  • provide that claims are filed directly in the federal courts of appeals and provide supplemental jurisdiction for "other claims related to the validity of the authorization at issue"; and
  • bar judicial review of claims regarding whether a final agency action complies with NEPA "for any omission from the list" described in the definition of record of stakeholder engagement detailing "pre-application engagement of a project sponsor with local governmental, Tribal, and community entities related to an authorization."

In the event that a court were to find noncompliance with NEPA for claims covered under BAAJA Section 1110, this provision would generally require that the authorization remain in effect during remand, authorize the underlying project activity to proceed while NEPA violations were remedied, and require a court remanding the applicable authorization to provide specific instructions to correct errors or deficiencies and set a deadline for the agency to act on remand, not to exceed 180 days. Specifically, for covered claims, BAAJA Section 1110 would not allow courts to vacate the agency action and would also bar permanent injunctions in their entirety. BAAJA Section 1110 would allow a court to award a preliminary injunction only if (1) there is a substantial likelihood the authorization violates a Federal law other than NEPA or National Historic Preservation Act; (2) "necessary to prevent irreparable harm to the plaintiff"; (3) the balance of equities favors the plaintiff; and (4) ''a preliminary injunction is in the public interest." In other words, the only remedy for a claim covered under this provision that identified only NEPA violations would be to send the matter back to the lead agency to address the violations, while the underlying project or action proceeds.

Claims alleging noncompliance with NEPA's environmental review provisions are typically brought under the APA, which is generally unavailable if another statute provides for judicial review. Plaintiffs typically have up to six years to file an APA claim pursuant to 28 U.S.C. §2401. In NEPA cases brought under the APA, courts typically require that the agency was on notice of the concern if there was an opportunity to comment.

To have a case heard in court, plaintiffs must meet minimum standing requirements including demonstrating an injury in fact that is fairly traceable to the challenged action and that can be remedied by a court (see Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560 (1992)). To establish injury in fact, a plaintiff must allege concrete, particularized, and imminent or actual harm to a legally protected interest resulting from defendant's alleged conduct (see Spokeo, Inc. v. Robins, 578 U.S. 330, 340 (2016)). Procedural injuries such as NEPA violations can be sufficient to establish injury if a plaintiff shows a concrete interest affected by deprivation of a procedural right (see, e.g., Summers v. Earth Island Instit., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009)).

Courts have recognized vacatur as the "ordinary" remedy under the APA for a NEPA violation. If a court determines an agency's action with respect to NEPA requires reconsideration or a potential change, it may order remand with or without vacatur, often based on the seriousness of the deficiencies and disruptive consequences if vacatur were ordered; (see Allied-Signal, Inc. v. U.S. Nuclear Regul. Comm'n, 988 F.2d 146, 150–51 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

A preliminary injunction (i.e., pre-trial order) is an "extraordinary remedy" that is "never awarded as a matter of right"; see Winter v. Nat. Res. Def. Council, Inc., 555 U.S. 7 (2009). To obtain a preliminary injunction, a plaintiff must show (1) a likelihood of irreparable harm in the absence of preliminary relief, (2) a likelihood of success on the merits, (3) that the balance of the equities tips in the plaintiff's favor, and (4) that an injunction is in the public interest (see Winter, 555 U.S. at 24).

A plaintiff seeking a permanent injunction (i.e., post-judgment order) must demonstrate (1) irreparable injury; (2) that there are no other adequate remedies available (such as monetary damages) to compensate for that injury; (3) that, considering the balance of hardships between the plaintiff and defendant, a remedy in equity is warranted; and (4) that the public interest would not be disserved by a permanent injunction (see Monsanto Co. v. Geertson Seed Farms, 561 U.S. 139, 156–57 (2010)).

For NEPA cases, the duration of any vacatur or injunctive relief typically lasts as long as necessary to cure the NEPA violation.

Subtitle B—Council on Environmental Quality

Section 1121. Council on Environmental Quality

Section 1121(a) ENEPA Technologies—In General

Would strike current NEPA Section 209, which contains NEPA's general authorization of appropriations and replace it with new NEPA Section 209 that would detail requirements for establishing a government-wide eNEPA technology program.

NEPA Section 209 authorizes appropriations of up to $1 million annually to carry out NEPA but does not establish a permitting technology program.

NEPA Section 110 separately directed CEQ to study electronic permitting technologies and a unified portal and report back to Congress. Agencies currently use varying case-management, public comment, geospatial, and document systems. 42 U.S.C. §4347(a) Definitions

Section 1131. Definitions

Section 1131 Definitions

BAAJA Section 1131 does not amend NEPA; rather, it creates its own stand-alone law that would incorporate some terms and definitions from NEPA and newly define terms used in Subtitle C, including
