Tourism—both international and domestic—contributes millions of jobs and billions of dollars of
spending to the national economy. In the past decade, the tourism industry has experienced a      Adam G. Levin
period with some disruptions. In 2020, the COVID-19 pandemic caused a sudden, dramatic           Analyst in Economic
decrease in tourism worldwide, including in and among the United States. By many measures,      Development Policy
U.S. tourism spent the ensuing years rebounding from those losses. For example, international
arrivals to the United States rose every year from 2020 through 2024.

In 2025, international arrivals to the United States fell 5.5% from 2024. This made the United
States one of two countries among those with the 10 most international arrivals to see a decline in international tourists from
2024 to 2025. In addition, U.S. domestic airline passengers declined 1.0% from 2024 to 2025. Possible developments
factoring into these trends include increased immigration enforcement, travel restrictions, new fees on nonimmigrant visas,
and reduced funding for tourism promotion entities.

In Congress, various committees and subcommittees, including those of the Senate Committee on Commerce, Science, and
Transportation, and the House Committee on Energy and Commerce, have held hearings touching on different aspects of
U.S. tourism in recent years, including rural tourism, the impact of daylight saving time on tourism, and pandemic recovery
efforts relative to tourism. P.L. 119-21, the FY2025 reconciliation act, reduced the annual cap on federal funds provided to
Brand USA—a public-private entity promoting international tourism to the United States—from $100 million to $20 million.
Subsequent bills, including H.R. 6128 and S. 3220, would restore or increase Brand USA funding. Previous Congresses have
also passed legislation related to U.S. tourism. For example, the 117th Congress passed the Visit America Act (Subtitle A,
Title VI, Division BB of P.L. 117-103, the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2022) which, among other things, authorized
the creation of a new Assistant Secretary of Commerce for Travel and Tourism within the Department of Commerce.

Several considerations concerning tourism may be of interest to Congress. They include

    •  The U.S. tourism outlook. As noted, both international arrivals to the United States and U.S. domestic
          airline passengers decreased in 2025 from 2024, making the United States an outlier among major tourism
         destinations. These developments come as the United States prepares to host the 2028 Summer Olympics in
       Los Angeles and 2034 Winter Olympics in Salt Lake City.
    •   Tourism-related leadership positions. Although the Visit America Act established the position of the
         Assistant Secretary of Commerce for Travel and Tourism, to date, the position has not yet been filled. In
         addition, the U.S. Travel and Tourism Advisory Board (a Federal Advisory Committee Act body that
        provides recommendations related to tourism to the Secretary of Commerce) appears to not have met since
        October 2024—despite its charter calling for meetings twice annually.
    •  Brand USA funding. As mentioned, legislation in the 119th Congress reduced the annual cap on Brand
       USA’s federal funds. In addition, Brand USA’s statutory funding structure—that allows it to access federal
        funding—is set to expire at the end of FY2027 unless Congress acts.

Introduction

Tourism to and within the United States—from both domestic and international travelers—
contributes millions of jobs and billions of dollars of spending to the national economy. For
example, in 2025, travel and tourism generated $248.8 in U.S. exports, or money that
international tourists spent in visiting the United States.1 In addition, tourism may help shape
views of the United States among foreign citizens, for example, those that attended the 2026
World Cup (cohosted by the United States) and those that may travel for the 2028 Summer
Olympics in Los Angeles, CA (and other sites) or the 2034 Winter Olympics in Salt Lake City,
UT.2

Congress has taken an interest in tourism generally for decades, and has specifically been
interested in the industry’s recovery following the pandemic. This report details Congress’s recent
activities related to tourism, provides data on recent tourism trends, outlines the federal
government’s role in tourism and tourism promotion, and presents issues for congressional
consideration.

                                Defining Tourism
 There is no standard definition of “tourism.” Instead, there are multiple definitions of “tourism” and related terms
 among federal departments and other bodies tracking tourism. They include
  •   The U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA), which has definitions for
      •   “domestic tourism” (“travel-related expenditures by U.S. residents traveling within the United States”);
      •   “inbound tourism” (“travel-related expenditures by nonresidents traveling within the United States and
           expenditures by nonresidents on international transportation purchased from U.S. providers”); and
      •    “visitor” (“a person who travels outside of his or her usual environment [more than 50–100 miles from
           the area of normal, everyday activities] for less than a year or who stays overnight in a hotel or motel”).3
  •   The U.S. Department of Transportation (DOT), which defines “long-distance travel and tourism trips” as
      “any overnight leisure or business trip greater than 50 miles using any mode, or combination of modes, of
       transportation.”4
  •   The U.S. Department of Commerce (DOC), which defines “tourism” as “the economic output of goods and
       services sold to visitors who travel 50 miles or more or who stay overnight in a paid accommodation.”5
  •  UN Tourism (part of the United Nations), which defines a “visitor” as “a traveler taking a trip to a main
       destination outside his/her usual environment, for less than a year, for any main purpose (business, leisure or
      other personal purpose) other than to be employed by a resident entity in the country or place visited…. A
       visitor (domestic, inbound or outbound) is classified as a tourist (or overnight visitor), if his/her trip includes
      an overnight stay, or as a same-day visitor (or excursionist) otherwise.”6

Recent Developments Related to Tourism7

Some recent developments may have affected or have the potential to affect tourism to and within
the United States. These include increased immigration enforcement, travel restrictions, new visa
and admission fees, and tariffs and other economic considerations. As discussed in more depth in
“Tourism Data and Recent Trends,” international arrivals to the United States declined by 5.5% in
2025 as compared to 2024, and the number of domestic airline passengers declined by 1.0% in
2025 as compared to 2024.8 In addition, 2026 year-to-date international arrivals to the United
States through June 2026 were 0.4% lower than year-to-date through June 2025.9

The Trump Administration’s focus on immigration enforcement has led to some concerns about a
dampening effect on U.S. tourism.10 There have been reports of tourists and other noncitizens
being detained while traveling to the United States.11 Additionally, in December 2025, the Trump
Administration proposed a five-year social media review for Visa Waiver Program travelers.12
Some Members of Congress objected to the proposal in a January 2026 letter to the Department
of Homeland Security (DHS) and U.S. Customs and Border Protection, stating that “leading
hospitality industry groups and international travel advocates have raised serious concerns that
the breadth of this data collection could deter lawful, international visitors and impose significant
new barriers to travel.”13 More recently, in September 2026, the Department of State (DOS)
announced that it will expand its existing review of certain visa applicants’ social media history to
include additional nonimmigrant (temporary) visa categories, though tourist visas are not subject
to this review.14 Several countries, including Canada, Germany, and the United Kingdom, later
adjusted travel advisories for their citizens wishing to visit the United States.15

Tourism Data and Recent Trends

International and domestic tourism in the United States both experienced relative turmoil since
2020. On certain data points, U.S. tourism had nearly recovered its pandemic-era losses but has
seen some recent setbacks. This section presents data and recent trends for U.S. tourism. Other
data related to U.S. tourism, including on employment and economic activity, are not currently
available beyond 2023. These data are presented in Appendix B.

Domestic and International Visitor Levels

The U.S. tourism industry includes domestic and international tourists. By at least some
measures, the numbers of both had increased in the years following the COVID-19 pandemic,
although those trends appear to have slowed or reversed in 2025 and into 2026.

                   What Factors Affect Tourism?
  Researchers note that tourism is influenced by a confluence of factors. For example, some point to inflation as
  impacting travel decisions.35 Other factors that may affect tourism include
  •   economic conditions such as exchange rates and household savings;
  •    labor availability and supply chain dynamics;
  •   weather and natural disasters;
  •    international relations and safety concerns;
  •    immigration policy; and
  •    travel infrastructure.
 These variables can be fluid. For example, research suggests that currency exchange rates, particularly relative to
  U.S. dollars, can be important drivers of tourism.36 Some exchange rates, including the U.S. dollar/euro rate, tend
  to fluctuate over time.37 Monitoring these factors may help policymakers better understand tourism’s dynamics.

There are more domestic travelers than international tourists in the United States. According to
data from the Department of Transportation’s Bureau of Transportation Statistics, there were
approximately 841.6 million seasonally adjusted domestic airline passengers in 2025.38 That
compared to about 68.3 million international visitors to the United States in that same year.39 (Not
all airline passengers travel for tourism; for example, some travel for work. In addition, some
domestic tourism takes place by transportation modes other than flying.)

Exports and Visitor Spending

Economists monitor travel and tourism exports. Travel and tourism exports are the amount of
money that foreign visitors spend in the United States on passenger fares and travel-related goods
and services such as food, lodging, recreation, gifts, entertainment, and local transportation,
including travel to and from the United States on a U.S. airline. To improve the comparability of
U.S. travel statistics with those of other countries, The U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA)
in 2014 refined its travel statistics by expanding the definition of travel and tourism exports to
cover all kinds of travel, including travel for health and educational purposes.49

Goods and services purchased by international visitors to the United States constitute export
income for the U.S. economy. Likewise, purchases by U.S. residents while outside the country
constitute export income for that other country and import income for the United States.50 The
difference between U.S. travel and tourism exports and imports is the trade balance for travel.
U.S. tourism exports were approximately $248.8 billion in 2025, down 1.2% from 2024.51
However, 2024 and 2025 had the largest amount of U.S. tourism exports dating back to at least
1999, surpassing the pre-pandemic high of $240.5 billion in 2018.

From January through July 2026, year-to-date tourism exports were approximately $145.4 billion,
down 0.1% from the same period in 2025.52 U.S. travel and tourism exports in July 2025
accounted for 7.3% of total U.S. exports. (Travel and tourism exports’ highest share of total U.S.
exports on record was 9.6%, in April 2019.)53 The U.S. travel trade balance went from $102.8
billion in 2015 to $14.1 billion in 2025. The 2025 U.S. travel trade balance was also down from
$29.2 billion in 2024, a 51.6% decrease.54

Tourism-Related Departments and Programs

Much of the federal government’s involvement in tourism is done through DOC. Legislation in

Some federal programs may have goals that could benefit, but are not explicitly geared towards,

Department of Commerce (DOC)

The National Travel and Tourism Office (NTTO), within DOC’s ITA, has traditionally played a

Funding

Brand USA has two funding streams. First, it is funded by nonfederal sources, including state
tourism offices and private travel brands. These nonfederal contributions provide half of Brand
USA’s funding through annual cash contributions of at least 30% of the organization’s budget as
well as in-kind contributions such as advertising, tickets, and donated products.75

Second, the federal government matches those cash and in-kind contributions with an annual
grant capped at no more than $20 million. This money comes from the Travel Promotion Fund, an
account within the Department of the Treasury that was established in TPA.76 As noted in “Recent
Developments Related to Tourism,” prior to July 2025, the grant was capped at $100 million
annually, but P.L. 119-21, the FY2025 reconciliation act, lowered the cap to $20 million.77 The
money is generated from a fee charged for use of the Electronic System for Travel Authorization
(ESTA), which is required of international travelers who visit the United States for short business
or leisure stays from countries participating in the Visa Waiver Program (VWP). Each traveler
from a VWP country who uses ESTA is charged an approximately $40 fee. Of that, $17 is
available to fund travel promotion through Brand USA. (TPA authorized $10 of each ESTA fee to
be available to Brand USA, but the Brand USA Extension Act increased the amount to $17.)78

Appendix A. Impact of and Reaction to COVID-19
Pandemic

The COVID-19 pandemic significantly affected tourism throughout the world, including in the
United States.105 To cite one example, the tourism industry fell from accounting for nearly 3% of
U.S. GDP in 2019 to accounting for about 2.2% of U.S. GDP in 2020 (see Table A-2).

Like other countries, the United States took steps to reduce the number of incoming international
visitors in an attempt to stop the spread of the COVID-19 virus (each of which has since been
canceled). For example
    •   in January 2020, a presidential proclamation suspended entry into the United

       recovery from COVID-19 for all air passengers to the United States from the
       United Kingdom;109
    •   in January 2021, the CDC expanded the requirement of a negative COVID-19
         test or documentation of recovery from COVID-19 to all air passengers to the
       United States from any foreign country;110 and
    •   in May 2021, a presidential proclamation suspended entry into the United States

Legislation and Plans Related to the Pandemic and Tourism

Congress acted to support certain segments of the tourism industry and related sectors. In the
116th Congress, the Coronavirus Economic Stabilization Act of 2020 (Title IV, Division A of the
Coronavirus Aid, Relief, and Economic Security [CARES] Act, P.L. 116-136) authorized the
Secretary of the Treasury to make loans, loan guarantees, and other investments to air carriers.
That financial assistance was capped at $25 billion for loans and loan guarantees to passenger air
carriers, ticket agents, and aircraft repair businesses, and $4 billion for loans and loan guarantees
to cargo air carriers.112 In the 117th Congress, the American Rescue Plan Act of 2021 (ARPA, P.L.
117-2) also included support for the tourism industry. The law appropriated $8 billion to the
Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) to provide grants to airports to help with operations costs
and debt service.113 ARPA also appropriated $3 billion to the U.S. Economic Development
Administration to help state and local governments respond to economic injury as a result of the
pandemic.114 Of that amount, $750 million was reserved for states and local communities that
were specifically affected by job and GDP losses in the travel, tourism, or outdoor recreation
industries.115

The Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (IIJA, P.L. 117-58) also appropriated funding for
airport improvement projects. This included $15 billion to FAA for grants for airport
infrastructure projects that increase safety and expand capacity.116 IIJA also appropriated $5
billion to FAA for competitive grants for airport terminals, including replacing aging terminals
and airport-owned control towers.117

National Travel and Tourism Plans

Congress authorized the creation of national-level plans to address issues regarding the
transportation and business aspects of tourism to guide the tourism industry’s recovery following
the pandemic. Those included plans from DOT and DOC.

Department of Transportation (DOT)—National Travel and Tourism
Infrastructure Strategic Plan
In the 114th Congress, the Fixing America’s Surface Transportation (FAST) Act (P.L. 114-94)
required the Secretary of Transportation to develop and publish a national travel and tourism
infrastructure strategic plan within three years of the law’s enactment.118 Among other things, the
plan was to include an assessment of the condition and performance of the national transportation
network; an assessment of the issues on the national transportation network that create congestion
and barriers to long-haul passenger travel and tourism; and forecasts of long-haul passenger travel
and tourism over 20 years.

Department of Commerce (DOC)—National Travel and Tourism Strategy

The Visit America Act (Subtitle A, Title VI, Division BB of the Consolidated Appropriations Act,
2023, P.L. 117-328) required the Secretary of Commerce, at least every 10 years and in
consultation with the U.S. Travel and Tourism Advisory Board and the interagency Tourism
Policy Council (TPC, both described in more detail in “Department of Commerce (DOC)”), to
develop and submit to Congress a 10-year travel and tourism strategy. The Visit America Act
requires the strategy to establish goals for the number of annual international visitors to the
United States and the annual amount of travel and tourism commerce in the United States.124

Separate from the requirements of the Visit America Act, the Secretary of Commerce—on behalf
of the TPC—published the National Travel and Tourism Strategy (hereinafter “Strategy”) in June
2022.125 The Strategy set a goal of attracting 90 million international visitors annually to the
United States by 2027—up from the 68.3 million international visitors who came to the United
States in 2025—estimating that those visitors would spend $279 billion each year.126 The Strategy
is comprised of four “pillars” to help achieve those goals:
    •   Promoting the United States as a travel destination by leveraging existing
       programs and assets to promote U.S. tourism to international visitors and broaden
       marketing efforts towards underserved communities.
    •   Facilitating travel to and within the United States by reducing barriers to trade in
         travel services to make it easier for visitors to enter and travel around the United
         States.
